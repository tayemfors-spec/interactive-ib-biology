## Theme A — Unity and diversity

<a id="a1-1-water"></a>
### A1.1 Water

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 1 · visualisation **high**: Polarity and the hydrogen-bond network only make sense in 3D

**Big questions:** Which features of the water molecule explain why life depends on it? / How does living in water differ from living in air, and what does that mean for animals?

- **A1.1.1** Water as life's medium: Know that life began in water and that most biological processes still take place in watery surroundings.
- **A1.1.2** Polar bonds and hydrogen bonding in water: Explain that oxygen pulls shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen, making water polar, so neighbouring molecules attract by hydrogen bonds. Be able to draw at least two water molecules with partial charges and the hydrogen bonds linking them.
- **A1.1.3** Cohesion and why it matters: Explain how hydrogen bonds make water molecules stick together, allowing water to be pulled up xylem under tension and creating surface tension that lets some organisms live on the water surface.
- **A1.1.4** Adhesion to polar and charged surfaces: Explain how water is attracted to polar or charged materials and how this drives capillary action through soil and along plant cell walls.
- **A1.1.5** Water as a solvent: Explain why many hydrophilic substances dissolve in water, making it the medium for enzyme-catalysed metabolism and for transport in plants and animals, while recognising that some cell molecules work precisely because they are hydrophobic and insoluble.
- **A1.1.6** Water versus air for animals: Compare buoyancy, viscosity, thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity of water and air, and use animals such as the black-throated loon and ringed seal to show the consequences for life in each medium.
- **A1.1.7** `AHL` Where Earth's water came from and why it stayed: Describe the hypothesis that asteroids delivered Earth's water, and explain that gravity plus temperatures cool enough for condensation kept it here long enough for life to evolve.
- **A1.1.8** `AHL` Water and the search for alien life: Explain why the presence of liquid water guides the search for life elsewhere, including the idea of a Goldilocks zone around a star.

**Named examples:** black-throated loon (Gavia arctica); ringed seal (Pusa hispida); xylem transport under tension; Goldilocks zone

**Skills:** Draw two or more water molecules showing partial charges (polarity) and the hydrogen bonds between them

<a id="a1-2-nucleic-acids"></a>
### A1.2 Nucleic acids

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Double helix, antiparallel strands, base pairing and the nucleosome are 3D structures

**Big questions:** How can a chain of just four kinds of building block hold the instructions for a whole organism? / What is it about DNA's shape that lets it be copied so faithfully?

- **A1.2.1** DNA carries genes in all living things: Know that every living organism uses DNA as its genetic material; some viruses use RNA instead, but viruses are not classed as living.
- **A1.2.2** Parts of a nucleotide: Identify the phosphate, pentose sugar and nitrogenous base in a nucleotide, drawing them as a circle, pentagon and rectangle in their correct relative positions.
- **A1.2.3** The sugar–phosphate backbone: Explain how covalent bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next form a continuous, strong backbone in each DNA or RNA strand.
- **A1.2.4** Bases as the letters of the code: Name the nitrogenous bases found in DNA and RNA and understand that their sequence forms the genetic code.
- **A1.2.5** Building RNA by condensation: Explain that RNA forms when nucleotides join by condensation reactions, and be able to draw and recognise a single nucleotide and a short RNA polymer.
- **A1.2.6** DNA double helix and base pairing: Describe DNA as two antiparallel strands held together by hydrogen bonds between A–T and G–C pairs. Draw the strands antiparallel; the helix shape, base sizes and hydrogen-bond counts need not be shown or memorised.
- **A1.2.7** Comparing DNA and RNA: Compare DNA and RNA by strand number, bases present and pentose sugar, sketch how ribose differs from deoxyribose, and give examples of nucleic acids.
- **A1.2.8** Complementary pairing for copying and expression: Explain how hydrogen-bond-based complementary base pairing lets genetic information be both replicated and expressed.
- **A1.2.9** DNA's huge information capacity: Explain that DNA of any length can carry any base sequence, giving an almost unlimited and very compact way of storing information.
- **A1.2.10** Universal code points to common ancestry: Explain why the near-identical genetic code across all organisms is evidence that life shares one common ancestor; no specific examples need to be memorised.
- **A1.2.11** `AHL` 5' and 3' directionality: Describe the 5'-to-3' linkages along nucleic acid backbones and explain why this direction matters for replication, transcription and translation.
- **A1.2.12** `AHL` Purine–pyrimidine pairs keep the helix uniform: Explain that each base pair joins a purine to a pyrimidine, so A–T and C–G pairs are equal in length and the helix keeps the same 3D shape whatever the sequence.
- **A1.2.13** `AHL` Nucleosome structure: Describe a nucleosome as DNA wound around eight histone proteins, secured by a further histone on the linker DNA, and use molecular visualisation software to explore how the DNA and proteins associate.
- **A1.2.14** `AHL` Hershey–Chase experiment: Explain how the Hershey–Chase results, using radioactively labelled phage, showed DNA rather than protein is the genetic material, and note that the experiment depended on radioisotopes becoming available.
- **A1.2.15** `AHL` Chargaff's base ratio data: Use Chargaff's data on purine and pyrimidine amounts across species to show how one set of evidence falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis, illustrating falsification as an answer to the problem of induction.

**Named examples:** Hershey–Chase experiment; Chargaff's data; tetranucleotide hypothesis; nucleosome and histones; ribose and deoxyribose; RNA viruses

**Skills:** Draw nucleotides using circles, pentagons and rectangles for phosphate, sugar and base; Draw and recognise diagrams of a single nucleotide and an RNA polymer; Draw a section of DNA with antiparallel strands and correct A–T and G–C pairing; Sketch the structural difference between ribose and deoxyribose; Use molecular visualisation software to study protein–DNA association in a nucleosome

<a id="a2-1-origins-of-cells"></a>
### A2.1 Origins of cells

**Cells** · AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 0, AHL 2 · visualisation **medium**: Miller–Urey and vesicle self-assembly animate well; much of the topic is conceptual

**Big questions:** How could non-living chemistry on the young Earth have given rise to the first cells? / What steps might have bridged the gap between simple molecules and a living cell?

- **A2.1.1** `AHL` Early Earth and pre-biotic carbon chemistry: Describe early Earth conditions: no free oxygen or ozone, more carbon dioxide and methane, higher temperatures and strong UV. Explain how these may have let carbon compounds form spontaneously in ways that no longer happen.
- **A2.1.2** `AHL` Cells as the minimum unit of life: Distinguish living from non-living things and explain why cells are the smallest self-sustaining living units, including why viruses are regarded as non-living.
- **A2.1.3** `AHL` Why cell origins are hard to explain: Explain that cells now arise only from existing cells, and that the first cells needed catalysis, self-replicating molecules, self-assembly and compartments. Appreciate that such hypotheses are hard to test because early conditions cannot be recreated and protocells left no fossils.
- **A2.1.4** `AHL` Miller–Urey experiment: Evaluate the Miller–Urey experiment as evidence that organic compounds could form from simple inorganic gases under simulated early-Earth conditions.
- **A2.1.5** `AHL` Self-assembling fatty acid vesicles: Explain how fatty acids can spontaneously come together into spherical bilayer vesicles, and why an enclosed compartment lets internal chemistry differ from the outside.
- **A2.1.6** `AHL` RNA as the likely first genetic material: Explain why RNA, which can both replicate and catalyse reactions, may have served as early cells' genes and enzymes, citing ribozymes in ribosomes that still form peptide bonds.
- **A2.1.7** `AHL` Evidence for LUCA: Outline the case for a single common ancestor of all life (LUCA), such as the universal genetic code and genes found in every organism, and why rival early life forms may have died out.
- **A2.1.8** `AHL` Dating the first cells and LUCA: Outline approaches used to estimate when the first cells and LUCA existed, and appreciate the vast span of time over which life has evolved.
- **A2.1.9** `AHL` LUCA and hydrothermal vents: Describe evidence that LUCA evolved near hydrothermal vents, including fossil traces in ancient vent precipitates and conserved gene sequences found by genome analysis.

**Named examples:** Miller–Urey experiment; fatty acid vesicles; ribozymes in the ribosome; last universal common ancestor (LUCA); hydrothermal vents; viruses as non-living

**Skills:** Evaluate the design and findings of the Miller–Urey experiment

<a id="a2-2-cell-structure"></a>
### A2.2 Cell structure

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 1 · visualisation **high**: Organelles in 3D across cell types; endosymbiosis happens over time

**Big questions:** What do all cells have in common, and where do different kinds of cell part ways? / How do microscopes let us see and measure the inside of cells?

- **A2.2.1** Cell theory: Know that all organisms are built from cells, and use this theory deductively to predict that any newly found organism will consist of one or more cells.
- **A2.2.2** Practical microscopy skills: Gain hands-on experience preparing temporary mounts, staining, focusing with coarse and fine controls, measuring with an eyepiece graticule, calculating magnification and actual size, adding a scale bar and photographing specimens.
- **A2.2.3** Advances in microscopy: Describe the advantages of electron microscopy, freeze fracture and cryogenic electron microscopy, plus fluorescent stains and immunofluorescence in light microscopy.
- **A2.2.4** Features shared by all cells: Explain why typical cells all have DNA as genetic material, a mostly watery cytoplasm and a lipid-based plasma membrane enclosing it.
- **A2.2.5** Prokaryotic cell structure: Describe a Gram-positive eubacterium such as Bacillus or Staphylococcus: cell wall, plasma membrane, cytoplasm, a loop of naked DNA and 70S ribosomes. Appreciate that prokaryotes vary, without learning the specific variations.
- **A2.2.6** Eukaryotic cell structure: Describe the shared eukaryote features: compartmentalised cytoplasm with 80S ribosomes, a double-membraned nucleus with pores and histone-bound chromosomes, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, vesicles and vacuoles including lysosomes, and a cytoskeleton of microtubules and microfilaments.
- **A2.2.7** Life processes in single-celled organisms: Explain how one cell carries out homeostasis, metabolism, nutrition, movement, excretion, growth, response to stimuli and reproduction.
- **A2.2.8** Animal, fungal and plant cells compared: Compare these cell types for cell wall presence and composition, vacuole size and role, chloroplasts and other plastids, and centrioles, cilia and flagella.
- **A2.2.9** Atypical eukaryotic cells: Use nucleus number to show atypical structure in aseptate fungal hyphae, skeletal muscle fibres, red blood cells and phloem sieve tube elements.
- **A2.2.10** Identifying cells and structures in micrographs: Classify cells in light and electron micrographs as prokaryote, plant or animal, and identify listed structures in electron micrographs, such as nucleoid, nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, sap vacuole, Golgi, rough and smooth ER, ribosomes, cell wall, membrane and microvilli.
- **A2.2.11** Drawing from electron micrographs: Draw and annotate, with functions, organelles and structures seen in electron micrographs, including nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, sap vacuole, Golgi, rough and smooth ER, chromosomes, cell wall, plasma membrane, secretory vesicles and microvilli.
- **A2.2.12** `AHL` Endosymbiotic origin of eukaryotes: Explain that eukaryotes descend from a nucleated, sexually reproducing ancestor that gained mitochondria, and in some lineages later chloroplasts, by endosymbiosis. Cite evidence: 70S ribosomes, naked circular DNA and independent replication in these organelles.
- **A2.2.13** `AHL` Differentiation into specialised tissues: Explain that cells in multicellular organisms specialise because they express different sets of genes, often switched on by environmental changes.
- **A2.2.14** `AHL` How multicellularity evolved: Know that multicellularity arose many times independently, in many fungi and algae and in all plants and animals, and that it allows larger bodies and specialised cells.

**Named examples:** Bacillus; Staphylococcus; aseptate fungal hyphae; skeletal muscle fibres; red blood cells; phloem sieve tube elements; endosymbiosis (mitochondria and chloroplasts); freeze fracture; cryogenic electron microscopy; immunofluorescence

**Skills:** Prepare temporary mounts and stain cells and tissues; Focus a light microscope using coarse and fine adjustment; Measure cells with an eyepiece graticule; Calculate magnification and actual size, and add a scale bar; Identify cells as prokaryote, plant or animal in light and electron micrographs; Identify named structures in electron micrographs; Draw and annotate organelles and cell structures from electron micrographs, including their functions

<a id="a2-3-viruses"></a>
### A2.3 Viruses

**Cells** · AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 0, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Lytic and lysogenic cycles are step-by-step processes

**Big questions:** How do viruses manage to reproduce while carrying so little genetic information? / What makes viruses so varied, and how did they come to exist?

- **A2.3.1** `AHL` What all viruses share: List the features every virus shares: a small, fixed size; a genome of either DNA or RNA; a protein coat (capsid); no cytoplasm; and very few or no enzymes of its own.
- **A2.3.2** `AHL` Variety in virus structure: Describe how viruses differ in shape, in having single- or double-stranded DNA or RNA, and in being enveloped by host membrane or not, using bacteriophage lambda, coronaviruses and HIV as examples.
- **A2.3.3** `AHL` Lytic cycle: Describe the stages of the lytic cycle using bacteriophage lambda, and explain that viruses depend on host cells for energy, materials and protein synthesis.
- **A2.3.4** `AHL` Lysogenic cycle: Describe the lysogenic cycle using bacteriophage lambda as the example.
- **A2.3.5** `AHL` Multiple origins of viruses: Explain why virus diversity points to several separate origins from other organisms, with shared features possibly due to convergent evolution under extreme obligate parasitism, and note that viruses use the same genetic code as living things.
- **A2.3.6** `AHL` Fast evolution in viruses: Explain why some viruses evolve very quickly, using influenza and HIV as examples, and discuss what this means for treating the diseases they cause.

**Named examples:** bacteriophage lambda; coronaviruses; HIV; influenza viruses

<a id="a3-1-diversity-of-organisms"></a>
### A3.1 Diversity of organisms

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 2 · visualisation **low**: Mostly conceptual; karyogram and key tools are enough

**Big questions:** Where should we draw the line between one species and another? / How much do genomes differ within a species compared with between species?

- **A3.1.1** Variation as a hallmark of life: Every individual differs from every other in some characteristics; these complex patterns of variation are the basis for naming and classifying organisms.
- **A3.1.2** Morphological species concept: Describe Linnaeus's original idea of a species as a group of organisms sharing similar observable traits.
- **A3.1.3** Binomial naming: Explain that a scientific name gives the genus first, capitalised, then the species, in lower case, and that species in one genus share similar traits.
- **A3.1.4** Biological species concept: Define a species as organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, discuss the problems with this definition, and recognise that other species definitions are in use.
- **A3.1.5** Populations versus species during speciation: Explain that speciation, one species splitting into several, usually happens gradually as isolated populations diverge, so deciding when they count as separate species can be arbitrary.
- **A3.1.6** Chromosome number varies between species: Know that chromosome numbers differ widely between species, that humans have 46 and chimpanzees 48, and that diploid cells always carry an even number. No other numbers are required.
- **A3.1.7** Karyotypes and karyograms: Sort chromosomes in a karyogram by size, banding pattern and centromere position, and evaluate evidence that human chromosome 2 formed by fusion of two ancestral primate chromosomes, as a testable hypothesis.
- **A3.1.8** Genome unity and variation within a species: Define the genome as an organism's full genetic information, and explain that members of a species share most of it, with differences such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms.
- **A3.1.9** Eukaryote genomes differ in size and sequence: Explain that eukaryote genomes vary in total DNA content and in base sequence, and that differences between species far exceed those within one.
- **A3.1.10** Comparing genome sizes: Use a database to find genome sizes for different taxonomic groups and judge whether genome size tracks organism complexity.
- **A3.1.11** Uses of whole genome sequencing: Describe how sequencing has become faster and cheaper, its current use in studying evolutionary relationships, and its potential future use in personalised medicine.
- **A3.1.12** `AHL` Species concept fails for asexual organisms and bacteria: Explain why the biological species concept struggles with organisms that reproduce asexually and with bacteria that swap genes by horizontal gene transfer.
- **A3.1.13** `AHL` Chromosome number as a species trait: Explain that members of a species share a chromosome number, and that crosses between related species with different numbers rarely give fertile offspring.
- **A3.1.14** `AHL` Building a dichotomous key: Construct a dichotomous key to identify species that live in your local area.
- **A3.1.15** `AHL` eDNA barcoding to identify species: Explain how DNA barcodes in environmental DNA samples let scientists quickly identify which species are present and assess a habitat's biodiversity.

**Named examples:** Linnaeus; humans (46 chromosomes) and chimpanzees (48 chromosomes); human chromosome 2 fusion hypothesis; single-nucleotide polymorphisms; environmental DNA barcoding

**Skills:** Classify chromosomes in a karyogram by banding pattern, length and centromere position; Evaluate evidence for the fusion origin of human chromosome 2; Extract genome size data from a database and compare it with organism complexity; Construct a dichotomous key for local plant or animal species

<a id="a3-2-classification-and-cladistics"></a>
### A3.2 Classification and cladistics

**Organisms** · AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 0, AHL 3 · visualisation **medium**: Reading cladogram topology is easier when you can manipulate the tree

**Big questions:** How can we sort millions of species into groups that reflect their real evolutionary history? / What does a cladogram tell us that a traditional ranked hierarchy cannot?

- **A3.2.1** `AHL` Why classify organisms: Explain that the huge number of species makes classification necessary and that a good classification opens up many further lines of study.
- **A3.2.2** `AHL` Problems with ranked taxa: Explain why the kingdom-to-species hierarchy does not always match evolutionary divergence, why fixed ranks are arbitrary, and how unranked clades in cladistics represent a paradigm shift.
- **A3.2.3** `AHL` Benefits of evolution-based classification: Explain that groups whose members all share a common ancestor are ideal, because traits of members can then be predicted from others in the clade.
- **A3.2.4** `AHL` Clades defined by common ancestry: Define a clade as an ancestor and its descendants sharing characteristics, and explain that gene or protein sequences give the most objective evidence, though morphology can also be used.
- **A3.2.5** `AHL` Molecular clock: Explain how accumulated sequence differences are used to estimate when clades split, and why these are only estimates, since mutation rates depend on generation time, population size, selection pressure and more.
- **A3.2.6** `AHL` Building cladograms from sequences: Construct simple cladograms from sample DNA or protein sequence data, using parsimony to choose the tree that needs the fewest sequence changes, and recognise that different criteria can produce different hypotheses.
- **A3.2.7** `AHL` Reading cladograms: Use a cladogram to work out relationships, shared ancestors and clades, using the terms root, node and terminal branch; each node represents a hypothetical common ancestor.
- **A3.2.8** `AHL` Testing classifications with cladistics: Use a case study, such as the reshuffling of the figwort family (Scrophulariaceae), to show how cladistics revealed groupings based on convergent morphology to be false. Case details need not be memorised.
- **A3.2.9** `AHL` Three domains from rRNA evidence: Describe the 1977 reclassification of life into three domains, a level above kingdoms, based on comparing ribosomal RNA base sequences.

**Named examples:** molecular clock; parsimony analysis; figwort family (Scrophulariaceae) reclassification; three-domain system (1977, rRNA)

**Skills:** Construct a simple cladogram from sample base or amino acid sequence data using parsimony; Deduce relationships, common ancestors and clades from a cladogram

<a id="a4-1-evolution-and-speciation"></a>
### A4.1 Evolution and speciation

**Ecosystems** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 1 · visualisation **medium**: Speciation happens over time; homologous limbs compare well side by side

**Big questions:** What lines of evidence convince biologists that species change over time? / How does one species become two, and what keeps them apart afterwards?

- **A4.1.1** Defining evolution: Define evolution as change in a population's heritable traits, distinguishing it from Lamarckian inheritance of acquired traits, and explain why natural selection is called a theory despite overwhelming evidence.
- **A4.1.2** Molecular sequence evidence: Explain how comparing DNA, RNA and protein sequences provides strong evidence that organisms share common ancestors.
- **A4.1.3** Evidence from selective breeding: Explain how differences among domesticated breeds and crop varieties, and between them and their wild ancestors, show how quickly evolution can happen.
- **A4.1.4** Homologous structures: Explain how structures with a shared underlying plan but different uses, such as the pentadactyl limb, support common descent.
- **A4.1.5** Analogous structures and convergent evolution: Explain that analogous structures do a similar job but evolved separately through convergent evolution, and know at least one example.
- **A4.1.6** Speciation by splitting: Explain that new species arise only when existing species split, that speciation adds species while extinction removes them, and that gradual change within one lineage is not speciation.
- **A4.1.7** Isolation and differential selection: Explain how reproductive isolation, for example by geography, and different selection pressures drive speciation, using bonobos and common chimpanzees separated by the Congo River.
- **A4.1.8** `AHL` Sympatric versus allopatric speciation: Compare speciation with and without geographic separation, and know that reproductive isolation can be geographic, behavioural or temporal.
- **A4.1.9** `AHL` Adaptive radiation: Explain how adaptive radiation lets related species fill vacant niches and coexist without competing, raising ecosystem biodiversity.
- **A4.1.10** `AHL` Hybridisation barriers and sterile hybrids: Explain how barriers such as courtship behaviour, and the sterility of hybrids like the mule, stop alleles mixing between species.
- **A4.1.11** `AHL` Sudden plant speciation via polyploidy: Explain how hybridisation and polyploidy can create new plant species abruptly, using knotweeds or smartweeds (genus Persicaria) as the example.

**Named examples:** Lamarckism; domesticated breeds and crop varieties; pentadactyl limb; bonobos and common chimpanzees (Congo River); mule; knotweed / smartweed (Persicaria)

<a id="a4-2-conservation-of-biodiversity"></a>
### A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity

**Ecosystems** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 0 · visualisation **low**: Case studies and data; charts suffice

**Big questions:** Why are species disappearing so quickly today, and how do we know? / Which strategies give threatened species and ecosystems the best chance, and who decides what to save?

- **A4.2.1** What biodiversity means: Define biodiversity as the variety of life at all levels, covering ecosystem, species and genetic diversity.
- **A4.2.2** Today's species count versus the past: Explain that many species remain undiscovered, that fossils suggest more species live now than ever before, and that lumpers and splitters may classify the same observations differently.
- **A4.2.3** Human-driven extinctions: Use at least three case studies of extinctions caused by humans in today's 'sixth mass extinction': the North Island giant moa, the Caribbean monk seal and one extinct species from your own region.
- **A4.2.4** Causes of ecosystem loss: Examine direct and indirect human causes of ecosystem loss through two case studies: mixed dipterocarp forest in Southeast Asia and, ideally, a lost ecosystem near the student.
- **A4.2.5** Evidence of a biodiversity crisis: Evaluate evidence such as IPBES reports and repeated surveys of species richness and evenness worldwide, recognising the roles and methodological issues of both professional and citizen scientists and the value of peer review.
- **A4.2.6** Drivers of biodiversity loss: Explain human population growth as the underlying cause, plus over-exploitation, urbanisation, deforestation and land clearance for farming, pollution, and the global spread of pests, diseases and invasive species.
- **A4.2.7** Multiple conservation approaches: Explain why no single method suffices, covering in situ conservation and reserve management, rewilding and restoration of degraded land, ex situ conservation in zoos and botanic gardens, and seed or tissue banks.
- **A4.2.8** EDGE species prioritisation: Explain why the EDGE of Existence programme targets species that are both evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered, and appreciate the ethical, political and economic debate over conservation priorities.

**Named examples:** North Island giant moa (Dinornis novaezealandiae); Caribbean monk seal (Neomonachus tropicalis); a locally extinct species of the student's choice; mixed dipterocarp forest, Southeast Asia; a local lost ecosystem; IPBES reports; EDGE of Existence programme

**Skills:** Use data from repeated biodiversity surveys (species richness and evenness) as evidence of change

<a id="b1-1-carbohydrates-and-lipids"></a>
### B1.1 Carbohydrates and lipids

**Molecules** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 0 · visualisation **medium**: Alpha/beta glucose, cellulose vs starch and fatty-acid kinks are clearer in 3D

**Big questions:** Why can small changes in the shape of a sugar or lipid molecule give it a completely different job? / When should an organism store energy as carbohydrate and when as fat?

- **B1.1.1** Why carbon can build so many molecules: Explain covalent bonding and how carbon forms up to four bonds (single or double) with carbon or other non-metals, giving branched, unbranched and ring-shaped compounds. Know the SI prefixes kilo, centi, milli, micro and nano.
- **B1.1.2** Building polymers by condensation: Describe how condensation reactions join monomers into polymers, releasing water, using polysaccharides, polypeptides and nucleic acids as examples.
- **B1.1.3** Breaking polymers by hydrolysis: Explain that hydrolysis splits polymers into monomers by using a water molecule, whose -H and -OH end up on the products.
- **B1.1.4** Monosaccharide structure and glucose's properties: Recognise pentose and hexose ring structures as monosaccharides. Use glucose to link its solubility, ease of transport, chemical stability and energy yield on oxidation to how cells use it.
- **B1.1.5** Starch and glycogen as energy stores: Explain why starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) make good stores: coiling and branching make them compact, their size makes them nearly insoluble, and alpha-glucose units are easily added or removed.
- **B1.1.6** Cellulose as a structural polysaccharide: Relate cellulose's strength to alternating beta-glucose units forming straight chains that bundle together and are cross-linked by hydrogen bonds.
- **B1.1.7** Glycoproteins and cell recognition: Describe how glycoproteins on cell surfaces allow cells to recognise each other, using the ABO blood group antigens as the example.
- **B1.1.8** Lipids are hydrophobic: Lipids are a varied group (fats, oils, waxes, steroids) defined by dissolving readily in non-polar solvents but hardly at all in water.
- **B1.1.9** Making triglycerides and phospholipids: Describe condensation of glycerol with three fatty acids to make a triglyceride, or with two fatty acids plus a phosphate group to make a phospholipid.
- **B1.1.10** Saturated versus unsaturated fatty acids: Distinguish saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids by number of C=C bonds, link this to melting point, and explain why plants store oils while endotherms store solid fats.
- **B1.1.11** Triglycerides for storage and insulation: Explain why triglycerides in adipose tissue suit long-term energy storage, and relate their use as heat insulation to an animal's body temperature and habitat.
- **B1.1.12** Why phospholipids form bilayers: Explain that phospholipids are amphipathic (hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails) and so spontaneously arrange into bilayers in water. Use the term amphipathic correctly.
- **B1.1.13** Steroids crossing membranes: Explain that non-polar steroids such as oestradiol and testosterone can diffuse through the bilayer, and identify steroids from their structural diagrams.

**Named examples:** starch; glycogen; cellulose; glucose (alpha and beta); ABO blood group antigens; triglycerides; phospholipids; adipose tissue; oestradiol; testosterone

**Skills:** recognise pentose and hexose monosaccharides from ring-form diagrams; identify steroids from molecular diagrams; use SI prefixes kilo, centi, milli, micro and nano

<a id="b1-2-proteins"></a>
### B1.2 Proteins

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: The four levels of protein structure are spatial

**Big questions:** How can just twenty building blocks produce proteins with almost any shape or job? / Why does heat or a change in pH stop a protein from working?

- **B1.2.1** General structure of an amino acid: Draw a generalised amino acid: a central alpha carbon bonded to an amine group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a variable R-group.
- **B1.2.2** Linking amino acids by condensation: Write the word equation for joining two amino acids and draw a generalised dipeptide, ideally after building it with molecular models.
- **B1.2.3** Essential and non-essential amino acids in diet: Explain that essential amino acids must come from food while non-essential ones can be made from others; vegans must plan diets to get all essential ones. Named examples are not needed.
- **B1.2.4** Almost unlimited possible polypeptides: Explain that 20 genetically coded amino acids, in any order and chains of any length from a few to thousands, allow huge variety. Know some example polypeptides.
- **B1.2.5** Denaturation by heat and pH: Explain how high temperature and pH changes alter a protein's structure, and use the term denaturation.
- **B1.2.6** `AHL` R-group chemistry drives protein diversity: Explain that R-groups set a polypeptide's properties: they may be hydrophobic or hydrophilic, and hydrophilic ones may be polar or charged (acidic or basic). Specific R-groups need not be named.

- **B1.2.8** `AHL` Secondary structure: helices and sheets: Describe how hydrogen bonds at regular intervals along the backbone stabilise alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets.
- **B1.2.9** `AHL` Bonds that hold tertiary structure: Explain how hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges between cysteines and hydrophobic interactions stabilise tertiary structure, including how R-group amine and carboxyl groups gain or lose H+ to become charged.
- **B1.2.10** `AHL` Polar and non-polar amino acids in folding: Explain that water-soluble globular proteins bury hydrophobic amino acids in their core, while integral membrane proteins have hydrophobic regions that anchor them in the bilayer.
- **B1.2.11** `AHL` Quaternary structure and conjugated proteins: Describe proteins made of several chains, contrasting non-conjugated insulin and collagen with conjugated haemoglobin, which has non-protein haem groups. Appreciate how cryo-electron microscopy images single proteins.
- **B1.2.12** `AHL` Globular versus fibrous proteins: Contrast the shapes of globular and fibrous proteins and explain how shape suits function, using insulin and collagen as examples.

<a id="b2-1-membranes-and-membrane-transport"></a>
### B2.1 Membranes and membrane transport

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Fluid mosaic membrane in 3D plus transport processes over time

**Big questions:** How do lipids and proteins self-assemble into a working membrane? / What decides which substances get through a membrane, and how fast?

- **B2.1.1** Lipid bilayers form cell membranes: Explain that phospholipids and other amphipathic lipids spontaneously form continuous bilayer sheets in water, which is the basis of every cell membrane.
- **B2.1.2** Bilayers as barriers: Explain that the hydrophobic core of a membrane is poorly permeable to large molecules, ions and polar molecules, so it separates two watery solutions effectively.
- **B2.1.3** Simple diffusion through the bilayer: Describe simple diffusion across membranes using oxygen and carbon dioxide slipping between phospholipids as the example.
- **B2.1.4** Integral and peripheral proteins: Distinguish integral proteins (embedded in one or both lipid layers) from peripheral proteins (attached to a surface), and appreciate the variety of membrane protein structures and roles.
- **B2.1.5** Osmosis and aquaporins: Explain osmosis using random particle motion, membranes that solutes cannot cross and differences in solute concentration, and describe how aquaporins speed water movement.
- **B2.1.6** Channel proteins and facilitated diffusion: Explain how channel protein structure lets specific ions diffuse through only when the channel is open, making membranes selectively permeable.
- **B2.1.7** Pump proteins and active transport: Explain that pump proteins use energy from ATP to move specific particles across membranes, which allows movement against a concentration gradient.
- **B2.1.8** How membranes achieve selectivity: Contrast selective transport by facilitated diffusion and active transport with simple diffusion, which is unselective and depends only on particle size and polarity.
- **B2.1.9** Glycoproteins and glycolipids: Describe carbohydrate chains attached to membrane proteins and lipids, their position on the outer surface, and their roles in cell adhesion and recognition. Go no further than this.
- **B2.1.10** Fluid mosaic model: Draw a 2D fluid mosaic model labelling phospholipids, cholesterol, integral and peripheral proteins and glycoproteins, and mark the hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions.
- **B2.1.11** `AHL` Fatty acid type and membrane fluidity: Explain that unsaturated fatty acids keep membranes fluid and flexible, while saturated ones strengthen them at high temperature. Know one example of membrane composition adapted to habitat.
- **B2.1.12** `AHL` Cholesterol as a fluidity buffer: Describe where cholesterol sits in animal membranes and how it moderates fluidity, stabilising membranes when warm and stopping them stiffening when cold.
- **B2.1.13** `AHL` Vesicles, endocytosis and exocytosis: Explain how membrane fluidity allows vesicles to form and fuse, using the terms endocytosis and exocytosis with an example of each.
- **B2.1.14** `AHL` Gated ion channels in neurons: Describe neurotransmitter-gated channels, using the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels.
- **B2.1.15** `AHL` Sodium–potassium pump: Describe the sodium–potassium pump as an exchange transporter and explain its importance in setting up membrane potentials.
- **B2.1.16** `AHL` Sodium-dependent glucose cotransport: Explain sodium–glucose cotransporters as indirect active transport, and their role in absorbing glucose in the small intestine and reabsorbing it in the nephron.
- **B2.1.17** `AHL` Cell adhesion molecules and tissues: Explain that cells stick together into tissues using cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs), with different CAMs for different junction types. Detailed knowledge of specific CAMs or junctions is not needed.

**Named examples:** oxygen and carbon dioxide (simple diffusion); aquaporins; cholesterol; nicotinic acetylcholine receptor; voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels; sodium–potassium pump; sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter (small intestine, nephron); cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs); membrane adaptation to habitat (one example)

**Skills:** draw a 2D fluid mosaic model with phospholipids, cholesterol, integral and peripheral proteins and glycoproteins, marking hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions

<a id="b2-2-organelles-and-compartmentalization"></a>
### B2.2 Organelles and compartmentalization

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 1, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Mitochondrion, chloroplast, nucleus and ER→Golgi→vesicle traffic are 3D and dynamic

**Big questions:** Why does dividing a cell into separate compartments make it more efficient? / How does the structure of each organelle fit the job it does?

- **B2.2.1** What counts as an organelle: Define organelles as discrete cell subunits specialised for a function: nuclei, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane count; the cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm do not. Know how ultracentrifuge cell fractionation enabled organelle study.
- **B2.2.2** Benefit of separating nucleus and cytoplasm: Explain that a nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation, so mRNA can be modified before reaching ribosomes, whereas in prokaryotes ribosomes can meet mRNA straight away.
- **B2.2.3** Compartments in the cytoplasm: Explain how compartments concentrate enzymes and metabolites and keep incompatible reactions apart, using lysosomes and phagocytic vacuoles as examples.
- **B2.2.4** `AHL` Mitochondrion adapted for aerobic respiration: Relate mitochondrial structure to ATP production: a double membrane enclosing a small intermembrane space, folded cristae with large surface area, and a matrix holding Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates.
- **B2.2.5** `AHL` Chloroplast adapted for photosynthesis: Relate chloroplast structure to function: extensive thylakoid membranes carrying photosystems, small fluid volumes inside thylakoids, and Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated in the stroma.
- **B2.2.6** `AHL` Double membrane of the nucleus: Explain the benefits of the nuclear double membrane, why it needs pores, and why it breaks into vesicles during mitosis and meiosis.
- **B2.2.7** `AHL` Free ribosomes and rough ER: Contrast free ribosomes, which make proteins kept in the cell, with ribosomes on the rough ER, which make proteins for transport elsewhere or secretion.
- **B2.2.8** `AHL` Golgi apparatus: Describe the Golgi apparatus structure and its role in processing proteins and preparing them for secretion. Other Golgi functions are not required.
- **B2.2.9** `AHL` Vesicles and clathrin: Describe the structure and roles of vesicles in moving materials around the cell, including how clathrin helps vesicles form.

**Named examples:** ultracentrifuge cell fractionation; lysosomes; phagocytic vacuoles; mitochondrion; chloroplast; nuclear pores; rough endoplasmic reticulum; Golgi apparatus; clathrin

### B2.3 Cell specialization

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 1 · visualisation **medium**: SA:V is a variable relationship; cell adaptations compare well side by side

**Big questions:** How does one fertilised egg give rise to hundreds of very different cell types? / Why are cells small, and how do specialised cells get around the limits of size?

- **B2.3.1** From zygote to specialised cells: Describe how fertilisation produces unspecialised cells that then differentiate, and explain how chemical gradients in the early embryo switch different genes on in different cells.
- **B2.3.2** What makes a stem cell: State the two defining properties of stem cells: they can keep dividing indefinitely and can differentiate along different pathways.

### B3.1 Gas exchange

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 1 · visualisation **high**: Alveolus in 3D, ventilation over time, oxygen dissociation curve and Bohr shift

**Big questions:** Why do bigger organisms need special organs for gas exchange? / How do a leaf and a lung solve the same problem in different ways?

### B3.2 Transport

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Heart and cardiac cycle, transpiration and tissue fluid are processes over time

**Big questions:** How do animals and plants move fluids around large bodies using pressure? / In what ways are blood vessels and xylem alike, and how are they different?

- **B3.2.1** Capillary adaptations for exchange: Explain how capillaries suit exchange: branching and narrow diameter give large surface area, walls are thin, and some have fenestrations where very rapid exchange is needed.

- **B3.2.12** `AHL` Tissue fluid exchange with cells: Describe how substances pass between tissue fluid and body cells, and compare the composition of plasma with tissue fluid.
- **B3.2.13** `AHL` Lymph drainage: Explain how excess tissue fluid enters thin-walled, gapped lymph ducts with valves and is returned to the blood. Go no further than this.
- **B3.2.14** `AHL` Single versus double circulation: Compare single circulation in bony fish with double circulation in mammals using simple circuit diagrams showing the order of organs blood passes through.
- **B3.2.15** `AHL` Heart structure for pumping: Relate cardiac muscle, pacemaker, atria, ventricles, AV and semilunar valves, septum and coronary vessels to function; label them on a frontal-section heart diagram and trace blood flow from named veins to arteries.
- **B3.2.16** `AHL` Cardiac cycle: Describe the sequence of events on the left side of the heart after the sinoatrial node starts a beat, and interpret systolic and diastolic pressures from data and graphs.
- **B3.2.17** `AHL` Root pressure: Explain that active transport of mineral ions into roots creates positive root pressure, which moves water up when transpiration is too low, such as in humid conditions or before leaves open in spring.
- **B3.2.18** `AHL` Phloem sieve tubes and companion cells: Relate sieve plates, reduced cytoplasm, no nucleus in sieve tube elements, mitochondria-rich companion cells and linking plasmodesmata to easy sap flow and loading at sources and unloading at sinks.

### B4.2 Ecological niches

**Ecosystems** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 0 · visualisation **medium**: Niche and competitive exclusion are relationships between variables

**Big questions:** What are the many different ways organisms get the energy and matter they need? / How do competing species share an ecosystem without one always winning?

- **B4.2.8** Hominid teeth and diet: Infer the diet of herbivorous and omnivorous hominids from skull and teeth features using models or digital collections, for example Homo sapiens, Homo floresiensis and Paranthropus robustus.
- **B4.2.9** Herbivores versus plant defences: Describe herbivore adaptations such as piercing and chewing insect mouthparts, plant defences such as thorns and toxic secondary compounds, and animal adaptations for detoxifying these toxins.
- **B4.2.10** Predator and prey adaptations: Describe chemical, physical and behavioural adaptations predators use to find, catch and kill prey, and that prey use to avoid being eaten.
- **B4.2.11** Plant strategies for capturing light: Using forest examples, describe how canopy trees, lianas, epiphytes, strangler epiphytes and shade-tolerant shrubs and herbs on the forest floor each obtain light.
- **B4.2.12** Fundamental and realized niches: Distinguish the fundamental niche, set by a species' adaptations and tolerances, from the realized niche it actually occupies when competing with other species.
- **B4.2.13** Competitive exclusion: Explain that two species cannot share an identical niche: competition either eliminates one species or restricts both to part of their fundamental niches.

**Named examples:** Euglena; oceanic plankton mixotrophs; saprotrophic fungi and bacteria; archaea; Homo sapiens; Homo floresiensis; Paranthropus robustus; leaf-eating insects; lianas; epiphytes; strangler epiphytes

**Skills:** examine models or digital collections of hominid skulls to infer diet from dentition

## Theme C — Interaction and interdependence

<a id="c1-1-enzymes-and-metabolism"></a>
### C1.1 Enzymes and metabolism

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Rate vs temperature, pH and substrate concentration, and inhibition types, are variable relationships

**Big questions:** How does an enzyme's shape let it speed up one particular reaction? / How do thousands of linked reactions in a cell stay coordinated and under control?

- **C1.1.1** Enzymes speed up cell reactions: Enzymes are biological catalysts; explain why cells benefit from reactions running much faster than they would without a catalyst.
- **C1.1.2** Enzymes control the metabolic network: Metabolism is a web of interlinked reactions. Because each enzyme is specific, organisms need many different enzymes, and controlling enzymes is how metabolism is regulated.
- **C1.1.3** Anabolism versus catabolism: Anabolism builds macromolecules from monomers by condensation (e.g. protein synthesis, glycogen formation, photosynthesis); catabolism breaks them down, e.g. hydrolysis in digestion and oxidation of substrates in respiration.
- **C1.1.4** Globular enzymes and their active sites: Enzymes are globular proteins. The active site is made of only a few amino acids, but the whole 3D fold of the protein gives it the properties needed for catalysis.
- **C1.1.5** Induced fit between substrate and enzyme: Describe induced-fit binding, recognising that the shapes of both the substrate and the enzyme change as they bind.
- **C1.1.6** Molecular motion and collisions in catalysis: Substrate and active site must collide to react, so molecular movement matters; note that sometimes the substrate is fixed in place (large molecules) and sometimes the enzyme is (embedded in membranes).
- **C1.1.7** Active site shape, specificity and denaturation: Be able to explain how the active site's structure gives an enzyme its specificity for a substrate, and how denaturation alters that structure and stops catalysis.
- **C1.1.8** Temperature, pH and substrate concentration effects: Explain how temperature, pH and substrate concentration affect enzyme activity using collision theory and denaturation; interpret graphs of these effects and treat sketch graphs as models testable with experimental data.
- **C1.1.9** Measuring enzyme reaction rates: Work out rates of enzyme-catalysed reactions both from your own experiments and from secondary data.
- **C1.1.10** Enzymes lower activation energy: Energy is needed to break substrate bonds and released when product bonds form; enzymes reduce the activation energy. Interpret energy diagrams showing this.
- **C1.1.11** `AHL` Reactions inside versus outside cells: Contrast intracellular enzyme reactions (glycolysis and the Krebs cycle) with extracellular ones such as chemical digestion in the gut.
- **C1.1.12** `AHL` Metabolism releases heat: Because metabolic energy transfers are never fully efficient, heat is always produced; mammals, birds and some other animals rely on this heat to keep body temperature constant.
- **C1.1.13** `AHL` Linear and cyclical metabolic pathways: Distinguish linear from cyclical pathways using glycolysis (linear) and the Krebs and Calvin cycles (cyclical) as examples.
- **C1.1.14** `AHL` Allosteric sites and non-competitive inhibition: Only specific molecules fit an allosteric site; their reversible binding changes the enzyme's conformation so the active site no longer catalyses the reaction.
- **C1.1.15** `AHL` Competitive inhibition at the active site: A competitive inhibitor reversibly occupies the active site (example: statins). Compare competitive and non-competitive inhibition, including how raising substrate concentration affects each.
- **C1.1.16** `AHL` End-product feedback inhibition: Explain how an end product inhibits an early enzyme in its own pathway, using isoleucine synthesis as the example.
- **C1.1.17** `AHL` Irreversible mechanism-based inhibition: Some inhibitors bind permanently and chemically alter the active site; use penicillin acting on transpeptidases, and explain how altered transpeptidases give bacteria penicillin resistance.

**Named examples:** protein synthesis; glycogen formation; photosynthesis; digestion; cell respiration; glycolysis; Krebs cycle; Calvin cycle; statins; isoleucine synthesis pathway; penicillin; transpeptidases

**Skills:** interpret graphs of enzyme activity against temperature, pH and substrate concentration; evaluate sketch-graph models using experimental enzyme data; determine reaction rates from experiments and secondary data; interpret activation-energy graphs

<a id="c1-2-cell-respiration"></a>
### C1.2 Cell respiration

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 3 · visualisation **high**: Respiration stages are located in a 3D mitochondrion; chemiosmosis happens over time

**Big questions:** How do cells capture energy from food molecules in a form they can spend? / Why does having oxygen make such a difference to how much ATP a cell can make?

- **C1.2.1** ATP as the cell's energy currency: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide; know its full name and the properties that make it well suited to carrying and distributing energy within cells.
- **C1.2.2** Cell processes powered by ATP: ATP fuels active transport across membranes, anabolic synthesis of macromolecules, and movement of whole cells or parts of cells such as chromosomes.
- **C1.2.3** Energy changes in ATP–ADP interconversion: Hydrolysing ATP to ADP and phosphate releases energy, while making ATP from them requires energy. No kilojoule values needed, only that the amount suits many cell tasks.
- **C1.2.4** Respiration makes ATP from carbon compounds: Cell respiration releases energy from organic compounds (mainly glucose and fatty acids, but many others possible) to make ATP; distinguish respiration from gas exchange.
- **C1.2.5** Aerobic versus anaerobic respiration in humans: Compare substrates, oxygen need, relative ATP yield, waste products and location in the cell; write word equations for both with glucose, noting only aerobic respiration needs mitochondria.
- **C1.2.6** Factors affecting respiration rate: Take measurements to find the rate of cell respiration and calculate rates from your own raw data or from secondary data.
- **C1.2.7** `AHL` NAD as a hydrogen carrier in oxidation: Oxidation is loss of electrons, so removing hydrogen (dehydrogenation) oxidises a substrate. Redox reactions pair oxidation with reduction; NAD is reduced when it gains hydrogen.
- **C1.2.8** `AHL` Glycolysis: glucose to pyruvate: Outline glycolysis as phosphorylation, lysis, oxidation and ATP formation, giving a net gain of ATP and reduced NAD; each step uses a different enzyme, but intermediate names are not needed.
- **C1.2.9** `AHL` Lactate formation regenerates NAD: In anaerobic respiration in humans, pyruvate is reduced to lactate. This regenerates NAD so glycolysis can continue, giving only 2 ATP (net) per glucose.
- **C1.2.10** `AHL` Yeast fermentation in brewing and baking: Yeast and human anaerobic pathways match except for how pyruvate regenerates NAD, giving different end products; relate yeast's products to brewing and baking.
- **C1.2.11** `AHL` The link reaction: Pyruvate is oxidised and decarboxylated to an acetyl group (2C); carbohydrates and lipids both yield acetyl groups, which coenzyme A carries into the Krebs cycle.
- **C1.2.12** `AHL` The Krebs cycle: Acetyl joins oxaloacetate (4C) to form citrate (6C); the cycle's four oxidations (dehydrogenations) and two decarboxylations regenerate oxaloacetate, producing ATP and reduced NAD. Only citrate and oxaloacetate need naming.
- **C1.2.13** `AHL` Reduced NAD feeds the electron transport chain: Reduced NAD made in glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle passes a pair of electrons to the first carrier of the electron transport chain and is re-oxidised to NAD.
- **C1.2.14** `AHL` Electron flow builds a proton gradient: Explain how electrons passing along the transport chain pump protons to create a gradient; names of the protein complexes are not required.
- **C1.2.15** `AHL` Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase in mitochondria: Explain how ATP synthase uses the energy of protons flowing down their gradient to phosphorylate ADP into ATP.
- **C1.2.16** `AHL` Oxygen as the final electron acceptor: Oxygen takes electrons from the end of the chain plus protons from the matrix, forming metabolic water and keeping electrons flowing.
- **C1.2.17** `AHL` Lipids versus carbohydrates as respiratory fuels: Lipids release more energy per gram (less oxygen, more oxidisable H and C). Only carbohydrate enters glycolysis or anaerobic respiration; fatty acids enter as 2C acetyl groups via acetyl-CoA.

**Named examples:** ATP; ADP; NAD; glycolysis; lactate; yeast; brewing; baking; coenzyme A; acetyl-CoA; citrate; oxaloacetate; Krebs cycle; electron transport chain; ATP synthase; mitochondrion

**Skills:** write word equations for aerobic and anaerobic respiration of glucose; measure and calculate rates of cell respiration from experimental or secondary data

<a id="c1-3-photosynthesis"></a>
### C1.3 Photosynthesis

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 3 · visualisation **high**: Light-dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle are processes; limiting factors are relationships

**Big questions:** How does a leaf turn light into the chemical energy stored in sugar? / What sets the upper limit on how fast a plant can photosynthesise?

- **C1.3.1** Light energy becomes chemical energy: Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in carbon compounds, and this supplies most of the chemical energy used by life in ecosystems.
- **C1.3.2** Carbon dioxide reduced using hydrogen from water: Photosynthesis reduces carbon dioxide to glucose using hydrogen obtained by splitting water; be able to give the word equation.
- **C1.3.3** Oxygen released as a by-product: Plants, algae and cyanobacteria release oxygen as a by-product; know the word equation and that the oxygen comes from splitting water molecules.
- **C1.3.4** Chromatography of photosynthetic pigments: Separate pigments by thin-layer or paper chromatography, calculate Rf values, and identify each pigment by its colour and Rf value.
- **C1.3.5** Pigments absorb particular wavelengths: Absorbed light excites electrons in pigment molecules, converting light to chemical energy; explain why only some wavelengths are absorbed and read absorption spectra labelled with both wavelengths and colours.
- **C1.3.6** Absorption spectra compared with action spectra: Compare absorption and action spectra; calculate photosynthesis rates at different wavelengths from oxygen output or carbon dioxide uptake and plot these as an action spectrum.
- **C1.3.7** Investigating limiting factors experimentally: Use methods to vary CO2 concentration, light intensity or temperature; propose and test hypotheses about limiting factors and identify independent and dependent variables.
- **C1.3.8** CO2 enrichment experiments and future growth: Use enclosed greenhouse studies and free-air CO2 enrichment (FACE) experiments to predict future photosynthesis and plant growth; identify controlled variables and weigh lab versus field control.
- **C1.3.9** `AHL` Photosystems as pigment arrays: Photosystems are membrane-bound arrays of chlorophyll and accessory pigments, found in cyanobacteria and chloroplasts, with a special reaction-centre chlorophyll that emits an excited electron.
- **C1.3.10** `AHL` Why pigments work as organised arrays: Explain the advantages of grouping many pigment types into a photosystem, recognising that one isolated pigment molecule could not carry out any part of photosynthesis.
- **C1.3.11** `AHL` Photolysis of water in photosystem II: Photosystem II splits water; its protons and electrons are used in photosynthesis while oxygen is waste. Appreciate the huge biological and geological impact of oxygen production on Earth.
- **C1.3.12** `AHL` Chemiosmosis makes ATP in thylakoids: Electron carriers pump protons to build a gradient that drives ATP synthase; electrons come from photosystem I in cyclic or photosystem II in non-cyclic photophosphorylation.
- **C1.3.13** `AHL` Photosystem I reduces NADP: NADP is reduced by gaining two electrons from photosystem I and a hydrogen ion from the stroma; use paired terms consistently (NADP/reduced NADP or NADP+/NADPH).
- **C1.3.14** `AHL` Thylakoid as light-dependent reaction site: Locate where in the thylakoid photolysis, chemiosmotic ATP synthesis and NADP reduction take place.
- **C1.3.15** `AHL` Carbon fixation by Rubisco: Rubisco fixes CO2 onto RuBP to give glycerate 3-phosphate. It is Earth's most abundant enzyme and is needed in high amounts in the stroma because it is slow and weak at low CO2.
- **C1.3.16** `AHL` Making triose phosphate: Glycerate 3-phosphate (GP) is converted to triose phosphate (TP) using ATP and reduced NADP.
- **C1.3.17** `AHL` RuBP regeneration keeps the Calvin cycle going: Using ATP, five triose phosphates are rearranged into three RuBP; if glucose is the product, five-sixths of TP must be recycled. Individual reaction details are not needed.
- **C1.3.18** `AHL` Building other compounds from Calvin cycle products: All carbon in a photosynthesiser is fixed in the Calvin cycle; carbohydrates, amino acids and other compounds are made, with mineral nutrients, from cycle intermediates. No pathway details required.
- **C1.3.19** `AHL` Light-dependent and light-independent reactions depend on each other: Explain why darkness halts the light-dependent reactions and why a shortage of CO2 stops photosystem II working.

**Named examples:** plants; algae; cyanobacteria; chlorophyll; accessory pigments; thin-layer chromatography; paper chromatography; greenhouse CO2 enrichment experiments; FACE (free-air carbon dioxide enrichment) experiments; photosystem I; photosystem II; Rubisco; RuBP; glycerate 3-phosphate; triose phosphate; NADP; Calvin cycle

**Skills:** write a word equation for photosynthesis; calculate Rf values and identify pigments from a chromatogram; read absorption spectra labelled with wavelengths and colours; calculate photosynthesis rates from O2/CO2 data and plot an action spectrum; design experiments on limiting factors, stating hypotheses and identifying independent, dependent and controlled variables

<a id="c2-1-chemical-signalling"></a>
### C2.1 Chemical signalling

**Cells** · AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 0, AHL 4 · visualisation **high**: Signal-transduction pathways are step-by-step processes

**Big questions:** How can a cell pick out one chemical message among the many it is exposed to? / What happens inside a cell between a signal arriving and the cell responding?

- **C2.1.1** `AHL` Receptors bind specific signalling chemicals: Receptors are proteins with binding sites specific to particular signalling chemicals; use the term ligand for the signal.
- **C2.1.2** `AHL` Quorum sensing in bacteria: Bacteria use chemical signals to sense population density and act together; use bioluminescence in the marine bacterium Vibrio fischeri as the example.
- **C2.1.3** `AHL` Functional categories of animal signals: Distinguish hormones, neurotransmitters, cytokines and calcium ions as different functional categories of signalling chemical in animals.
- **C2.1.4** `AHL` Chemical variety of hormones and neurotransmitters: Hormones may be amines, proteins or steroids; neurotransmitters include amino acids, peptides, amines and nitric oxide. Suggest why so many different chemicals serve as signals.
- **C2.1.5** `AHL` Local versus long-distance signalling: Contrast blood-borne hormones acting at a distance with neurotransmitters diffusing across a synaptic gap to act locally.
- **C2.1.6** `AHL` Membrane receptors versus intracellular receptors: Compare transmembrane and cytoplasmic/nuclear receptors, including the placement of hydrophilic and hydrophobic amino acids and whether the ligand enters the cell or stays outside.
- **C2.1.7** `AHL` Receptors trigger signal transduction: Ligand binding to a receptor starts a chain of responses inside the cell, known as a signal transduction pathway.
- **C2.1.8** `AHL` Neurotransmitter receptors alter membrane potential: Using the acetylcholine receptor: binding opens an ion channel in the receptor, positive ions diffuse in, and the resulting voltage change across the membrane can trigger further effects.
- **C2.1.9** `AHL` G protein-coupled receptors: Explain how G protein-coupled receptors pass a signal into the cell, and appreciate that humans have a very large number of them.
- **C2.1.10** `AHL` How epinephrine (adrenaline) receptors work: Outline epinephrine signalling via a G protein and cyclic AMP as second messenger; note that both names persist worldwide as an example of scientific naming conventions.
- **C2.1.11** `AHL` Tyrosine kinase receptors and insulin: Insulin binds a membrane receptor, causing tyrosine phosphorylation inside the cell and a cascade ending with vesicles carrying glucose transporters moving to the membrane. No further detail needed.
- **C2.1.12** `AHL` Intracellular receptors and gene expression: Steroid hormones (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone) bind and activate receptors inside the cell; the activated receptor binds specific DNA sequences to promote transcription.
- **C2.1.13** `AHL` Oestradiol and progesterone on target cells: Limit to oestradiol acting on hypothalamus cells that secrete GnRH, and progesterone acting on endometrium cells.
- **C2.1.14** `AHL` Positive and negative feedback in signalling: Distinguish positive from negative feedback regulation of signalling pathways and briefly outline one example of each.

**Named examples:** Vibrio fischeri bioluminescence; acetylcholine receptor; G protein-coupled receptors; epinephrine (adrenaline); cyclic AMP; insulin; glucose transporters; oestradiol; progesterone; testosterone; gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH); endometrium; cytokines; calcium ions; nitric oxide

<a id="c2-2-neural-signalling"></a>
### C2.2 Neural signalling

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 3 · visualisation **high**: Neuron and synapse are 3D; the action potential happens over time

**Big questions:** How does a neuron create and send an electrical signal using only ions? / How does one neuron pass its message to the next cell?

- **C2.2.1** Neurons carry electrical impulses: A neuron has a cell body (nucleus and cytoplasm) with long fibres: a single long axon and several shorter dendrites, along which impulses are conducted.
- **C2.2.2** Sodium–potassium pumping sets resting potential: ATP-driven pumping moves Na+ and K+ in opposite directions, building gradients; explain membrane polarisation, membrane potential and why the resting potential is negative.
- **C2.2.3** Nerve impulses as travelling action potentials: A nerve impulse is an action potential propagated along a fibre; it is electrical because positively charged ions move.
- **C2.2.4** Why impulse speeds differ: Compare speeds in squid giant axons versus thin unmyelinated fibres, and myelinated versus unmyelinated fibres; describe correlations and use correlation coefficients and R² to judge their strength.
- **C2.2.5** Synapses as junctions between cells: Synapses link neurons to neurons or to effector cells; consider chemical synapses only, and understand that signals cross them in one direction.
- **C2.2.6** Neurotransmitter release at the presynaptic membrane: Depolarisation of the presynaptic membrane causes calcium ions to enter, and calcium acts as an internal signal triggering neurotransmitter release.
- **C2.2.7** Excitatory postsynaptic potentials: Neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds transmembrane receptors, generating an excitatory potential; use acetylcholine, which acts at many synapses including neuromuscular junctions.
- **C2.2.8** `AHL` Depolarisation and repolarisation in action potentials: Explain the roles of voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels, and why the threshold potential must be reached before sodium channels open.
- **C2.2.9** `AHL` Local currents propagate action potentials: Explain how sodium ions diffusing along the inside and outside of the axon bring the next region to threshold, so the impulse moves along the fibre.
- **C2.2.10** `AHL` Reading oscilloscope traces of nerve activity: Interpret oscilloscope traces of resting and action potentials in terms of events at the membrane, and measure impulse frequency (impulses per second).
- **C2.2.11** `AHL` Saltatory conduction in myelinated fibres: Pumps and channels are concentrated at nodes of Ranvier, so the action potential jumps from node to node, making conduction faster.
- **C2.2.12** `AHL` External chemicals that disrupt synapses: Use neonicotinoid pesticides as an example of blocking synaptic transmission, and cocaine as an example of blocking neurotransmitter reuptake.
- **C2.2.13** `AHL` Inhibitory neurotransmitters and hyperpolarisation: Inhibitory neurotransmitters produce inhibitory postsynaptic potentials by hyperpolarising the postsynaptic membrane.
- **C2.2.14** `AHL` Summation of excitatory and inhibitory inputs: Inputs from many presynaptic neurons combine in the postsynaptic neuron, and their net effect decides, all-or-nothing, whether it depolarises to fire.
- **C2.2.15** `AHL` Pain detection by free nerve endings: Free nerve endings in the skin contain channels that open in response to heat, acid or chemicals such as capsaicin; the inflow of positive ions can trigger impulses that the brain interprets as pain.
- **C2.2.16** `AHL` Consciousness as an emergent property: Consciousness arises from interactions among many individual neurons in the brain, an example of an emergent property.

**Named examples:** squid giant axon; myelinated and unmyelinated fibres; nodes of Ranvier; acetylcholine; neuromuscular junction; calcium ions; neonicotinoids; cocaine; capsaicin (chilli peppers); free nerve endings

**Skills:** describe positive and negative correlations and apply correlation coefficients; use the coefficient of determination (R²) to evaluate how much variation is explained; interpret oscilloscope traces of resting and action potentials; measure impulse frequency from a trace

<a id="c3-1-integration-of-body-systems"></a>
### C3.1 Integration of body systems

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 2 · visualisation **medium**: Reflex arcs, feedback loops and phototropism animate well in 2D

**Big questions:** How do nerves and hormones work together so the body acts as one coordinated whole? / How do feedback loops keep vital variables like heart and breathing rate in check?

- **C3.1.1** Why systems need integration: Living systems need integration: their parts must be coordinated to achieve an overall function together.
- **C3.1.2** Hierarchy of cells, tissues, organs, systems: Cells, tissues, organs and body systems form nested subsystems; their integration produces emergent properties, e.g. a cheetah's body systems combine to make it an effective predator.
- **C3.1.3** Organs linked by signals and transport: Distinguish how nervous and endocrine systems send messages, and use examples to stress the blood system's role in moving materials and energy between organs.
- **C3.1.4** The brain integrates information: The brain combines and processes information from many inputs and underlies learning and memory; detail such as slow-acting neurotransmitters is not required.
- **C3.1.5** Spinal cord handles unconscious processes: The spinal cord coordinates processes that happen without conscious thought; be able to tell conscious and unconscious processes apart.
- **C3.1.6** Sensory neurons carry input to the CNS: Sensory neurons carry messages from receptor cells into the spinal cord and cerebral hemispheres.
- **C3.1.7** Motor neurons carry output to muscles: Motor neurons carry signals from the cerebral hemispheres to muscles, stimulating them to contract.
- **C3.1.8** Structure of a nerve: A nerve is a bundle of sensory and motor fibres; use a transverse section to show its protective sheath and both myelinated and unmyelinated fibres.
- **C3.1.9** The pain reflex arc: Describe an involuntary pain reflex from a free nerve ending in the hand, through one interneuron in the spinal cord's grey matter, to a skeletal muscle effector.
- **C3.1.10** Cerebellum coordinates movement and balance: Give a general account of how the cerebellum coordinates skeletal muscle contraction and balance in overall body movement.
- **C3.1.11** Melatonin and the sleep–wake cycle: The pineal gland secretes melatonin in a daily (diurnal) pattern that helps set the circadian cycle of sleeping and waking.
- **C3.1.12** Epinephrine prepares for vigorous activity: The adrenal glands release epinephrine (adrenaline), whose widespread effects on the body support intense muscle contraction.
- **C3.1.13** Hypothalamus and pituitary control hormones: Give a general account of how the hypothalamus and pituitary control the endocrine system; anterior versus posterior pituitary differences are not needed.
- **C3.1.14** Feedback control of heart rate: Baroreceptors (blood pressure) and chemoreceptors (pH, O2, CO2) send input to the medulla, which signals the heart to adjust rate and stroke volume. Include receptor locations.
- **C3.1.15** Feedback control of ventilation rate: Explain what causes blood pH to change; brainstem chemoreceptors detect this and signals to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles adjust ventilation rate.
- **C3.1.16** Nervous control of peristalsis: Swallowing and egestion are voluntary, controlled by the CNS; peristalsis in between is involuntary, coordinated by the enteric nervous system (ENS).
- **C3.1.17** `AHL` Observing tropisms in seedlings: Record seedling tropisms qualitatively with diagrams and quantitatively by measuring curvature angle; distinguish qualitative from quantitative data and consider precision, accuracy and reliability.
- **C3.1.18** `AHL` Positive phototropism in shoots: Shoots grow towards light coming from one side, a directional growth response; other specific tropisms are not required.
- **C3.1.19** `AHL` Phytohormones as plant signals: Plants use a variety of chemicals as phytohormones to control growth, development and responses to stimuli.
- **C3.1.20** `AHL` Auxin efflux carriers create gradients: Auxin diffuses into plant cells but can only leave through efflux carriers. If neighbouring cells place these carriers on matching sides, auxin is moved from cell to cell and builds up in one region.
- **C3.1.21** `AHL` Auxin promotes cell elongation: Auxin causes H+ secretion into the apoplast, acidifying and loosening cellulose cross-links so cells elongate; auxin gradients produce the uneven growth behind phototropism.
- **C3.1.22** `AHL` Auxin–cytokinin interplay in roots and shoots: Root tips make cytokinin that travels to shoots, shoot tips make auxin that travels to roots; their interaction keeps root and shoot growth coordinated.
- **C3.1.23** `AHL` Ethylene and positive feedback in ripening: Ethylene (ethene) triggers ripening and ripening boosts ethylene output; explain how this positive feedback makes ripening fast and synchronised.

**Named examples:** cheetah; pineal gland and melatonin; adrenal glands and epinephrine; hypothalamus; pituitary gland; cerebellum; medulla; baroreceptors; chemoreceptors; enteric nervous system; pain reflex arc in the hand; seedlings; auxin; auxin efflux carriers; cytokinin; ethylene (ethene)

**Skills:** interpret a transverse section of a nerve; record tropic responses of seedlings using diagrams; measure the angle of curvature of seedlings; distinguish qualitative and quantitative data and assess precision, accuracy and reliability

<a id="c3-2-defence-against-disease"></a>
### C3.2 Defence against disease

**Organisms** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 0 · visualisation **high**: The immune response is a multi-step process over time

**Big questions:** How does the body tell invaders apart from its own cells and destroy them? / Why do some infections spread through populations while others die out?

- **C3.2.1** Pathogens cause infectious disease: Many organisms can infect humans; pathogens are usually viruses, bacteria, fungi and protists, and no archaea are known to cause human disease. Note how observation of childbed fever (Vienna) and cholera (London) advanced disease control.
- **C3.2.2** Skin and mucous membranes as first barrier: The skin and mucous membranes are the first line of defence; skin blocks pathogens both physically and chemically. Diagrams of skin are not needed.
- **C3.2.3** Blood clotting seals cuts: Platelets release clotting factors, starting a cascade in which thrombin rapidly turns fibrinogen into fibrin, which traps red blood cells to form a clot. No further detail needed.
- **C3.2.4** Innate versus adaptive immunity: The innate system responds to broad pathogen groups and stays the same through life; the adaptive system responds specifically and builds memory so responses improve. Phagocytes are the only innate component needed.
- **C3.2.5** Phagocytes control infection: Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries by amoeboid movement to reach infection sites, recognise pathogens, take them in by endocytosis and break them down with enzymes from lysosomes.
- **C3.2.6** Lymphocytes cooperate to make antibodies: Lymphocytes circulate in blood and reside in lymph nodes; a person has huge numbers of B-lymphocytes, each producing one specific type of antibody.
- **C3.2.7** Antigens trigger antibody production: Antigens are usually glycoproteins or proteins on pathogen surfaces; antigens on red blood cells can trigger antibody production after transfusion into someone of a different blood group.
- **C3.2.8** Helper T-cells activate B-cells: B-cells and helper T-cells are antigen-specific; a B-cell is activated only by binding its antigen and contacting a helper T-cell activated by the same antigen, after which it makes antibodies and memory cells.
- **C3.2.9** Clonal expansion into plasma cells: Few B-cells match a given antigen, so activated ones divide by mitosis into large clones of plasma cells all secreting the same antibody.
- **C3.2.10** Memory cells give immunity: Immunity is the ability to clear a specific infection, due to long-lived memory lymphocytes able to make the needed antibodies.
- **C3.2.11** How HIV is transmitted: HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) spreads in body fluids; give examples of its transmission routes.
- **C3.2.12** HIV infects lymphocytes, leading to AIDS: HIV kills only certain lymphocyte types, but losing them weakens antibody production and the ability to fight opportunistic infections, resulting in AIDS.
- **C3.2.13** Antibiotics target bacterial processes: Antibiotics block processes found in bacteria but not eukaryotic cells; explain why they cannot treat viral infections.
- **C3.2.14** Evolution of multiple antibiotic resistance: Pathogenic bacteria evolve resistance to several antibiotics, so careful use is needed to slow multiresistant strains; new techniques like screening chemical libraries are finding new antibiotics.
- **C3.2.15** Zoonoses jump from animals to humans: Show how common zoonoses are and how they spread using tuberculosis, rabies and Japanese encephalitis, plus COVID-19 as a recent transfer with major consequences.
- **C3.2.16** Vaccines and immunisation: A vaccine supplies antigens (or DNA/RNA that codes for them) so the body builds immunity to a particular pathogen without the person developing the disease.
- **C3.2.17** Herd immunity prevents epidemics: When enough people are immune, transmission is greatly impeded, so individuals depend on each other; consider how vaccine research and its media reporting are evaluated.
- **C3.2.18** Analysing COVID-19 pandemic data: Evaluate data from the COVID-19 pandemic, calculating both percentage difference and percentage change.

**Named examples:** childbed fever in Vienna; cholera in London; platelets, thrombin, fibrinogen and fibrin; phagocytes; B-lymphocytes and helper T-lymphocytes; plasma cells; memory cells; blood groups and transfusion; HIV/AIDS; tuberculosis; rabies; Japanese encephalitis; COVID-19; chemical library screening for antibiotics

**Skills:** calculate percentage difference and percentage change from COVID-19 data

<a id="c4-1-populations-and-communities"></a>
### C4.1 Populations and communities

**Ecosystems** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 0 · visualisation **high**: Population growth and sampling are best learned by simulation

**Big questions:** What keeps a population from growing forever? / How do different species in a community depend on, compete with and limit each other?

- **C4.1.1** What counts as a population: A population is a group of the same species living and normally interbreeding in an area; reproductive isolation separates one population from another.
- **C4.1.2** Estimating population size by random sampling: Explain why populations are estimated rather than fully counted, why sampling must be random, and that sampling error is the gap between the estimate and the true size.
- **C4.1.3** Quadrat sampling for sessile organisms: Use random quadrats to estimate numbers of countable sessile plants or animals; use a calculator to find the standard deviation of the mean per quadrat and interpret it as spread (formula not required).
- **C4.1.4** Capture–mark–recapture and the Lincoln index: Estimate motile populations with the Lincoln index: population = M × N ÷ R (M first marked, N total recaptured, R marked recaptured); know the method's assumptions.
- **C4.1.5** Carrying capacity and resource competition: Give a simple definition of carrying capacity, with examples of resources whose limited supply sets it.
- **C4.1.6** Density-dependent negative feedback: Density-independent factors cause fluctuations, while density-dependent factors (competition, predation risk, spread of pathogens and pests) push populations back towards carrying capacity.
- **C4.1.7** Population growth curves: Study a real ecosystem case study; explain early exponential growth (no lag phase expected) and test against the exponential model by plotting population on a log scale against linear time.
- **C4.1.8** Modelling sigmoid growth: Collect population growth data to model a sigmoid curve, ideally using yeast or duckweed or another organism that multiplies readily in experiments.
- **C4.1.9** Competition and cooperation within a species: Explain why members of one species compete, and give a range of real examples of both intraspecific competition and cooperation.
- **C4.1.10** Communities of interacting organisms: A community is all the interacting populations in an ecosystem, including plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
- **C4.1.11** Types of interspecific relationship: Know herbivory, predation, interspecific competition, mutualism, parasitism and pathogenicity, with at least one example of each.
- **C4.1.12** Mutualism benefits both partners: Explain the benefits to both species in root nodules of legumes (Fabaceae), mycorrhizae of orchids (Orchidaceae), and zooxanthellae in hard corals.
- **C4.1.13** Invasive versus endemic species competition: Use one local example to show how an introduced species becomes invasive by outcompeting endemic species for resources.
- **C4.1.14** Testing for interspecific competition: One species doing better without another suggests, but does not prove, competition; compare lab experiments, random-sampling field observations and removal experiments, and distinguish experiments from observations.
- **C4.1.15** Chi-squared test for species association: Apply a chi-squared test to presence/absence data for two species across sampling sites to assess association, which may suggest interspecific competition.
- **C4.1.16** Predator–prey density-dependent control: Use a real case study to show predator–prey interactions as density-dependent control of animal populations.
- **C4.1.17** Top-down and bottom-up control: Communities can be regulated from the top (predators) or bottom (resources); both occur, though one usually dominates in a given community.
- **C4.1.18** Allelopathy and antibiotic secretion: Both involve releasing chemicals into the environment to deter competitors; give one specific example of each, preferably local.

**Named examples:** Lincoln index; yeast; duckweed; root nodules in Fabaceae (legumes); mycorrhizae in Orchidaceae (orchids); zooxanthellae in hard corals; local invasive species case study; population growth case study; predator–prey case study; allelopathy example; antibiotic secretion example

**Skills:** interpret standard deviation of mean quadrat counts (calculated on a calculator); calculate population size with the Lincoln index; plot population growth on a logarithmic vertical axis to test exponential growth; collect data to model sigmoid population growth; apply a chi-squared test to species presence/absence data

<a id="c4-2-transfers-of-energy-and-matter"></a>
### C4.2 Transfers of energy and matter

**Ecosystems** · SL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 0 · visualisation **high**: Energy pyramids and the carbon cycle are quantitative relationships

**Big questions:** Why do atoms cycle round an ecosystem while energy passes through and is lost? / Why are there so few top predators compared with plants?

- **C4.2.1** Ecosystems are open systems: Ecosystems are open: both energy and matter enter and leave. Contrast with closed systems, where only energy crosses the boundary.
- **C4.2.2** Sunlight powers most ecosystems: Sunlight is the main energy source for most ecosystems, with exceptions such as caves and the deep ocean beyond light; consider what makes a scientific law or generalisation useful.
- **C4.2.3** Chemical energy flows along food chains: Chemical energy passes to a consumer when it eats the organism at the previous stage of a food chain.
- **C4.2.4** Building food chains and webs: Construct food chains and webs, ideally for a local community, with arrows pointing in the direction energy and biomass move.
- **C4.2.5** Decomposers feed on dead organic matter: Decomposers get energy from carbon compounds in faeces, dead parts of organisms and whole dead organisms.
- **C4.2.6** Autotrophs make their own carbon compounds: Autotrophs use an external energy source to build carbon compounds from simple inorganic substances; energy is needed both for carbon fixation and for building macromolecules.
- **C4.2.7** Photoautotrophs versus chemoautotrophs: Photoautotrophs use light energy while chemoautotrophs use energy released by oxidation reactions; use iron-oxidising bacteria as the chemoautotroph example.
- **C4.2.8** Heterotrophs rely on other organisms' carbon: Heterotrophs digest complex carbon compounds (e.g. proteins, nucleic acids) externally or internally, then assimilate the products to build the compounds they need.
- **C4.2.9** Both trophic types respire: Autotrophs and heterotrophs alike release energy by oxidising carbon compounds in cell respiration; photoheterotrophs are not required.
- **C4.2.10** Assigning trophic levels: Use the terms producer, primary, secondary and tertiary consumer, recognising that organisms with varied diets can occupy different levels in different chains.
- **C4.2.11** Drawing energy pyramids: Build energy pyramids from real data for particular ecosystems, showing how much energy passes to each trophic level and how much is lost.
- **C4.2.12** Energy shrinks at each trophic level: Explain the causes of large energy losses between levels; decomposers and detritivores are usually outside food chains but still matter for energy transformations.
- **C4.2.13** Respiration loses heat to the environment: Energy transfers are inefficient, so heat is released both when respiration makes ATP and when cells use ATP, in autotrophs and heterotrophs alike.
- **C4.2.14** Energy loss limits food chain length: Higher levels have fewer or smaller organisms and less biomass, though energy per unit mass stays the same; this restricts how many trophic levels exist.
- **C4.2.15** Primary production by autotrophs: Primary production is biomass accumulated by autotrophs, measured as mass of carbon per area per time (usually g m⁻² yr⁻¹); biomes differ in how much they accumulate.
- **C4.2.16** Secondary production by heterotrophs: Secondary production is biomass built up by heterotrophs; it is lower than primary production because carbon is lost as CO2 and water in respiration.
- **C4.2.17** Drawing the carbon cycle: Draw a carbon cycle diagram showing carbon recycled through photosynthesis, feeding and respiration.
- **C4.2.18** Carbon sinks and carbon sources: An ecosystem is a net CO2 sink when photosynthesis exceeds respiration and a net source when respiration exceeds photosynthesis.
- **C4.2.19** Combustion releases stored carbon: Burning biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas releases CO2; these stores formed at different times, and although lightning fires occur naturally, humans have greatly raised combustion rates.
- **C4.2.20** Interpreting the Keeling Curve: Explain both the annual zig-zag and the long-term upward trend of the Keeling curve using photosynthesis, respiration and the burning of fuels.
- **C4.2.21** Respiration and photosynthesis depend on each other: Aerobic respiration relies on oxygen from photosynthesis and photosynthesis on CO2 from respiration; the huge annual fluxes make this a major autotroph–heterotroph interaction.
- **C4.2.22** All elements are recycled: Every element organisms use, not just carbon, is recycled in ecosystems, with decomposers playing a central role; details of the nitrogen and other nutrient cycles are not required.

**Named examples:** cave ecosystems; deep-ocean ecosystems below light penetration; local food web; iron-oxidising bacteria; energy pyramids from specific ecosystems; peat, coal, oil and natural gas; Keeling Curve

**Skills:** construct food chains and food webs with arrows showing energy flow; construct energy pyramids from ecosystem research data; use units of g m⁻² yr⁻¹ for production; draw carbon cycle diagrams; analyse annual fluctuation and long-term trend in the Keeling Curve

## Theme D — Continuity and change

<a id="d1-1-dna-replication"></a>
### D1.1 DNA replication

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: The replication fork works over time; PCR and gels are step processes

**Big questions:** How does a cell make a faithful copy of its entire genome? / How have scientists turned the copying of DNA into tools for medicine and forensics?

- **D1.1.1** Replication makes identical DNA copies: Replication produces DNA molecules with the same base sequence as the original. Know why this matters: it is needed for reproduction, and for growth and replacing tissues in multicellular organisms.
- **D1.1.2** Semi-conservative copying and base pairing: Each new double helix keeps one old strand and gains one new strand, built by complementary base pairing. Explain how these two features make copying highly accurate.
- **D1.1.3** Helicase and DNA polymerase roles: Helicase unwinds the helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands; DNA polymerase builds the new strands. Only this general level of detail is needed.
- **D1.1.4** PCR and gel electrophoresis techniques: PCR copies DNA many times using primers, cycles of temperature change and heat-stable Taq polymerase. Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments; explain what the separation is based on.
- **D1.1.5** Uses of PCR and gels: Be aware of the wide range of uses of these techniques, especially DNA profiling for paternity cases and forensic work. Using more markers lowers the chance of a false match.
- **D1.1.6** `AHL` DNA polymerase works in one direction: Distinguish the 5' and 3' ends of a nucleotide strand. DNA polymerases only extend a strand by attaching the 5' end of an incoming nucleotide to the 3' end of the growing strand.
- **D1.1.7** `AHL` Leading versus lagging strand synthesis: The leading strand is made continuously from a single RNA primer; the lagging strand is made discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, each needing its own primer. Use the terms continuous, discontinuous and Okazaki fragments.
- **D1.1.8** `AHL` Primase, polymerases I and III, ligase: State the job of DNA primase, DNA polymerase III, DNA polymerase I and DNA ligase during replication, using the prokaryotic system only.
- **D1.1.9** `AHL` Proofreading by DNA polymerase III: DNA polymerase III checks its work: if the nucleotide just added at the 3' end is mismatched, it is removed and replaced with the correct one. No other repair mechanisms are required.

**Named examples:** Helicase; DNA polymerase; Taq polymerase; DNA profiling (paternity and forensics); Okazaki fragments; DNA primase; DNA polymerase I; DNA polymerase III; DNA ligase; RNA primer

**Skills:** Interpret gel electrophoresis results from DNA profiles, e.g. matching paternity or forensic samples

<a id="d1-2-protein-synthesis"></a>
### D1.2 Protein synthesis

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 3 · visualisation **high**: Transcription and translation are processes over time

**Big questions:** How is a message written in DNA bases turned into a working chain of amino acids? / What keeps protein production accurate, and what happens when it goes wrong?

- **D1.2.1** Transcription builds RNA from DNA: Transcription makes an RNA copy of a gene using one DNA strand as a template. Know the roles of RNA polymerase in the process.
- **D1.2.2** Base pairing during transcription: Explain how hydrogen bonds and complementary base pairing set the RNA sequence, including that adenine on the DNA template pairs with uracil in RNA.
- **D1.2.3** DNA templates stay unchanged: A DNA strand can be transcribed repeatedly without its base sequence being altered. In non-dividing body cells this sequence must stay intact for the cell's whole life.
- **D1.2.4** Transcription switches genes on or off: Only some genes are expressed at any one time. As the first stage of gene expression, transcription is a key control point for turning genes on and off.
- **D1.2.5** Translation builds polypeptides from mRNA: Translation uses the base sequence of an mRNA molecule to assemble a polypeptide with a matching sequence of amino acids.
- **D1.2.6** mRNA, ribosomes and tRNA in translation: Describe the roles of the three components. mRNA attaches to the ribosome's small subunit, and the large subunit can hold two tRNAs at the same time.
- **D1.2.7** Codon–anticodon pairing: tRNA anticodons pair with complementary mRNA codons to bring the correct amino acid. Use the terms codon and anticodon.
- **D1.2.8** Key features of the genetic code: Explain why codons must be three bases long, and use the terms degeneracy (several codons per amino acid) and universality (the same code in almost all organisms).
- **D1.2.9** Reading an mRNA codon table: Use a table of mRNA codons to work out the amino acid sequence encoded by a given mRNA strand.
- **D1.2.10** Ribosome movement and peptide bond formation: During elongation the ribosome moves along mRNA one codon at a time, joining each new amino acid to the growing chain by a peptide bond. Initiation and termination are not the focus here.
- **D1.2.11** Mutations that alter protein structure: Know that a change in base sequence can change a protein's structure, and be able to describe one example of a point mutation with this effect (e.g. sickle cell anaemia).
- **D1.2.12** `AHL` 5' to 3' direction of synthesis: Explain what it means that both transcription and translation proceed in the 5' to 3' direction.
- **D1.2.13** `AHL` Transcription starts at the promoter: Transcription begins at a promoter, where proteins such as transcription factors bind. Specific transcription factors do not need to be named.
- **D1.2.14** `AHL` DNA that does not code for polypeptides: Much DNA does not code for polypeptides. Examples needed: sequences that regulate gene expression, introns, telomeres, and genes for rRNA and tRNA in eukaryotes.
- **D1.2.15** `AHL` Processing eukaryotic mRNA after transcription: In eukaryotes, introns are cut out and exons joined to make mature mRNA, and a 5' cap and 3' poly-A tail are added to stabilise it.
- **D1.2.16** `AHL` Alternative splicing gives protein variants: Joining different combinations of exons lets one gene code for more than one polypeptide. Only the principle is needed, with no specific examples.
- **D1.2.17** `AHL` How translation begins: Describe the small subunit binding at the mRNA's 5' end and moving to the start codon, initiator tRNA and a second tRNA arriving, then the large subunit joining. Know the roles of the A, P and E sites during elongation.
- **D1.2.18** `AHL` Modifying polypeptides into working proteins: Many polypeptides need modification before they work. The required example is the two-step conversion of pre-proinsulin into insulin.
- **D1.2.19** `AHL` Proteasomes recycle amino acids: A healthy proteome depends on continuous protein breakdown by proteasomes alongside new synthesis, so amino acids are recycled.

**Named examples:** RNA polymerase; Point mutation affecting protein structure (e.g. sickle cell anaemia); Transcription factors; Telomeres; Introns and exons; 5' cap and 3' poly-A tail; A, P and E sites of the ribosome; Pre-proinsulin to insulin; Proteasomes

**Skills:** Use an mRNA codon table to deduce the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide

<a id="d1-3-mutation-and-gene-editing"></a>
### D1.3 Mutation and gene editing

**Molecules** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 2 · visualisation **medium**: A sequence editor shows each mutation's effect; CRISPR animates well

**Big questions:** What causes the base sequence of a gene to change, and is the change ever directed? / Why can a single change in DNA be harmless, harmful or the raw material of evolution?

- **D1.3.1** Gene mutations as molecular changes: A gene mutation is a change to the base sequence of a gene. Distinguish substitutions, insertions and deletions.
- **D1.3.2** Effects of base substitutions: Substitutions create single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Because the code is degenerate, a substitution may or may not change one amino acid in the polypeptide.
- **D1.3.3** Effects of insertions and deletions: Insertions and deletions often stop a polypeptide working, either by shifting the reading frame or by adding or removing large sections. No specific examples are required.
- **D1.3.4** What causes gene mutations: Mutations arise from mutagens and from mistakes in DNA replication or repair. Give examples of chemical mutagens and of mutagenic radiation.
- **D1.3.5** Mutations happen at random: Mutations can occur anywhere in a genome, though some bases mutate more readily. No natural mechanism is known that deliberately changes a particular base to alter a trait.
- **D1.3.6** Germ-line versus somatic mutations: Mutations in germ cells can be passed to offspring, while mutations in somatic cells are not inherited but can lead to cancer.
- **D1.3.7** Mutation as the origin of variation: Mutation is the ultimate source of all genetic variation. Most mutations are neutral or harmful to an individual, yet over time they are essential for evolution by natural selection.
- **D1.3.8** `AHL` Knocking out genes to study function: Gene knockout disables a gene so its function can be inferred from the effect. Technical details are not required; know that libraries of knockout organisms exist for some model research species.
- **D1.3.9** `AHL` Gene editing with CRISPR-Cas9: Explain how CRISPR sequences and the Cas9 enzyme are used to edit genes, and know one example of a successful application. Its natural role in prokaryotes is not required; consider ethical and regulatory issues.
- **D1.3.10** `AHL` Why some gene sequences are conserved: Some sequences stay the same or similar across species or over long evolutionary time. Consider two hypotheses: functional constraints on the gene product, and slower mutation rates.

**Named examples:** Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); Chemical mutagens; Mutagenic radiation; Cancer from somatic mutation; Knockout model organism libraries; CRISPR-Cas9 (one successful application); Commercial genetic tests

<a id="d2-1-cell-and-nuclear-division"></a>
### D2.1 Cell and nuclear division

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 1 · visualisation **high**: Mitosis and meiosis are processes over time

**Big questions:** How does one cell become many identical cells for growth and repair? / How does nuclear division produce gametes that are all genetically different?

- **D2.1.1** Cell division produces new cells: New cells in all organisms come from a parent (mother) cell dividing to form two daughter cells.
- **D2.1.2** Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm: Compare how cytoplasm is split: in animal cells a contracting ring of actin and myosin pinches the membrane inwards; in plant cells vesicles build new membrane and cell wall between the daughter cells.
- **D2.1.3** Equal and unequal cytoplasm division: Cytokinesis is usually but not always equal, and each daughter cell needs at least one mitochondrion and other self-replicating organelles. Use human oogenesis and yeast budding as unequal examples.
- **D2.1.4** Why eukaryotes need mitosis and meiosis: The nucleus must divide before the cell does, so no cell lacks a nucleus. Mitosis keeps chromosome number and genome unchanged; meiosis halves chromosome number and creates genetic diversity.
- **D2.1.5** DNA replicates before division: Both mitosis and meiosis follow DNA replication, after which each chromosome is two chromatids held together until anaphase.
- **D2.1.6** Chromosome condensation and movement: In both divisions chromosomes condense by supercoiling around histones and are moved by microtubules and microtubule motor proteins.
- **D2.1.7** The stages of mitosis: Name the phases of mitosis and explain how the whole process gives two genetically identical daughter nuclei.
- **D2.1.8** Recognising mitosis phases: Identify each phase of mitosis from diagrams and from cells seen under a microscope or in micrographs.
- **D2.1.9** Meiosis halves chromosome number: Use the terms diploid and haploid, outline the two rounds of segregation, and explain how one diploid nucleus gives four haploid nuclei. Explain why meiosis is needed in a sexual life cycle.
- **D2.1.10** Non-disjunction and Down syndrome: Use Down syndrome as the example of a meiosis error, where chromosomes fail to separate correctly (non-disjunction).
- **D2.1.11** How meiosis creates variation: Explain how random orientation of bivalents and crossing over produce genetically diverse gametes.
- **D2.1.12** `AHL` Proliferation for growth, replacement and repair: Cells proliferate for growth, routine replacement and healing. Examples: plant meristems and early animal embryos for growth; skin for routine replacement and wound healing (skin structure not needed).
- **D2.1.13** `AHL` Stages of the cell cycle: Proliferation uses the cell cycle: interphase (G1, S, G2) followed by mitosis and then cytokinesis. Know the order of events.
- **D2.1.14** `AHL` Growth during interphase: Interphase is a busy metabolic period in which the cell makes proteins, DNA and other components. Mitochondria and chloroplasts grow and divide to increase in number.
- **D2.1.15** `AHL` Cyclins control the cell cycle: Different cyclins rise and fall during the cycle, and a specific cyclin must reach a threshold for the cell to pass each checkpoint. The roles of individual cyclins are not needed.
- **D2.1.16** `AHL` Mutations in cell-cycle control genes: Mutations that turn proto-oncogenes into oncogenes, or that disable tumour suppressor genes, can cause uncontrolled cell division.
- **D2.1.17** `AHL` Comparing benign and malignant tumours: Tumours differ in division rate, growth and ability to invade and spread. Use benign, malignant, primary and secondary tumour, distinguish cancerous from non-cancerous tumours, and determine mitotic index from cell populations.

**Named examples:** Actin–myosin contractile ring; Human oogenesis; Budding in yeast; Histones; Down syndrome (non-disjunction); Plant meristems; Early-stage animal embryos; Skin (replacement and wound healing); Cyclins; Proto-oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes

**Skills:** Identify phases of mitosis in diagrams, microscope slides and micrographs; Determine the mitotic index of a cell population from observations

<a id="d2-2-gene-expression"></a>
### D2.2 Gene expression

**Cells** · AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 0, AHL 3 · visualisation **medium**: Chromatin and methylation changes are clearer as toggles

**Big questions:** How can cells with identical DNA end up doing completely different jobs? / Can the environment leave marks on genes that are passed to the next generation?

- **D2.2.1** `AHL` Gene expression links genes to phenotype: Gene expression is how information in a gene affects phenotype, most commonly through transcription, translation and the action of the protein made, such as an enzyme.
- **D2.2.2** `AHL` DNA-binding proteins regulate transcription: Transcription is controlled by proteins that bind particular DNA sequences. Include the roles of promoters, enhancers and transcription factors.
- **D2.2.3** `AHL` mRNA breakdown controls translation: How long mRNA survives before nucleases destroy it regulates how much protein is made; in human cells this ranges from minutes to days.
- **D2.2.4** `AHL` Epigenesis and cell differentiation: Epigenesis is the development of differentiation patterns in a multicellular organism's cells. Epigenetic changes alter phenotype but not genotype, because the base sequence is unchanged.
- **D2.2.5** `AHL` Genome, transcriptome and proteome compared: Distinguish a cell's full set of genes, the genes it is transcribing, and the proteins it contains. No cell expresses every gene, and its expression pattern determines how it differentiates.
- **D2.2.6** `AHL` DNA and histone methylation as tags: Methylated cytosine in a promoter represses transcription of the gene downstream. Methylated amino acids on histones can either repress or activate transcription; the mechanism is not required.
- **D2.2.7** `AHL` Inheriting gene expression patterns: Phenotypic changes can pass to daughter cells or offspring without DNA sequence changes if epigenetic tags such as methylation or histone modification survive mitosis or meiosis.
- **D2.2.8** `AHL` Environment changes gene expression: The environment can alter gene expression in cells and organisms. The required example is changes to DNA methyl tags caused by air pollution.
- **D2.2.9** `AHL` Incomplete erasure of tags in gametes: Most, but not all, epigenetic tags are removed in eggs and sperm. Illustrate the consequences by explaining the epigenetic basis of differences between tigons and ligers.
- **D2.2.10** `AHL` Studies of identical twins: Studies of monozygotic twins are used to investigate how the environment affects gene expression; limit to this purpose.
- **D2.2.11** `AHL` External signals alter expression patterns: Know one hormone that changes gene expression and one biochemical example in bacteria, such as lactose or tryptophan regulating their genes.

**Named examples:** Promoters, enhancers and transcription factors; DNA methylation of cytosine; Histone methylation; Air pollution altering methyl tags; Tigons and ligers; Monozygotic twin studies; One hormone affecting gene expression; Lactose or tryptophan regulation in bacteria

<a id="d2-3-water-potential"></a>
### D2.3 Water potential

**Cells** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Water potential is a relationship between solute and pressure potential

**Big questions:** What decides whether water moves into a cell or out of it? / Why can a plant cell survive in pure water when a red blood cell bursts?

- **D2.3.1** Water as a solvent: Explain solvation: solutes form hydrogen bonds with water, and both positive and negative ions are attracted to polar water molecules.
- **D2.3.2** Water moves toward higher solute concentration: Explain water movement by comparing solute concentrations (never 'water concentration'), using the terms hypertonic, hypotonic and isotonic.
- **D2.3.3** Osmosis into and out of cells: Predict the net direction of water movement for a cell in a hypotonic or hypertonic environment. In an isotonic environment water still moves, but in dynamic equilibrium.
- **D2.3.4** Plant tissue in different solutions: Measure changes in length and mass of plant tissue in a range of solutions and deduce the isotonic concentration. Use standard deviation and standard error (formulae not memorised), shown as error bars.
- **D2.3.5** Cells without walls and osmosis: Cells without walls swell and burst in hypotonic media and shrink and crenate in hypertonic media. Freshwater protists use contractile vacuoles; multicellular animals keep tissue fluid isotonic.
- **D2.3.6** Walled cells: turgor and plasmolysis: Cells with walls become turgid in hypotonic media as pressure builds against the wall, and plasmolyse in hypertonic media.
- **D2.3.7** Isotonic solutions in medicine: Explain why isotonic solutions are used, with intravenous fluids and bathing donor organs before transplantation as examples.
- **D2.3.8** `AHL` Defining water potential: Water potential is the potential energy of water per unit volume. Absolute values cannot be measured, so values are given relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure and 20°C, usually in kPa.
- **D2.3.9** `AHL` Water flows down water potential gradients: Water moves from higher to lower water potential; explain this in terms of potential energy.
- **D2.3.10** `AHL` Solute and pressure potential combined: Use ψw = ψs + ψp for walled cells. Solute potential is zero or negative; pressure potential is usually positive in cells but negative in xylem under tension.
- **D2.3.11** `AHL` Water potential in bathed plant tissue: Use solute and pressure potentials to explain the changes seen when plant tissue is placed in hypotonic or hypertonic solutions.

**Named examples:** Contractile vacuoles in freshwater unicellular organisms; Red blood cell crenation and bursting; Intravenous fluids; Organs stored for transplantation; Xylem sap under tension

**Skills:** Measure length and mass changes in plant tissue and deduce the isotonic solute concentration; Calculate and use standard deviation and standard error, and plot error bars; Calculate water potential using ψw = ψs + ψp (AHL)

<a id="d3-1-reproduction"></a>
### D3.1 Reproduction

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 3 · visualisation **medium**: The menstrual-cycle hormone timeline and anatomy suit 2D interactives

**Big questions:** When is copying yourself a better strategy than mixing genes with a partner? / How do hormones coordinate the body changes needed to produce and nurture offspring?

- **D3.1.1** Sexual versus asexual reproduction: Compare the two: asexual reproduction gives identical offspring suited to an unchanging environment; sexual reproduction gives new gene combinations, providing variation for adapting to change.
- **D3.1.2** Meiosis and fertilization in life cycles: Meiosis breaks up the parents' allele combinations and fertilization (fusion of gametes) creates new ones.
- **D3.1.3** How male and female sexes differ: The defining difference between the sexes is that male gametes travel to female gametes, so they are small with few food reserves; link this to the numbers of gametes made and to reproductive strategies.
- **D3.1.4** Human reproductive system anatomy: Draw diagrams of the male-typical and female-typical reproductive systems, labelling structures and annotating their functions.
- **D3.1.5** Hormones of the menstrual cycle: Describe changes in the ovarian and uterine cycles and how oestradiol, progesterone, LH and FSH regulate them through both positive and negative feedback.
- **D3.1.6** Events of human fertilization: Sperm and egg membranes fuse; the sperm nucleus enters but its tail and mitochondria are destroyed. Both nuclear membranes break down and all chromosomes join a single mitosis giving two diploid nuclei.
- **D3.1.7** Hormones used in IVF: In IVF the body's normal hormone secretion is first suppressed, then artificial hormone doses cause superovulation.
- **D3.1.8** Sexual reproduction in flowering plants: Outline gamete production in ovules and pollen, pollination, pollen development and fertilization to form an embryo. Plant reproduction is sexual even in hermaphrodite species.
- **D3.1.9** Structure of an insect-pollinated flower: Draw an insect-pollinated flower, labelling its structures and annotating their functions.
- **D3.1.10** Ways plants promote cross-pollination: Include pollen and stigma maturing at different times, separate male and female flowers or plants, and transfer of pollen between plants by animals or wind.
- **D3.1.11** Self-incompatibility increases variation: Self-pollination causes inbreeding, lowering genetic diversity and vigour. Many species have genetic mechanisms ensuring fertilization only happens between gametes from different plants.
- **D3.1.12** Seed dispersal and germination: Distinguish seed dispersal from pollination, and describe germination as the embryo growing and developing while its stored food is mobilised.
- **D3.1.13** `AHL` Hormonal control of puberty: Rising GnRH release from the hypothalamus in childhood triggers increased LH and FSH secretion, which raises sex hormone production and causes the changes of puberty. Limit to this sequence.
- **D3.1.14** `AHL` Producing sperm and eggs: Compare spermatogenesis and oogenesis through mitosis, growth, two meiotic divisions and differentiation. Explain why they yield very different numbers of gametes with different amounts of cytoplasm.
- **D3.1.15** `AHL` Preventing more than one sperm entering: The acrosome reaction lets a sperm get through the zona pellucida; the cortical reaction then blocks other sperm.
- **D3.1.16** `AHL` Blastocyst formation and implantation: Describe how the embryo develops into a blastocyst and implants in the endometrium. Other embryo stages need not be named.
- **D3.1.17** `AHL` Pregnancy tests detect hCG: The embryo and early placenta secrete hCG; pregnancy tests use monoclonal antibodies that bind to it.
- **D3.1.18** `AHL` The placenta supports the foetus: Know which substances cross the placenta and why the large surface area of its villi matters; the placenta allows the foetus to develop much further inside the uterus than in non-placental mammals.
- **D3.1.19** `AHL` Hormones in pregnancy and birth: Progesterone, first from the corpus luteum then the placenta, maintains pregnancy. At birth progesterone falls, allowing oxytocin secretion to rise through positive feedback.
- **D3.1.20** `AHL` HRT and heart disease risk: Use HRT as a case study of correlation versus causation: early studies linked HRT to less heart disease, but trials showed slightly higher risk; higher socioeconomic status was the real cause.

**Named examples:** Oestradiol, progesterone, LH and FSH; In vitro fertilization (IVF); Insect-pollinated flower; GnRH; Acrosome and cortical reactions; Zona pellucida; Blastocyst; Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and monoclonal antibodies; Placental villi; Corpus luteum; Oxytocin; Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and coronary heart disease

**Skills:** Draw and annotate diagrams of the human male-typical and female-typical reproductive systems; Draw and annotate a diagram of an insect-pollinated flower; Distinguish correlation from causation using the HRT epidemiology case (AHL)

<a id="d3-2-inheritance"></a>
### D3.2 Inheritance

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 5, AHL 3 · visualisation **medium**: Punnett, pedigree and chi-squared tools suit 2D interactives

**Big questions:** Why do offspring resemble their parents, yet never exactly? / How do the movements of chromosomes explain the ratios we see in genetic crosses?

- **D3.2.1** Gametes and zygotes in inheritance: Haploid gametes from each parent fuse to form a diploid zygote, a pattern shared by all sexually reproducing eukaryotes. A diploid cell carries two copies of each autosomal gene.
- **D3.2.2** Carrying out crosses in plants: Use P, F1, F2 and Punnett grid. Pollination is needed to cross plants; species such as peas can self-fertilise. Crosses are used to breed new crop and ornamental varieties.
- **D3.2.3** Genotype: the alleles an organism carries: Genotype is the pair of alleles inherited. Use homozygous and heterozygous correctly and distinguish genes from alleles.
- **D3.2.4** Phenotype from genes and environment: Phenotype is the set of observable traits. Suggest human traits caused only by genotype, only by environment, and by their interaction.
- **D3.2.5** Dominant and recessive alleles: Explain why a homozygous dominant and a heterozygous individual show the same phenotype.
- **D3.2.6** Phenotypic plasticity: Organisms can develop traits suited to their environment by changing gene expression, without changing genotype; such changes may be reversible during life.
- **D3.2.7** Phenylketonuria: a recessive disease: PKU is an autosomal recessive condition caused by a mutated gene for the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine.
- **D3.2.8** SNPs and multiple alleles: A species' gene pool can contain many alleles of one gene (often differing by single-nucleotide polymorphisms), but a diploid individual carries just two.
- **D3.2.9** ABO blood groups: ABO blood groups illustrate multiple alleles; write the alleles as IA, IB and i.
- **D3.2.10** Codominance and incomplete dominance: Codominant heterozygotes show both phenotypes (AB blood group, IAIB); incompletely dominant heterozygotes are intermediate (four o'clock flower, Mirabilis jalapa). Compare at the phenotype level.
- **D3.2.11** Sex chromosomes and sex-linked genes: The sex chromosome in the sperm decides whether the zygote develops male-typical or female-typical features. The X chromosome carries far more genes than the Y.
- **D3.2.12** Haemophilia as sex-linked disorder: Use haemophilia as the example of sex-linked inheritance, writing alleles as superscripts on a capital X.
- **D3.2.13** Using pedigree charts: Deduce inheritance patterns and individual genotypes from pedigree charts. Explain the genetic reasons many societies ban marriage between close relatives, and distinguish inductive from deductive reasoning.
- **D3.2.14** Continuous variation and polygenic traits: Continuous variation results from many genes and/or the environment, with human skin colour as the example. Distinguish continuous from discrete variables (e.g. ABO group) and calculate mean, median and mode.
- **D3.2.15** Box-and-whisker plots: Draw box-and-whisker plots for continuous data such as height, showing minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum and outliers. Outliers lie more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the quartiles.
- **D3.2.16** `AHL` Segregation and independent assortment: Link how chromosomes move in meiosis to the results of dihybrid crosses with two unlinked genes.
- **D3.2.17** `AHL` Dihybrid Punnett grids: Use Punnett grids to predict genotype and phenotype ratios for two unlinked autosomal genes, explaining where 9:3:3:1 and 1:1:1:1 come from and when Mendel's second law fails.
- **D3.2.18** `AHL` Gene loci and their products: Search databases for human genes and their polypeptides, finding gene pairs on different chromosomes and pairs close together on the same chromosome.
- **D3.2.19** `AHL` Linkage of autosomal genes: Explain why alleles of linked genes may not assort independently. Show linked alleles beside vertical lines representing homologous chromosomes.
- **D3.2.20** `AHL` Identifying recombinants: Work out the outcome of a test cross (double heterozygote × double recessive) for linked or unlinked genes, and identify recombinants among gametes, offspring genotypes and offspring phenotypes.
- **D3.2.21** `AHL` Chi-squared test for dihybrid data: Apply a chi-squared test to dihybrid cross results, using null and alternative hypotheses, observed versus expected values, and significance at p = 0.05. The F2 generation acts as a sample of the population.

**Named examples:** Pea plants (self-pollination); Phenylketonuria (PKU); ABO blood groups; AB blood group (codominance); Four o'clock flower, Mirabilis jalapa (incomplete dominance); Haemophilia; Human skin colour (polygenic); Student height (box-and-whisker data); Gene databases for human gene loci

**Skills:** Construct Punnett grids for monohybrid and (AHL) dihybrid crosses; Analyse pedigree charts to deduce inheritance patterns and genotypes; Calculate mean, median and mode; Draw box-and-whisker plots and identify outliers using 1.5 × IQR; Search gene databases for loci of human genes (AHL); Perform a chi-squared test on dihybrid cross data (AHL)

<a id="d3-3-homeostasis"></a>
### D3.3 Homeostasis

**Organisms** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: The nephron is 3D (AHL); feedback loops suit simulations

**Big questions:** How does the body keep its internal conditions steady when the outside world keeps changing? / What goes wrong, and why does it matter, when a control system fails?

- **D3.3.1** Keeping the internal environment steady: Homeostasis keeps internal variables within set limits despite external change. Human examples to know: body temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and blood osmotic concentration.
- **D3.3.2** Negative feedback in homeostasis: Explain why homeostasis relies on negative rather than positive feedback, and how negative feedback returns a variable to its set point whether it has risen above or fallen below it.
- **D3.3.3** Hormonal control of blood glucose: Describe how pancreatic endocrine cells control insulin and glucagon release, how the hormones travel in blood, and their effects on target cells.
- **D3.3.4** Type 1 and type 2 diabetes: Explain the physiological changes behind each type of diabetes, plus their risk factors, prevention and treatment.
- **D3.3.5** Thermoregulation as negative feedback: Include peripheral thermoreceptors, the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland and thyroxin, and examples of muscle and adipose tissue acting as effectors.
- **D3.3.6** Human temperature control mechanisms: Birds and mammals use physiological and behavioural means, but details are for humans only: vasodilation, vasoconstriction, shivering, sweating, uncoupled respiration in brown fat and hair erection.
- **D3.3.7** `AHL` Kidney roles: osmoregulation and excretion: Distinguish excretion from osmoregulation (control of osmotic concentration, measured in osmol L⁻¹), and describe the kidney's role in both.
- **D3.3.8** `AHL` Glomerulus, Bowman's capsule and PCT: Ultrafiltration in the glomerulus forces small solutes out of the blood into the Bowman's capsule; the proximal convoluted tubule then reclaims useful substances, leaving wastes in the filtrate to form urine.
- **D3.3.9** `AHL` Function of the loop of Henle: Active transport of sodium ions out of the ascending limb keeps the medulla's osmotic concentration high, which helps water be reabsorbed from the collecting ducts. Limit to this.
- **D3.3.10** `AHL` ADH and water reabsorption: Hypothalamic osmoreceptors adjust ADH release from the pituitary; ADH moves aquaporins between intracellular vesicles and the membranes of collecting-duct cells, changing water reabsorption.
- **D3.3.11** `AHL` Blood flow shifts with activity: Compare blood supply to skeletal muscle, gut, brain and kidneys during sleep, wakeful rest and vigorous exercise.

**Named examples:** Insulin and glucagon; Pancreatic endocrine cells; Type 1 and type 2 diabetes; Hypothalamus and pituitary gland; Thyroxin; Brown adipose tissue; Nephron: glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, collecting duct; Antidiuretic hormone (ADH); Aquaporins

**Skills:** Interpret data on blood flow to organs during sleep, rest and exercise (AHL)

<a id="d4-1-natural-selection"></a>
### D4.1 Natural selection

**Ecosystems** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 2, AHL 2 · visualisation **high**: Natural selection and Hardy–Weinberg are best learned by simulation

**Big questions:** Why do some variants leave more offspring than others, and how does that change a species? / How can we measure evolution happening in a population's genes?

- **D4.1.1** Natural selection drives evolution: Natural selection acts continuously and, over billions of years, has produced Earth's biodiversity. Darwin's mechanism replacing Lamarckism illustrates a paradigm shift; know the term.
- **D4.1.2** Sources of variation for selection: Mutation creates new alleles and sexual reproduction creates new combinations of alleles, providing the variation natural selection acts on.
- **D4.1.3** Overproduction and competition: Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support, so individuals compete; give examples of resources, such as food, that set the carrying capacity.
- **D4.1.4** Abiotic selection pressures: Non-living, density-independent factors such as extreme temperatures can act as selection pressures affecting survival; give examples.
- **D4.1.5** Variation in survival and reproduction: Selection depends on individuals differing in how well adapted they are and how many offspring they leave. Study selection through intraspecific competition, using the concept of fitness.
- **D4.1.6** Only heritable traits evolve: Evolution requires heritable traits. Characteristics acquired during life from the environment are not encoded in genes, so they are not inherited.
- **D4.1.7** Sexual selection in animals: Physical and behavioural traits that signal fitness affect mating success and so drive evolution. Use examples such as the plumage of birds of paradise.
- **D4.1.8** Experiments controlling selection pressures: Interpret data from John Endler's guppy experiments, which modelled natural and sexual selection by manipulating predation pressures.
- **D4.1.9** `AHL` What a gene pool is: A gene pool is all the genes, and all their alleles, present in a population.
- **D4.1.10** `AHL` Allele frequencies in isolated populations: Use databases to compare allele frequencies in geographically separated populations, including at least one human example.
- **D4.1.11** `AHL` Selection changes allele frequencies: Natural selection between individuals with different heritable traits shifts allele frequencies in the gene pool. Combining genetics with Darwin's theory is known as neo-Darwinism.
- **D4.1.12** `AHL` Directional, disruptive and stabilizing selection: Distinguish the three patterns of selection and recognise that each one changes allele frequencies.
- **D4.1.13** `AHL` Hardy–Weinberg equation: Using p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1, calculate expected genotype frequencies from allele frequencies, or allele frequencies from one known genotype frequency.
- **D4.1.14** `AHL` Conditions for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: Know the conditions for genetic equilibrium. If observed genotype frequencies do not fit the equation, a condition is being broken, such as non-random mating or genotype-dependent survival.
- **D4.1.15** `AHL` Artificial selection by humans: Breeders choose individuals with desirable traits in crops and domestic animals. Unintended outcomes such as antibiotic resistance in bacteria result from natural, not artificial, selection.

**Named examples:** Darwin versus Lamarckism (paradigm shift); Birds of paradise plumage; John Endler's guppy experiments; Human allele frequency database example; Neo-Darwinism; Crop plants and domesticated animals; Antibiotic resistance in bacteria

**Skills:** Interpret data from Endler's guppy experiments; Search databases for allele frequencies in isolated populations (AHL); Calculate allele and genotype frequencies using the Hardy–Weinberg equation (AHL)

<a id="d4-2-stability-and-change"></a>
### D4.2 Stability and change

**Ecosystems** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 4, AHL 2 · visualisation **medium**: Succession, eutrophication and biomagnification suit timelines and charts

**Big questions:** What lets some ecosystems persist almost unchanged for millions of years? / How are human activities pushing ecosystems toward collapse, and can they be restored?

- **D4.2.1** Natural ecosystems can be stable: Cite evidence that some ecosystems, such as particular forests or deserts, have stayed stable for very long periods—in some cases millions of years.
- **D4.2.2** What ecosystems need to stay stable: Stability needs an energy supply, nutrient recycling, genetic diversity and climate variables that stay within tolerance limits.
- **D4.2.3** Amazon deforestation as tipping point: A large rainforest area is needed for transpiration to produce water vapour, cooling, air flows and rainfall; the minimum area is uncertain. Calculate percentage change in forest area.
- **D4.2.4** Mesocosms as ecosystem models: Set up mesocosms to test variables affecting stability. Sealed glass vessels block matter exchange but allow energy transfer; aquatic or microbial systems work better than terrestrial ones. Care for them following IB experimental guidelines.
- **D4.2.5** Keystone species and stability: Keystone species have an effect on community structure out of proportion to their abundance, and removing them risks ecosystem collapse.
- **D4.2.6** Sustainable harvesting of wild resources: Harvesting is sustainable only if slower than replacement. Use one terrestrial plant species and one marine fish species to show how sustainability is assessed.
- **D4.2.7** What makes agriculture sustainable: Consider soil erosion, nutrient leaching, supply of fertilisers and other inputs, agrochemical pollution and carbon footprint.
- **D4.2.8** Eutrophication from fertiliser leaching: Explain how nitrate and phosphate leaching from fertilisers causes eutrophication in freshwater and marine ecosystems, including the rise in biochemical oxygen demand (BOD).
- **D4.2.9** Biomagnification of toxins: Explain how toxins build up to higher concentrations in consumers at higher trophic levels, using DDT and mercury as examples.
- **D4.2.10** Plastic pollution in the oceans: Plastics persist because they are not biodegradable. Give examples of how micro- and macroplastics harm marine life, and note how media coverage shifted public action.
- **D4.2.11** Rewilding to restore ecosystems: Rewilding methods include reintroducing apex predators and other keystone species, reconnecting habitats over large areas and reducing human impact. Use Hinewai Reserve, New Zealand, as the example.
- **D4.2.12** `AHL` Ecological succession and its triggers: Succession can be started by changes in abiotic conditions and in biotic factors.
- **D4.2.13** `AHL` Trends during primary succession: Using a suitable land example, describe increases in plant size, primary production, species diversity, food-web complexity and nutrient cycling.
- **D4.2.14** `AHL` Cyclical succession: In some ecosystems communities follow a repeating cycle instead of reaching one fixed climax. Refer to a specific example.
- **D4.2.15** `AHL` Climax communities and arrested succession: Given conditions tend to produce a particular climax community, but human activity can stop it forming. Use livestock grazing and wetland drainage as examples.

**Named examples:** Amazon rainforest deforestation; Mesocosms; Keystone species; One terrestrial plant and one marine fish species harvested; Nitrogen and phosphate fertilisers (eutrophication, BOD); DDT; Mercury; Microplastics and macroplastics; Hinewai Reserve, New Zealand; Primary succession (terrestrial example); Cyclical succession example; Livestock grazing and wetland drainage (arrested succession)

**Skills:** Calculate percentage change, e.g. in area of forest lost to deforestation; Design and maintain a mesocosm to investigate a variable affecting stability

<a id="d4-3-climate-change"></a>
### D4.3 Climate change

**Ecosystems** · SL+AHL · teaching hours: SL/HL 3, AHL 1 · visualisation **low**: Feedback loops and phenology mismatch suit simple 2D interactives

**Big questions:** How do human emissions set off warming that can then feed itself? / What happens to species and ecosystems when the timing and places they depend on shift?

- **D4.3.1** Human causes of climate change: Limit to human-caused rises in atmospheric carbon dioxide and methane concentrations.
- **D4.3.2** Positive feedback in global warming: Include CO₂ release from the deep ocean, reduced reflection as snow and ice melt, faster decomposition of peat and permafrost organic matter, methane from thawing permafrost, and more droughts and fires.
- **D4.3.3** Boreal forests flipping to carbon sources: Warmer conditions and less snow bring drought, lower production, browning and more frequent, intense fires in the taiga, burning legacy carbon and turning a carbon sink into a source.
- **D4.3.4** Melting polar ice destroys habitats: Use emperor penguins (Aptenodytes forsteri), at risk as Antarctic landfast ice breaks out early, and Arctic walruses losing sea ice habitat as examples.
- **D4.3.5** Ocean currents and nutrient upwelling: Warming of surface waters can prevent upwelling of nutrient-rich deep water, lowering primary production in the ocean and the energy available to marine food chains.
- **D4.3.6** Species ranges shift poleward and upslope: Use evidence from tropical montane birds moving upslope in New Guinea and North American tree species contracting and spreading northward.
- **D4.3.7** Coral reefs at risk of collapse: Higher CO₂ acidifies oceans and suppresses coral calcification; higher temperatures cause bleaching. Losing corals can collapse the whole reef ecosystem.
- **D4.3.8** Sequestering carbon in forests and peat: Evaluate afforestation, forest regeneration and peatland restoration for storing carbon, including the debate over non-native plantations versus native rewilding, and where peat forms.
- **D4.3.9** `AHL` Phenology: timing of biological events: Phenology is the study of the timing of biological events such as flowering, budburst, bud set, migration and nesting; day length and temperature act as cues that trigger them.
- **D4.3.10** `AHL` Climate change breaks phenological synchrony: If one species uses temperature as its cue and another uses photoperiod, warming can desynchronise them. Examples: Arctic mouse-ear chickweed and migrating reindeer; great tit breeding and caterpillar peaks, or a local example.
- **D4.3.11** `AHL` More insect generations per year: Warming lets some insects complete more life cycles in a year, illustrated by the spruce bark beetle (Ips typographus or Dendroctonus micans).
- **D4.3.12** `AHL` Climate change drives evolution: Reduced snow cover has changed the relative fitness of grey and brown colour variants of the tawny owl (Strix aluco), an example of evolution caused by climate change.

**Named examples:** Carbon dioxide and methane; Permafrost and peat; Boreal forest (taiga); Emperor penguin (Aptenodytes forsteri); Walrus; New Guinea montane birds; North American tree species; Coral reefs (acidification and bleaching); Arctic mouse-ear chickweed (Cerastium arcticum) and reindeer (Rangifer tarandus); Great tit (Parus major) and caterpillars; Spruce bark beetle (Ips typographus or Dendroctonus micans); Tawny owl (Strix aluco)
