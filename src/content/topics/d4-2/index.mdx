  - term: Ecosystem stability

    level: SL
  - term: Keystone species
    definition: "A species that has a disproportionately large effect on the structure of its community relative to its abundance. Removing it can cause the community to change drastically or collapse, e.g. sea otters in kelp forests."

    definition: "The largest harvest that can be taken from a population every year indefinitely. In a logistic model it is reached when the population is held at half its carrying capacity (K/2), where the rate of replacement is greatest."

    definition: "Enrichment of a body of water with nutrients, especially nitrate and phosphate leached from fertilizers, causing algal blooms, death of plants, rising BOD as saprotrophic bacteria decompose the dead matter, and loss of dissolved oxygen."

<Section id="stability" title="Stable ecosystems and what keeps them stable" level="SL" covers="D4.2.1 D4.2.2">

A **stable ecosystem** keeps the same broad structure, the same main species and the same processes over long periods. It may fluctuate from year to year, but it resists change or returns to its former state after a disturbance. Ecosystems have the potential to be **sustainable**: to continue indefinitely.

### Evidence that ecosystems can last for millions of years

- **The Daintree rainforest** in north-east Queensland, Australia, is part of a rainforest that has persisted in the region, at least in refuges, through tens of millions of years of climate change. It contains many plant lineages that are very old, including some of the most ancient families of flowering plants, which survive almost nowhere else.
- **The Namib Desert** in south-west Africa has been arid or semi-arid for tens of millions of years, which is why so many of its organisms show extreme adaptations to drought, such as beetles that collect fog.

Evidence for long-term continuity comes from **fossils**, **pollen preserved in sediments** (which records the plants growing nearby layer by layer) and from **endemic species** whose lineages could only have survived if their habitat did.

### Requirements for stability

| Requirement | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Supply of energy** | Energy flows through ecosystems and is lost as heat, so it must be resupplied continuously, almost always by sunlight. |
| **Recycling of nutrients** | Elements are finite. Decomposers must return them to the soil, water and air as fast as producers use them. |
| **Genetic diversity** | Variation within populations allows adaptation by natural selection when conditions change, and makes it less likely that one disease wipes out a species. |
| **Climatic variables within tolerance** | Temperature and rainfall must stay within the range of tolerance of the species present (see <TopicLink code="B4.1" />). |

High **biodiversity** also helps: if one species declines, others can take over its role in the food web.

<Callout type="exam">

"Explain the requirements for ecosystem stability" needs all four: an energy supply, nutrient recycling, genetic diversity and climate within tolerance limits. Explain each one; do not just list them.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="tipping-point" title="Amazon deforestation: a possible tipping point" level="SL" covers="D4.2.3">

A **tipping point** is a threshold beyond which a small extra change triggers **positive feedback**, so the ecosystem shifts quickly to a different state that may be very hard to reverse.

### The forest makes its own rain

The Amazon rainforest is so large that it shapes its own climate:

1. Trees take up water from the soil and **transpire** huge amounts of water vapour.
2. Evaporation from leaves **cools** the forest, and the vapour rises and condenses into clouds. The resulting air flows help to draw moist air inland from the Atlantic Ocean (exactly how strongly is still debated).
3. The vapour falls as **rain** further inland, where more trees take it up and transpire it again. Much of the rain in the western Amazon has been recycled through the forest several times.

### Deforestation and positive feedback

Clearing forest for cattle ranching, soya farming and logging means **less transpiration**, so less rainfall, longer dry seasons and higher temperatures downwind. Drought makes trees die and lets **fires** spread, which kills more trees and reduces transpiration further. Past a tipping point the remaining forest could not sustain itself and might turn into degraded, savanna-like vegetation.

<Figure name="d4-2-amazon" caption="Left: a large area of rainforest generates its own rainfall. Right: if deforestation goes too far, less transpiration leads to drought and fire, which kill more trees — a positive feedback loop." />

Nobody knows the **minimum area** of forest needed to keep the cycle going. Some scientists have suggested a tipping point at around 20–25% loss of the original forest; the Amazon has already lost about 17%. The uncertainty is a strong argument for caution.

### Calculating percentage change

> percentage change = (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100

Worked example: in the Brazilian Amazon, about 27,800 km² of forest was cleared in 2004 and about 4,600 km² in 2012. Change = (4,600 − 27,800) ÷ 27,800 × 100 = **−83%**, an 83% fall in the annual rate of clearance. In 2021 about 13,000 km² was cleared: (13,000 − 4,600) ÷ 4,600 × 100 = **+183%** compared with 2012.

<Callout type="exam">

Always divide by the **initial** value, keep the sign (negative for a decrease) and state whether you mean a change in the forest *area* or in the *rate* of clearance.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="mesocosms" title="Mesocosms: modelling ecosystem stability" level="SL" covers="D4.2.4">

Whole ecosystems are too big and complex for controlled experiments, so ecologists use **mesocosms**: small, enclosed experimental ecosystems that can be replicated and manipulated. They are **models** — simpler than nature, but useful for testing how one variable affects stability.

- **Open tanks** or fenced enclosures are easy to set up, but matter (gases, water, organisms) can enter and leave.
- **Sealed glass vessels** are better for studying stability, because they are **closed systems**: no matter enters or leaves, so nutrients must be recycled inside, but **light energy** enters through the glass and **heat** leaves.
- **Aquatic** or **microbial** mesocosms work better than terrestrial ones: they are easier to seal, keep moist and sample.

<Figure name="d4-2-mesocosm" caption="A sealed aquatic mesocosm. Producers and decomposers are essential; consumers are optional. The panel shows how an investigation could be designed with replicates." />

### Setting up a mesocosm investigation

1. Fill several identical clear jars with pond water, a layer of pond mud (containing **decomposers**), aquatic plants or algae (**producers**) and, if wanted, a few small invertebrates such as snails (**consumers**). Leave an air space and seal each jar.
2. Choose **one independent variable**, e.g. light intensity, temperature, or nitrate added.
3. Set up **at least three replicates** at each level, plus a control.
4. Keep other variables constant: volumes of water and mud, number of each organism, temperature (avoid direct sunlight, which overheats sealed jars).
5. Measure **dependent variables** over weeks: dissolved oxygen (probe), pH (rises as CO₂ is removed by photosynthesis), turbidity or colour (algal growth), and survival and growth of organisms.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: ethics of mesocosms">

Follow the IB animal experimentation policy. Do not include vertebrates such as fish, which could suffocate or starve in a sealed jar. Use small invertebrates only if they will not suffer, check the jars regularly, end the experiment if animals are distressed, and return organisms to their habitat afterwards.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam" title="A mesocosm diagram must include">

A sealed lid, clear container, air space, water, producers, decomposers (mud or soil) and, optionally, consumers. State that light energy enters and heat leaves, but matter cannot.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="keystone" title="Keystone species" level="SL" covers="D4.2.5">

A **keystone species** has an effect on the structure of its community that is **disproportionate to its abundance**. Like the keystone at the top of an arch, removing it can make the whole structure collapse. Keystone species are often predators that control herbivores (top-down control, see <TopicLink code="C4.1" />), but they can also be ecosystem engineers such as beavers, or species that many others depend on.

### Two classic examples

- **The sea star *Pisaster ochraceus*** on rocky shores of the Pacific coast of North America. In the 1960s Robert Paine removed it from experimental areas of shore. Without the sea star, mussels (*Mytilus californianus*) took over the rock, crowding out most other species of seaweeds and invertebrates, and the number of species fell sharply. Where *Pisaster* remained, the community stayed diverse.
- **Sea otters** in kelp forests along the same coast. Otters eat sea urchins. Where otters were hunted almost to extinction for their fur, urchin numbers exploded and the urchins grazed the kelp down to bare rock — an **urchin barren** — and the fish and invertebrates that live in kelp declined. Where otters returned, the urchins were kept scarce and the kelp forests regrew.

<Sim topic="d4-2" id="keystone" />

The model shows the difference: removing crabs, a common prey of otters, reduces otter numbers, but urchins stay scarce, so the kelp forest survives. Removing the otters changes every population. This is why losing a keystone species risks **ecosystem collapse**, and why conservation often targets keystone species (see <TopicLink code="A4.2" />).

<Callout type="exam">

To explain why a species is a keystone, describe the **chain of effects** of removing it: otters removed → urchins released from predation → urchins overgraze kelp → loss of habitat and food for many other species → lower diversity.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="harvesting" title="Sustainable harvesting of natural resources" level="SL" covers="D4.2.6">

Wild plants and animals are **renewable resources**, but only if they are harvested **sustainably**: the **rate of harvesting** must not be greater than the **rate of replacement** by reproduction and growth.

### Maximum sustainable yield

A population growing logistically (see <TopicLink code="C4.1" />) adds the most new biomass per year when it is at **half its carrying capacity (K/2)**: there are enough breeding adults, and competition for resources is not yet intense. At K, births and natural deaths balance and there is nothing spare. The largest catch that can be taken every year for ever is the **maximum sustainable yield (MSY)**; in the logistic model MSY = rK/4, taken at N = K/2.

<Sim topic="d4-2" id="harvest" />

### A marine fish: Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*)

- **Grand Banks, off Newfoundland:** cod were caught for centuries, but industrial trawlers in the 1960s–1980s took far more than the replacement rate. Catches were based on over-optimistic estimates of the stock. The stock **collapsed**, and Canada stopped commercial fishing in 1992. Decades later it has still not fully recovered.
- **North Sea:** after cod numbers fell sharply, measures were agreed internationally: **catch quotas** based on surveys of the numbers and ages of fish, **larger net mesh sizes** so young fish escape, **closed areas** to protect spawning and nursery grounds, and fewer fishing boats. The stock then increased, though it has fluctuated since.

Sustainability of a fishery is assessed by estimating stock size and age structure (survey catches, catch per unit effort, counts of young fish) and comparing the catch with the estimated rate of replacement.

### A terrestrial plant: the Brazil nut (*Bertholletia excelsa*)

Brazil nuts are gathered from wild trees in the Amazon rainforest. The trees live for centuries, and seeds are spread and buried by **agoutis**. Harvesting is sustainable if enough nuts are left for new trees to establish; surveys of the **age structure** of the trees show whether young trees are replacing old ones. In heavily harvested areas there are few young trees, so harvesting is not sustainable in the long term. Logging of the forest also reduces the yield, and trees need their natural pollinators (large bees), so they crop poorly in plantations.

<Callout type="exam">

For "how can sustainability be assessed?", give measurements: population or stock size, age structure (are young individuals present?), rate of replacement compared with harvest rate, and trends over several years.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="agriculture" title="Sustainability of agriculture" level="SL" covers="D4.2.7">

Most human food now comes from agriculture. Intensive farming produces high yields, but several factors determine whether it can continue indefinitely:

| Factor | The problem | More sustainable practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Soil erosion** | Ploughing and leaving soil bare let wind and rain remove fertile topsoil far faster than new soil forms (about 1 cm per century or slower). | Cover crops, no-till farming, contour ploughing, terraces, hedges and windbreaks |
| **Leaching of nutrients** | Soluble nitrate (and some phosphate) drains out of the soil in rain water, wasting fertilizer and polluting water. | Apply fertilizer little and often, when crops are growing; buffer strips by rivers |
| **Supply of fertilizers and other inputs** | Nitrogen fertilizer is made by the Haber process using natural gas; phosphate is mined from rock reserves that are finite; irrigation can drain aquifers. | Recycle manure and sewage nutrients; grow nitrogen-fixing legumes in crop rotations |
| **Pollution by agrochemicals** | Pesticides and herbicides kill non-target species such as pollinators, can persist and biomagnify, and pests evolve resistance. | Integrated pest management, biological control, targeted spraying |
| **Carbon footprint** | Fossil fuels for machinery, fertilizer manufacture and transport; methane from cattle and rice paddies; nitrous oxide from fertilized soils; CO₂ from clearing forests. | Less tillage, efficient fertilizer use, less food waste, eating less meat |

The **carbon footprint** is the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by an activity or product, expressed as CO₂ equivalent. Food production is a major source of greenhouse gases, contributing to climate change (see <TopicLink code="D4.3" />).

<Callout type="note" title="Balanced view">

Many of these practices involve trade-offs: no-till farming reduces erosion and fuel use but may rely more on herbicides. A strong answer weighs advantages against disadvantages.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="eutrophication" title="Eutrophication and biochemical oxygen demand" level="SL" covers="D4.2.8">

**Leaching** is the loss of soluble ions such as **nitrate** (NO₃⁻) and **phosphate** (PO₄³⁻) from soil, carried away by water draining through it. Fertilizer that crops do not absorb, and manure or slurry that washes off fields, ends up in streams, rivers, lakes and coastal seas. Enrichment of water with nutrients is **eutrophication**.

<Anim topic="d4-2" id="eutrophication" />

### The sequence

1. Nitrate and phosphate had been **limiting factors** for algae; with more of them, **algae and cyanobacteria** grow explosively: an **algal bloom**.
2. The bloom **blocks light**, so submerged plants die. Bloom organisms are short-lived and also die.
3. **Saprotrophic bacteria** decompose the dead organic matter. They multiply and respire **aerobically**, using up dissolved oxygen: the **biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)** rises.
4. Dissolved oxygen falls (**hypoxia**). Fish and other aerobic animals die, adding more dead matter, so the process feeds on itself.

**BOD** is the amount of dissolved oxygen that microorganisms use to break down the organic matter in a water sample, usually measured over 5 days at 20 °C in the dark. Clean water has a low BOD; water with sewage or dead algae has a high BOD.

Freshwater growth is usually limited by phosphate and coastal growth by nitrogen, so both cause problems. In the sea, nutrient run-off creates seasonal **dead zones** of hypoxic water, for example in the Gulf of Mexico at the mouth of the Mississippi. Some blooms of cyanobacteria also release toxins that harm animals and people.

<Callout type="exam">

The oxygen is used mainly by **bacteria decomposing dead organisms**, not by the algae. Name them as saprotrophic (or aerobic) bacteria and link their respiration to the rise in BOD.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="biomagnification" title="Biomagnification of pollutants" level="SL" covers="D4.2.9">

Some pollutants become more concentrated at each step of a food chain. This **biomagnification** happens to substances that are:

- **persistent** — not broken down by organisms or the environment;
- **fat-soluble** — stored in fatty tissue rather than dissolved in blood and urine, so they are **not excreted**.

Within one organism the pollutant builds up over its lifetime (**bioaccumulation**). Then, because only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next (see <TopicLink code="C4.2" />), each consumer must eat a **large biomass** of food, and it retains the pollutant from all of it. The pollutant is concentrated at every step, so **top predators** receive the highest doses.

<Figure name="d4-2-biomagnification" caption="DDT residues in a Long Island estuary (Woodwell, Wurster and Isaacson, 1967), in parts per million on a log scale. The concentration in the fish-eating cormorant was about half a million times that in the water. The first step, from water to plankton, is uptake from the water itself." />

### DDT

**DDT** is an insecticide widely sprayed from the 1940s. It, and its breakdown product DDE, built up in birds of prey and fish-eating birds such as **peregrine falcons, ospreys, bald eagles and brown pelicans**. DDE interferes with calcium deposition in eggshells, so shells were **thin and broke** during incubation, and populations crashed. Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* (1962) publicized the problem; most countries banned DDT for farm use from the 1970s, and the bird populations recovered. It is still used in a few countries, inside houses, to control malaria mosquitoes.

### Mercury

Mercury released by **burning coal**, **gold mining** and industry is converted by bacteria in sediments into **methylmercury**, which is fat-soluble and biomagnifies. From the 1930s to the 1960s a chemical factory discharged methylmercury into **Minamata Bay**, Japan. People who ate fish and shellfish from the bay suffered severe nerve damage — **Minamata disease**, identified in 1956 — and children were born with brain damage. Today pregnant women are advised to limit large predatory fish such as **swordfish, shark and some tuna**, which have the highest mercury levels.

<Callout type="exam">

To explain biomagnification, use three ideas: the toxin is not broken down or excreted (fat-soluble, persistent); energy losses mean consumers eat a large mass of prey; so concentration rises at each trophic level.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="plastics" title="Plastic pollution of the oceans" level="SL" covers="D4.2.10">

Plastics are synthetic polymers, mostly made from oil. They are **persistent** because they are **not biodegradable**: very few decomposers have enzymes that can digest them. Instead plastics are broken into ever smaller fragments by UV light, waves and abrasion, and they build up in the oceans, on beaches and in sediments.

<Figure name="d4-2-plastics" caption="Plastic in the oceans ranges from whole nets and bags to fragments too small to see. Each size class harms marine life in different ways." />

### Macroplastics (larger than 5 mm)

- **Entanglement:** discarded fishing nets ("ghost nets"), ropes and packaging bands trap seals, turtles, whales and seabirds, which drown, starve or are injured.
- **Ingestion:** sea turtles swallow plastic bags that look like jellyfish. **Albatrosses** on Pacific islands such as Midway Atoll pick up floating plastic and feed it to their chicks; the plastic fills the stomach, causing blockage and starvation.

### Microplastics (smaller than 5 mm)

Microplastics include **microbeads** from cosmetics, plastic pellets, **fibres** shed from synthetic clothing, tyre dust and fragments of larger items. They are eaten by **zooplankton**, **filter feeders** such as mussels and oysters, and fish, where they can block guts and reduce feeding. Toxic chemicals such as PCBs stick to their surfaces, and these can pass up food chains. Even smaller **nanoplastics** can cross membranes into cells; their effects are still being researched.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: communicating science">

Clear communication of research can change public behaviour. Television coverage of plastic harming marine life, such as the BBC series *Blue Planet II* (2017), shifted public opinion in many countries and helped drive bans on microbeads, charges for plastic bags and moves to cut single-use plastics.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="rewilding" title="Rewilding: restoring natural processes" level="SL" covers="D4.2.11">

**Rewilding** aims to restore natural processes in a degraded area so that the ecosystem becomes **self-sustaining**, with as little human management as possible. Methods include:

- **Reintroducing apex predators and other keystone species**, which restore top-down control and engineer habitats. Grey wolves returned to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 reduced overgrazing by elk; Eurasian beavers released in Scotland create ponds and wetlands.
- **Re-establishing connectivity**: linking fragments of habitat with corridors over large areas so populations can move, interbreed and recolonize.
- **Minimizing human impact**: stopping farming, logging or fishing in the area, controlling invasive species, and otherwise allowing natural succession to proceed (**ecological management**).

### Hinewai Reserve, New Zealand

**Hinewai Reserve** on Banks Peninsula, in the South Island of New Zealand, is former sheep farmland that has been allowed to return to native forest since 1987. It now covers well over 1,000 hectares.

- Grazing farm animals were removed, and **introduced browsing mammals** such as goats, deer and brushtail possums are controlled, because they eat native seedlings.
- Otherwise there is **minimal interference**: no burning, no herbicide spraying and little planting. Birds disperse seeds of native trees into the area.
- The surprising strategy concerns **gorse** (*Ulex europaeus*), an invasive shrub that farmers fight. At Hinewai gorse is left alone: it acts as a **nurse canopy**, sheltering native tree seedlings, which grow up through it, overtop it and shade it out, because gorse cannot grow in shade.

This is secondary succession allowed to run its course. The result is regenerating native forest, with native birds and insects returning.

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to evaluate rewilding, mention that it is cheap to maintain and restores whole ecosystems, but it is slow, outcomes are unpredictable, and reintroduced predators can conflict with farmers.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="succession" title="Ecological succession and primary succession" level="AHL" covers="D4.2.12 D4.2.13">

**Ecological succession** is a sequence of changes in a community over time. Each community changes the **abiotic** conditions — light, soil depth, water, nutrients — in ways that allow other species to colonize and outcompete it. Species composition and abiotic factors keep changing each other until a relatively stable community develops.

### Triggers of succession

| Abiotic triggers | Biotic triggers |
|---|---|
| A glacier retreats, exposing bare rock | Beavers dam a stream, flooding woodland and creating a pond |
| A volcano produces new rock (Surtsey, Iceland, 1963) | A disease kills the dominant tree, e.g. ash dieback |
| A landslide, fire, flood or storm clears an area | An invasive species arrives or a grazer is removed |
| Sand is deposited to form new dunes | Farmland is abandoned |

**Primary succession** begins where there is no soil (bare rock, sand, volcanic ash). **Secondary succession** begins where soil remains after a disturbance.

<Anim topic="d4-2" id="succession" />

### Example: Glacier Bay, Alaska

Glaciers in Glacier Bay have retreated more than 100 km since the mid-1700s, exposing bare rock and gravel. Ecologists can study succession by comparing sites exposed at different times. The first colonists are cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses and low plants such as mountain avens (*Dryas*). These are replaced by **alder** shrubs, which fix nitrogen through symbiotic bacteria in root nodules and greatly enrich the soil. **Sitka spruce** then shades out the alders, and after about two centuries the community is a spruce–hemlock forest.

### Trends during primary succession

As succession proceeds there are increases in:

- the **size of plants** and the total **biomass**;
- **primary production**, because larger plants with more leaf area photosynthesize more per unit area;
- **species diversity**, as more species colonize than are lost;
- **complexity of food webs**, because more plant species provide more niches for animals and decomposers;
- **soil depth**, organic matter and water-holding capacity;
- **nutrient cycling**, as more dead organic matter is decomposed and more nutrients are held in soil and biomass rather than lost.

<Callout type="exam">

Name the trend **and** explain it. For example: "Species diversity increases because soil formation creates new habitats and niches, so more species can colonize than are outcompeted."

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="cyclical" title="Cyclical succession" level="AHL" covers="D4.2.14">

Succession does not always progress towards a single unchanging climax. Some ecosystems instead pass through a **cycle** of communities, each replacing the last, which repeats indefinitely. A patchwork of different phases exists side by side, so the ecosystem as a whole stays stable even though each patch keeps changing.

### Example: heather heathland

Studies of heathland dominated by **heather** (*Calluna vulgaris*), for example in Scotland, showed that each heather bush passes through four phases:

1. **Pioneer:** seedlings establish in a gap, with mosses, lichens and bare ground around them.
2. **Building:** the plant grows vigorously into a dense canopy that shades out most other plants.
3. **Mature:** growth slows and the canopy opens in the centre, so other plants return.
4. **Degenerate:** the old central stems die and collapse, leaving a gap colonized by lichens and mosses, until heather seedlings or shoots establish again.

<Figure name="d4-2-cyclical" caption="Cyclical succession in a heather patch. Ages are approximate and vary with site and climate; on managed moors, burning restarts the cycle artificially." level="AHL" />

### Other examples

- **Californian chaparral**: fire-adapted shrubland where fires every few decades clear old shrubs, and many species regenerate from seeds or roots that need fire.
- **Wood pasture** in north-west Europe: oak seedlings grow up in grassland where grazing is light; mature oaks shade out their own seedlings, and when old trees fall, grassland returns.

<Section id="climax" title="Climax communities and arrested succession" level="AHL" covers="D4.2.15">

Under given environmental conditions, succession tends to lead to a particular **climax community**: the community that is stable and self-perpetuating under that climate and soil. In lowland Britain it is usually deciduous woodland; in the Amazon basin, tropical rainforest; in drier continental interiors, grassland (see <TopicLink code="B4.1" />). The climax has the greatest biomass the conditions allow, and its species replace themselves rather than being replaced.

Human activities can stop succession before the climax is reached. This is **arrested succession**, and the community that persists is a **plagioclimax**.

<Figure name="d4-2-arrested" caption="Grazing arrests succession on land at grassland; drainage of a wetland stops the wetland community developing and allows farmland instead." level="AHL" />

### Grazing by farm livestock

Sheep and cattle kept at high densities eat almost every seedling of shrubs and trees. Grasses survive because they grow from the base, near the ground, so they recover after grazing. Grassland persists where the climax would be woodland. Much of upland Britain is grassland or moorland maintained this way; when grazing stops, scrub and then woodland return.

### Drainage of wetlands

Wetlands such as fens and bogs develop by succession in waterlogged ground, where lack of oxygen slows decomposition and **peat** accumulates. Drainage **lowers the water table**: the peat becomes aerated, saprotrophs decompose it, and wetland plants and animals die out. The land is usually ploughed or grazed as farmland, so the wetland community never reaches its climax. The Fens of eastern England, drained from the 1600s onwards, are an example: as the peat oxidized, the ground surface fell by several metres.
