<Section id="cell-theory" title="Cells are the basic unit of life" level="SL" covers="A2.2.1">

Every organism that has been examined is made of one or more **cells**. Single-celled organisms such as bacteria, yeasts and *Paramecium* are **unicellular**; plants, animals and most fungi are **multicellular**. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the functions of life on its own, so it is the basic structural unit of all living organisms. This is **cell theory**.

### A theory, not just a fact

Cell theory began with observations. In the 17th century Robert Hooke described the "cells" of cork, and later microscopists such as Schleiden and Schwann found cells in every plant and animal tissue they examined. A theory goes further than a list of observations: it is a **general explanation** that applies widely.

### Using cell theory deductively

**Deductive reasoning** moves from a general theory to a specific prediction. If all organisms are made of cells, then a newly discovered organism, from a deep-sea vent or a sample of soil, will consist of one or more cells. Each time a prediction like this is tested and found to be true, the theory is **corroborated**. If it were found to be false, the theory would have to be changed.

Testing a theory also means looking for awkward cases. Some cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds (see **Atypical cell structure** below). These challenge what a "typical" cell looks like, but none of them is a living thing that is not made of cells, so cell theory stands.

<Callout type="exam">

If asked to "deduce" what a new organism will be made of, quote the theory and then the prediction: "According to cell theory, all organisms consist of cells, so this organism will consist of one or more cells."

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="microscopy-skills" title="Microscopy skills and magnification" level="SL" covers="A2.2.2">

### Making a slide

1. **Temporary mount**: put a thin layer of cells or tissue (onion epidermis, a cheek-cell smear, moss leaf) on a glass slide in a drop of water or stain.
2. **Cover slip**: lower it at an angle so that no air bubbles are trapped; blot off excess liquid.
3. **Staining**: most cell structures are colourless, so a stain that binds to particular chemicals is added. **Methylene blue** binds to DNA and so shows up nuclei in animal cells; **iodine solution** stains starch blue-black and makes the nuclei of plant cells easier to see.

### Focusing

Start with the lowest-power objective and the stage lowered. Use the **coarse focusing knob** to bring the specimen roughly into focus, then the **fine focusing knob** to sharpen it. Move to a higher-power objective and use only the fine knob, so that the lens does not hit the slide. Total magnification = eyepiece × objective, for example ×10 × ×40 = ×400.

### Measuring with an eyepiece graticule

An **eyepiece graticule** is a scale inside the eyepiece. Its divisions have no fixed size, so they are **calibrated** for each objective against a **stage micrometer** (a slide ruled in 0.01 mm divisions). If 40 eyepiece units line up with 100 µm on the micrometer, one unit = 2.5 µm, and a cell 12 units across is 30 µm wide. Measuring in this way is **quantitative observation** made with an instrument.

### Calculating sizes and adding a scale bar

**Magnification = image size ÷ actual size.** Rearranged: actual size = image size ÷ magnification, and image size = actual size × magnification. Always convert both lengths to the same unit first (1 mm = 1,000 µm; 1 µm = 1,000 nm).

A **scale bar** is a line on an image labelled with the length it represents. Its length = the length it represents × magnification: at ×2,000, a bar for 10 µm is 20,000 µm = 20 mm long. Photographs taken through the eyepiece, even with a phone, record specimens for later measuring.

<Section id="microscopy-advances" title="Developments in microscopy" level="SL" covers="A2.2.3">

**Magnification** is how much larger an image is than the object. **Resolution** is the smallest distance between two points at which they can still be told apart. Light has a long wavelength, so a light microscope cannot resolve points closer than about 0.2 µm, however much the image is enlarged.

### Electron microscopy

Electrons have a far shorter wavelength than light, so an **electron microscope** resolves around 1 nm or better for biological specimens. This reveals the **ultrastructure** of cells: membranes, ribosomes, cristae and thylakoids.

| | Light microscope | Electron microscope |
|---|---|---|
| Resolution | about 200 nm (0.2 µm) | about 1 nm or better |
| Useful magnification | up to about ×1,500 | ×500,000 or more |
| Specimens | living or dead | dead, in a vacuum |
| Image | colour | black and white (colour can be added) |

A **transmission electron microscope (TEM)** sends electrons through very thin sections to show internal structure; a **scanning electron microscope (SEM)** scans the surface and gives three-dimensional-looking images.

### Freeze fracture

A specimen is frozen very rapidly and then cracked with a blade. The fracture follows the weakest regions, which are often the **middle of membranes**, between the two layers of phospholipids. The exposed surface is coated with a thin film of metal at an angle (shadowing) and carbon to make a **replica**, which is viewed by electron microscopy. It shows the inner faces of membranes, studded with proteins that span the bilayer, which was key evidence for the fluid mosaic model (<TopicLink code="B2.1" />).

### Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM)

A thin layer of a solution of a protein or virus is frozen so quickly, in liquid ethane, that the water forms glass-like (vitreous) ice instead of crystals. Thousands of images of individual molecules are combined by computer into a **3D structure at near-atomic resolution**. Molecules are seen in their natural state, without stains, and in different shapes they adopt as they work. Cryo-EM was used to find the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein within weeks in 2020.

### Fluorescent stains and immunofluorescence

**Fluorescent stains** absorb light of one wavelength and emit light of a longer wavelength, so stained structures glow brightly against a dark background (for example, DAPI binds DNA and glows blue). In **immunofluorescence**, a fluorescent dye is attached to **antibodies** that bind one specific protein, showing exactly where that protein is in the cell. Using several antibodies with different colours shows several proteins at once.

</Section>

<Section id="prokaryotes" title="Structures all cells share, and prokaryote cells" level="SL" covers="A2.2.4 A2.2.5">

<Viewer3D topic="a2-2" id="cells" />

### Three things every typical cell has

| Structure | Why every cell needs it |
|---|---|
| **DNA** as its genetic material | Stores the genetic instructions for making the cell's proteins (via mRNA) and is copied and passed on when the cell divides. |
| **Cytoplasm**, mainly water | Water is the solvent in which enzymes and their substrates dissolve, so metabolic reactions can happen. |
| **Plasma membrane** made of lipids | A barrier between the cell and its surroundings that controls what enters and leaves, so conditions inside (pH, concentrations) can differ from those outside. |

All cells also have ribosomes, which make proteins.

### Prokaryote cell structure

**Prokaryotes** (bacteria and archaea) have no nucleus. The structure you need is that of **Gram-positive eubacteria**, such as rod-shaped ***Bacillus*** and spherical ***Staphylococcus***, which grows in grape-like clusters:

- **Cell wall** of **peptidoglycan**, thick in Gram-positive bacteria, which have no outer membrane. It prevents the cell bursting when water enters by osmosis.
- **Plasma membrane** just inside the wall.
- **Cytoplasm** with enzymes and no membrane-bound compartments.
- **Naked DNA in a loop**: one circular chromosome, not bound to histones, in a paler region called the **nucleoid**.
- **70S ribosomes**, smaller than eukaryotic 80S ribosomes.

Prokaryote structure **varies**. Many bacteria have **plasmids**, **pili** or **flagella**, and some have a **capsule**; Gram-negative bacteria have a thin wall and an extra outer membrane; a few, such as mycoplasmas, have no wall. You do not need details of these variations.

<Section id="eukaryotes" title="Eukaryote cell structure" level="SL" covers="A2.2.6">

**Eukaryotes** (animals, plants, fungi and protists) have a **compartmentalized** cytoplasm: membranes divide it into separate compartments, each with its own conditions and enzymes. Almost every eukaryotic cell has:

- a **plasma membrane** around the outside, holding in the cytoplasm;
- **80S ribosomes**, some floating free and some bound to the rough ER;
- a **nucleus** wrapped in two membranes (the **nuclear envelope**) with **nuclear pores** through it; inside are the **chromosomes**, each a linear DNA molecule wound around **histone** proteins;
- organelles enclosed by membranes: **mitochondria**, rough and smooth **endoplasmic reticulum**, the **Golgi apparatus**, **lysosomes** and many **vesicles** and **vacuoles**;
- a **cytoskeleton** built from **microtubules** (hollow tubulin tubes) and **microfilaments** (thinner actin fibres) that gives the cell its shape and moves organelles, vesicles and chromosomes around.

### Organelles by number of membranes

| Membranes | Structures |
|---|---|
| None | 80S ribosomes, cytoskeleton, centrioles |
| One | rough and smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, vesicles, lysosomes, vacuoles |
| Two | nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts |

### Prokaryotes and eukaryotes compared

| | Prokaryote | Eukaryote |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | none: DNA in a nucleoid | DNA inside a double membrane with pores |
| DNA | naked, circular (plus plasmids) | linear chromosomes bound to histones |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytoplasm (70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | none | many |
| Typical size | 1–5 µm | 10–100 µm |

Compartmentalization lets incompatible processes run side by side: digestive enzymes are kept inside lysosomes, and the nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation. For how organelles work together, see <TopicLink code="B2.2" />.

</Section>

<Section id="unicellular" title="Life processes in unicellular organisms" level="SL" covers="A2.2.7">

A **unicellular** organism must carry out every function of life within a single cell. Two well-studied freshwater examples are ***Paramecium***, a heterotroph covered in cilia, and ***Chlamydomonas***, a photosynthetic alga with two flagella.

| Function | *Paramecium* | *Chlamydomonas* |
|---|---|---|
| **Homeostasis** (keeping internal conditions within limits) | **Contractile vacuoles** pump out water that keeps entering by osmosis | Contractile vacuoles pump out excess water |
| **Metabolism** (all the cell's chemical reactions) | Enzymes in the cytoplasm and organelles, such as respiration in mitochondria | Respiration and photosynthesis |
| **Nutrition** (obtaining food) | Cilia sweep bacteria into an oral groove; they are digested in **food vacuoles** | Makes its own food by photosynthesis in one large chloroplast |
| **Movement** | Beating cilia | Two beating flagella |
| **Excretion** (removing metabolic wastes) | CO₂ and ammonia diffuse out across the membrane | CO₂ (and O₂ from photosynthesis) diffuse out |
| **Growth** (increase in size and dry mass) | Assimilates digested food | Assimilates sugars made by photosynthesis |
| **Response to stimuli** | Reverses its cilia and backs away after hitting an obstacle | An **eyespot** detects light; it swims towards moderate light |
| **Reproduction** | Asexual by binary fission (mitosis); also a sexual process | Asexual by mitosis; also sexual, with gametes |

Notice that **excretion** means removing waste products of metabolism; getting rid of excess water is **osmoregulation**, part of homeostasis.

<Callout type="exam">

Questions often ask you to "outline how a unicellular organism carries out" two or three functions. Name the structure and say what it does, for example "contractile vacuoles expel excess water, so the cell does not burst (homeostasis)".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="cell-types" title="Animal, fungal and plant cells compared" level="SL" covers="A2.2.8">

All three are eukaryotic, so they share a nucleus, mitochondria, 80S ribosomes, ER, Golgi apparatus and a cytoskeleton. They differ in a few key features (compare the three eukaryotic 3D models above).

| Feature | Animal cells | Fungal cells | Plant cells |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell wall** | none | yes, mainly **chitin** | yes, mainly **cellulose** |
| **Vacuoles** | small and temporary (vesicles, food vacuoles, lysosomes) | often large and permanent | one large, permanent **sap vacuole** with a tonoplast |
| **Plastids** (chloroplasts, amyloplasts) | none | none | yes, though not all cells have chloroplasts |
| **Centrioles** | yes | absent from most | absent (except in the swimming sperm of mosses and ferns) |
| **Cilia and flagella** | in some cells (ciliated epithelium, sperm) | absent from most (a flagellum on the spores of chytrids) | absent from flowering plants and conifers; flagella on the sperm of mosses and ferns |
| **Carbohydrate store** | glycogen | glycogen | starch |

### Why the differences matter

- A **wall** resists the pressure of water entering by osmosis, so plant and fungal cells do not burst; animal cells have no wall, which lets them change shape and take in food by endocytosis.
- In plants the **large vacuole** stores sap and, by taking up water, keeps the cell turgid; in fungal hyphae, vacuoles fill the older parts and push cytoplasm towards the growing tip.
- **Plastids** are found only in plants and algae: **chloroplasts** for photosynthesis, **amyloplasts** for storing starch.
- **Centrioles** help organize the spindle in animal cells, and the related **basal bodies** anchor cilia and flagella; plant cells build a spindle without them.

<Callout type="exam">

In a "compare and contrast" answer, give paired points, for example "plant and fungal cells both have a cell wall, but it is made of cellulose in plants and chitin in fungi". Remember that plant cells have mitochondria as well as chloroplasts.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="atypical" title="Atypical cell structure in eukaryotes" level="SL" covers="A2.2.9">

A typical eukaryotic cell has **one nucleus**. Counting nuclei shows that some cells are **atypical**:

- **Anucleate** cells, with no nucleus, cannot transcribe DNA, so they cannot make new proteins.
- **Multinucleate** cells, with many nuclei, can make more mRNA and protein for a large volume of cytoplasm.

<Figure name="a2-2-atypical" caption="Four atypical cells. Red blood cells and mature sieve tube elements have no nucleus; skeletal muscle fibres and aseptate hyphae have many nuclei sharing one continuous cytoplasm." />

| Cell | Nuclei | How it comes about, and why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Mammalian **red blood cell** | 0 | The nucleus is pushed out as the cell matures; more space for haemoglobin, but no repairs, so it lives only about 120 days |
| **Phloem sieve tube element** | 0 at maturity | The nucleus and most organelles break down so sap flows freely; a neighbouring **companion cell** keeps it alive |
| **Skeletal muscle fibre** | many | Formed by the fusion of many cells; one fibre can be centimetres long |
| **Aseptate fungal hypha** | many | No cross-walls (septa), so nuclei share continuous cytoplasm, as in *Rhizopus* |

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science">

Atypical cells test cell theory without disproving it: each still consists of cytoplasm inside a plasma membrane and develops from typical cells. They show that a general theory can hold even when individual cases differ from the "typical" pattern.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="micrographs" title="Micrographs: identifying and drawing cells" level="SL" covers="A2.2.10 A2.2.11">

### Identifying the type of cell

1. **Prokaryote or eukaryote?** A **nucleoid** and no membrane-bound organelles means prokaryote; a **nucleus** (or visible chromosomes, if the cell is dividing) and organelles such as mitochondria means eukaryote. Cells that are part of a multicellular tissue are eukaryotic.
2. **Plant or animal?** A **cell wall**, a **large sap vacuole** and **chloroplasts** point to a plant; no wall, only small vacuoles and perhaps **microvilli** or cilia point to an animal. Remember that fungi and protists are neither, and that one thin section may miss structures that are present elsewhere in the cell.

### What structures look like in electron micrographs

| Structure | Appearance |
|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | Large, round; double membrane with gaps (pores); grainy chromatin with darker patches (heterochromatin) and a dense nucleolus |
| **Chromosomes** | Dense, dark threads, visible separately only during cell division |
| **Mitochondrion** | Oval, double membrane, inner one folded into **cristae**; matrix often darker than cytoplasm |
| **Chloroplast** | Larger, double membrane, stacks of thylakoids (**grana**), often pale starch grains |
| **Sap vacuole** | Large, very pale area bounded by a single membrane |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Stack of curved, flattened sacs with vesicles at the edges |
| **Rough ER / smooth ER** | Parallel sheets dotted with ribosomes / branching tubules without them |
| **Ribosomes** | Tiny dark dots, free or on rough ER |
| **Cell wall / plasma membrane** | Thick layer outside the membrane / a thin dark line |
| **Microvilli** | Many finger-like folds of the plasma membrane |
| **Nucleoid**, **prokaryotic wall** | Paler central region with no membrane / thick layer outside the membrane |

<Section id="endosymbiosis" title="The origin of eukaryotic cells by endosymbiosis" level="AHL" covers="A2.2.12">

All eukaryotes appear to descend from one single-celled **common ancestor** that already had a nucleus and reproduced sexually. Mitochondria and chloroplasts were added later, by **endosymbiosis**: one cell living inside another.

1. An aerobic bacterium was taken into the ancestral cell by endocytosis but not digested.
2. It survived inside a vesicle, so it was surrounded by two membranes, and it supplied ATP from aerobic respiration while the host provided food and protection. Both benefited (**mutualism**).
3. It divided by binary fission inside the host and was passed to daughter cells when the host divided.
4. Over many generations most of its genes moved to the host nucleus and it became a **mitochondrion**, found in almost all eukaryotes.
5. Later, in **some** eukaryotes, a photosynthetic **cyanobacterium** was taken in the same way and became the **chloroplast**. Its descendants include the green and red algae and the plants; some other algae gained chloroplasts later by engulfing such algae (**secondary endosymbiosis**).

<Anim topic="a2-2" id="endosymbiosis" />

### Evidence

Mitochondria and chloroplasts:

- have **double membranes**, as expected if a bacterium was enclosed by a vesicle;
- contain their own **naked, circular DNA**, like bacteria, with genes that are transcribed;
- have **70S ribosomes** and make some of their own proteins;
- **replicate** only by dividing in two (binary fission), so a cell cannot make them from scratch;
- are about the **size of bacteria** (around 1–10 µm);
- have gene sequences most similar to those of particular groups of bacteria, and some antibiotics that block bacterial 70S ribosomes also affect them.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science">

A theory is strong when it explains many separate observations and its predictions are confirmed. The endosymbiotic theory explains all the features above; it also predicted that organelle DNA would resemble bacterial DNA, which gene sequencing later confirmed. (Strictly, mitochondrial ribosomes vary in size between groups — in mammals they are nearer 55S — but they are built like bacterial ribosomes; for IB, call them 70S.)

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

When asked for evidence, give the feature **and** link it to bacteria, for example "mitochondria have 70S ribosomes, the same size as bacterial ribosomes, not the 80S ribosomes of eukaryotic cytoplasm".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="differentiation" title="Cell differentiation" level="AHL" covers="A2.2.13">

A multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so almost all its cells contain the **same genome**. Yet they become very different: neurons, muscle fibres, red blood cells and xylem vessels. **Differentiation** is the process by which a cell develops a specialized structure and function, and its basis is **different patterns of gene expression**.

- **Housekeeping genes**, such as those for the enzymes of respiration, are expressed in almost every cell.
- Other genes are expressed only in particular cell types. The genes for haemoglobin are expressed only in developing red blood cells; the insulin gene only in β cells of the pancreas.

### What switches genes on

Which genes a cell expresses is often triggered by **changes in its environment**, meaning its surroundings inside or outside the body:

- **chemical signals** from neighbouring cells, or concentration gradients of signal molecules that tell a cell its position in an embryo;
- **hormones** circulating in the body;
- **external conditions**: a honeybee larva fed royal jelly develops into a queen rather than a worker, with the same genes; in plants, light switches on the genes for chloroplast development.

Cells that receive the same signals develop in the same way and form a **tissue**: a group of cells with the same structure and function. The advantage is that each cell type can be specialized for one job and do it more efficiently. Stem cells, which have not yet differentiated, are covered in <TopicLink code="B2.3" />.

<Callout type="exam">

Avoid saying that specialized cells "have different genes" or "lose genes". Say they **express** different genes (switch different genes on or off).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="multicellularity" title="Evolution of multicellularity" level="AHL" covers="A2.2.14">

Multicellularity was not a single evolutionary event. It has **evolved independently many times**, by cells staying together after division (or gathering together) and becoming dependent on each other:

- **all animals** and **all plants** are multicellular (each group arose from a single multicellular origin);
- **many fungi** are multicellular (mushroom-forming fungi and moulds), but others, such as yeasts, are unicellular;
- **many eukaryotic algae** are multicellular, and multicellularity evolved separately in brown algae (such as kelps), red algae and several groups of green algae, for example *Volvox*.

Close relatives of multicellular groups are often unicellular, such as *Chlamydomonas* among the green algae, which is evidence that multicellularity arose separately in different lineages.

### Advantages

- **Larger body size**: a multicellular organism is not limited by the size one cell can reach, which helps it to compete for light (trees, kelps), to catch prey or to avoid being eaten.
- **Cell specialization**: different cells can differentiate for different functions (division of labour), so each function is carried out more efficiently and more complex body forms are possible.
- **Longer life**: the death of a few cells does not kill the organism, because they can be replaced.

Large organisms also need cells to stick together (cell-adhesion molecules, <TopicLink code="B2.1" />) and to communicate, so multicellularity brought new problems as well as advantages.
