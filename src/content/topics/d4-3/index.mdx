<Section id="causes" title="Human causes of climate change" level="SL" covers="D4.3.1">

The Earth's surface is warmed by sunlight, and it would be far colder without the **greenhouse effect**. Climate change today is driven by **anthropogenic** (human-caused) increases in two greenhouse gases: **carbon dioxide** and **methane**.

<Anim topic="d4-3" id="greenhouse" />

### Rising carbon dioxide and methane

| Gas | Before industrialization | 2024 | Main human sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CO₂** | about 280 ppm | about 425 ppm (Mauna Loa) | **combustion of fossil fuels** (coal, oil, natural gas); **deforestation** and land clearance (burning and decay of biomass); drainage and burning of **peat** |
| **CH₄** | about 0.72 ppm | about 1.93 ppm | **ruminant livestock** (methanogens in the rumen); **flooded rice paddies** and **landfill** (anaerobic decomposition); **leaks** from coal mines and gas wells |

The CO₂ rise is the long-term trend in the Keeling curve (<TopicLink code="C4.2" />). Natural processes balance in the long run: photosynthesis removes roughly as much CO₂ as respiration releases, and methane made by methanogens in wetlands is gradually oxidized in the atmosphere. Human activities add carbon from **fossil stores** faster than oceans and plants can absorb it.

Each molecule of methane absorbs far more infrared than CO₂: per kilogram it causes about **80 times** as much warming over 20 years. It stays in the air for only about a decade, though, whereas much of the extra CO₂ persists for centuries, so CO₂ causes the larger share of warming.

<Callout type="exam" title="Greenhouse effect vs enhanced greenhouse effect">

The **natural** greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable (about +15 °C rather than −18 °C). The **enhanced** greenhouse effect is the extra warming from human-caused rises in CO₂ and CH₄. Say "absorb and re-emit **long-wave infrared**", never "trap sunlight", and never mix it up with the ozone layer.

</Callout>

### Correlation, causation and the ice-core record

<Figure name="d4-3-ice-core" caption="Schematic of the Antarctic ice-core record. CO₂ in trapped air and temperature (from hydrogen isotopes in the ice) rose and fell together through eight glacial cycles, never exceeding about 300 ppm; today's level is far above that range." />

The two curves show a strong **positive correlation**, but correlation alone cannot show which variable causes which, or whether a third factor drives both. In fact, at the end of ice ages slight changes in Earth's orbit began the warming, and CO₂ released from the warming ocean then amplified it: CO₂ was both effect and cause. The evidence that **today's** CO₂ rise causes warming comes from several independent lines:

- **Mechanism**: laboratory measurements since the 1850s show CO₂ and CH₄ absorb infrared.
- **Fingerprint**: the extra CO₂ has the isotope signature of fossil carbon (no ¹⁴C and relatively little ¹³C).
- **Pattern**: the lower atmosphere is warming while the upper atmosphere cools, which fits more greenhouse gas, not a brighter Sun.

Global surface temperature in 2011–2020 averaged about 1.1 °C above 1850–1900, and 2024 was the warmest year on record, about 1.5 °C above.

</Section>

<Section id="feedback" title="Positive feedback in global warming" level="SL" covers="D4.3.2">

In **positive feedback**, a change sets off effects that **increase** that same change. Several processes in the climate system respond to warming by causing more warming, so the warming from our emissions is amplified.

| Feedback | How warming causes more warming |
|---|---|
| **Loss of snow and ice** | Snow and ice reflect most sunlight (high **albedo**). As they melt, darker ocean, rock and vegetation are exposed and absorb more solar radiation. |
| **Decomposition of peat and permafrost organic matter** | **Permafrost** is ground frozen for at least two years running. Dead organic matter in it has stayed undecomposed for thousands of years, because saprotrophs cannot act at low temperatures. As it thaws, and as peat warms, saprotrophs decompose it faster and release CO₂. |
| **Methane from thawing permafrost** | Thawed soils are often **waterlogged and anaerobic**, so methanogens convert organic matter to methane, a more potent greenhouse gas. |
| **CO₂ from the oceans** | Warm water dissolves less CO₂, and a warmer, more stratified surface layer mixes less with the deep ocean, so oceans absorb less of our emissions. Changes in ocean circulation can bring CO₂-rich **deep water** to the surface, where it degasses, as happened at the end of ice ages. |
| **Droughts and forest fires** | Hotter, drier summers increase drought and the frequency and intensity of fires, which release CO₂ by combustion and leave less vegetation to absorb CO₂ by photosynthesis. |

Permafrost soils hold roughly **1,400–1,600 billion tonnes** of carbon, about twice as much as the atmosphere, which is why their thaw is a major concern.

<Sim topic="d4-3" id="feedback" />

The feedbacks do not usually cause runaway warming: each round of feedback adds less than the round before, so warming settles at a higher level. But where a feedback becomes self-sustaining in one part of the system, a **tipping point** can be passed.

<Callout type="exam">

Describe a feedback loop as a **closed chain** that returns to its start: "warming → sea ice melts → darker ocean absorbs more solar radiation → **more warming**". Name the gas involved (CO₂ or CH₄) for the carbon feedbacks, and the process (decomposition by saprotrophs, methanogenesis, combustion).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="boreal" title="Boreal forests: a tipping point from sink to source" level="SL" covers="D4.3.3">

**Boreal forest (taiga)** stretches across Canada, Alaska, Scandinavia and Russia. It is dominated by conifers such as spruce, pine, fir and larch, and holds about a third of the carbon in the world's forests, much of it in cold, slowly decomposing soils and peat. For thousands of years it has been a **net carbon sink**: photosynthesis exceeded respiration and combustion, so carbon accumulated.

### How warming can flip it to a source

The high latitudes are warming roughly two to four times faster than the global average. In the taiga this brings:

1. **Warmer temperatures and less winter snow.** Snow melts earlier, so less meltwater is stored in the soil for summer.
2. **Drought.** Trees suffer water stress in hot, dry summers, and drought-weakened trees are more easily killed by insect pests.
3. **Lower primary production and browning.** Satellite images show "browning", a fall in green leaf cover, over large areas, as photosynthesis declines.
4. **More frequent and more intense fires.** Drier fuel burns more often and more deeply.
5. **Combustion of legacy carbon.** Deep organic soil layers that survived previous fires, storing carbon for decades or centuries, now burn too.

When losses from fire, respiration and decomposition exceed the carbon taken up by photosynthesis, the forest changes from **net carbon accumulation to net carbon loss**. Because that release causes more warming, which causes more fire and drought, the change can become **self-reinforcing**. Burned stands may regrow as broadleaf woodland or grassland that stores less carbon, so the system does not simply return to its old state.

This is an example of a **tipping point**: a threshold beyond which a small extra change shifts a system into a different state that is hard to reverse (tipping points are also discussed in <TopicLink code="D4.2" />). Canada's record 2023 fire season burned more than 15 million hectares, and in some recent years boreal fires have released as much carbon as a large industrial country emits from fossil fuels.

<Callout type="note" title="Sink or source?">

An ecosystem is a **sink** when it takes up more CO₂ (photosynthesis) than it releases (respiration + combustion), and a **source** when it releases more than it takes up. The same forest can switch between them: the boreal forest is a key example of such a switch acting as a tipping point.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="polar" title="Melting landfast ice and sea ice" level="SL" covers="D4.3.4">

Polar ice is a **habitat**: a solid platform on which animals rest, breed and reach their food. Two types matter here:

- **Landfast ice** (fast ice) is sea ice frozen to the coast or to grounded icebergs, so it stays in place.
- **Sea ice** (pack ice) floats freely and drifts with winds and currents.

Both are forming later, shrinking and breaking up earlier. The Arctic's September sea-ice extent has fallen by roughly 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.

### Emperor penguins (*Aptenodytes forsteri*): losing breeding grounds

Emperor penguins breed on **landfast ice** around Antarctica in the southern winter. Eggs are laid around May–June, incubated by the males on their feet, and the chicks need the ice to stay stable until about December–January, when they have grown **waterproof adult feathers** and can go to sea. If the fast ice **breaks out early**, chicks still covered in down fall into the sea and drown or freeze.

In 2022, record-low sea ice in the Bellingshausen Sea region led to **total breeding failure at four of five colonies** there, because the ice broke up before the chicks fledged. Models predict that most colonies could be lost by 2100 if warming continues at a high rate.

### Walruses: losing sea-ice platforms

Walruses dive to feed on clams and other animals on the sea floor of shallow continental shelves. Females and calves rest on **sea ice** between dives, which lets them feed over wide areas far from land and keeps calves away from predators and crowds. In the Chukchi Sea, summer ice now often retreats **beyond the shelf** over water too deep to feed in, so walruses must haul out on beaches instead:

- **tens of thousands crowd together**, and calves can be **crushed** when the herd stampedes;
- they must make **longer swims** from shore to feeding areas, using more energy;
- feeding grounds near the haul-out sites are **depleted**.

<Callout type="exam">

Name both examples precisely: emperor penguins lose **landfast ice** used for **breeding** (Antarctic); walruses lose **sea ice** used for **resting and access to feeding grounds** (Arctic). Either the common or the scientific name is acceptable.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="upwelling" title="Ocean currents and nutrient upwelling" level="SL" covers="D4.3.5">

In the open ocean, mineral nutrients such as nitrate, phosphate and iron are quickly used up by phytoplankton in the sunlit surface layer. Dead organisms and faeces sink, so the nutrients accumulate in **deep water**, where there is too little light for photosynthesis.

### Upwelling

In some places, **winds and the Earth's rotation** push surface water away from a coast, and cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises to replace it. This **upwelling** supplies nutrients to the surface, so phytoplankton grow fast. High **primary production** supports large populations of zooplankton, fish (such as the Peruvian anchoveta), seabirds and marine mammals. Upwelling zones off Peru, California and south-west Africa cover only a small fraction of the ocean surface but yield a large share of the world's fish catch.

### How warming disrupts it

Warm water is **less dense** than cold water, so as the surface warms it floats more stably on top of the cold deep water. This **stratification** reduces mixing, and changes in winds and currents can alter the timing and strength of upwelling. The result:

> warmer surface water → less upwelling of nutrients → less phytoplankton growth → **lower primary production** → less energy passed along **marine food chains**

A natural example shows the effect. During **El Niño** events, trade winds weaken and warm water spreads east across the Pacific, suppressing upwelling off Peru. In 1972–73 this contributed to the collapse of the anchoveta fishery, and seabird colonies that depend on the fish declined sharply.

<Callout type="tip">

This understanding links energy flow (<TopicLink code="C4.2" />) with climate change: nutrients limit **primary production**, and every higher trophic level depends on the energy fixed by producers.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="ranges" title="Range shifts: poleward and upslope" level="SL" covers="D4.3.6">

Every species can survive only within its **range of tolerance** for temperature and other factors (<TopicLink code="B4.1" />). As the climate warms, the zones with suitable conditions move **towards the poles** and **up mountains** (temperature falls with altitude). Populations at the **warm edge** of a range decline, while new populations can establish at the **cool edge**, so the range **shifts**. Animals do this by moving; plants do it through seed dispersal and differences in seedling survival.

<Figure name="d4-3-range-shift" caption="Left: on Karkar Island, birds' upper elevation limits moved upslope by about 152 m on average between 1969 and 2013 (one species' range shown schematically). Right: in eastern North American trees, comparing seedling and adult ranges suggests contraction is more common than a northward shift." />

### Upslope: tropical montane birds in New Guinea

Researchers repeated bird surveys first carried out by Jared Diamond on mountains in Papua New Guinea. On **Karkar Island** (surveyed 1969, resurveyed 2013), the **upper elevation limits** of the montane species had moved **upslope**, by about **152 m** on average. On **Mount Karimui** (1965 and 2012) upper limits rose by about 113 m. Tropical montane species often have narrow temperature tolerances, so they respond strongly. Species already near a summit have nowhere higher to go and may become extinct (<TopicLink code="A4.2" />).

### Poleward: North American trees

Trees cannot move, and they live for decades or centuries, so their ranges change slowly. A study of **92 tree species** in the eastern USA compared where **seedlings** grow with where **adult** trees grow:

- about **21%** showed a **northward spread** (seedlings beyond adults in the north, absent in the south);
- about **59%** showed **range contraction** at both edges (seedlings failing at the southern edge but not yet spreading north);
- about 16% shifted south.

Other studies show conifers tending to move north, while many broadleaf species have shifted west, perhaps following rainfall. Trees are often too slow to keep up with the speed of warming.

</Section>

<Section id="coral" title="Coral reefs: a risk of ecosystem collapse" level="SL" covers="D4.3.7">

Tropical coral reefs are built by **reef-building (hard) corals**: colonies of small polyps that secrete skeletons of **calcium carbonate** (CaCO₃). Inside the polyps' cells live **zooxanthellae**, photosynthetic algae that supply the coral with sugars, in a mutualism (<TopicLink code="C4.1" />). Reefs cover far less than 1% of the sea floor but support roughly a quarter of all marine species. Rising CO₂ threatens them in two separate ways.

<Figure name="d4-3-coral" caption="Two separate threats: ocean acidification (caused by dissolved CO₂) reduces carbonate ions and slows calcification; high temperature causes bleaching." />

### Ocean acidification: caused by increased CO₂

The oceans have absorbed roughly a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by humans. Dissolved CO₂ reacts with water:

> CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

The extra hydrogen ions lower the pH, from about **8.2** before industrialization to about **8.1** now (about 30% more H⁺, because pH is a logarithmic scale). They also react with **carbonate ions**:

> H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻

Corals need carbonate ions to combine with calcium (Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃), so fewer carbonate ions means **suppressed calcification**: skeletons grow more slowly and are weaker, and if carbonate falls far enough, existing CaCO₃ starts to dissolve.

### Bleaching: caused by high temperature

When water stays about **1 °C or more above the usual summer maximum** for several weeks, the zooxanthellae are damaged and the corals **expel** them. The corals turn white (**bleaching**) and lose most of their food supply. If the heat passes quickly the algae can return, but long or repeated bleaching kills the corals. Mass bleaching events have become more frequent; the fourth global event began in 2023.

### From coral loss to reef collapse

Corals are the **foundation species** of the reef: their skeletons create the three-dimensional structure that fish and invertebrates use for food, shelter and breeding. When corals die, the reef is overgrown by seaweed, erodes, and the community that depends on it collapses: a whole **ecosystem** is lost, not just one species.

<Callout type="exam">

Keep the causes separate: **CO₂ concentration → acidification → less carbonate → suppressed calcification**; **temperature → bleaching (loss of zooxanthellae)**. Write the equations with correct formulas and charges.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sequestration" title="Carbon sequestration: forests and peat" level="SL" covers="D4.3.8">

**Carbon sequestration** means removing CO₂ from the atmosphere and storing it for a long time. Biological approaches use photosynthesis to build long-lasting carbon stores: **wood** in trees and **peat** in wetlands.

| Approach | What it means | Points to evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| **Afforestation** | Planting trees where there was no recent forest | Stores carbon in wood and soil, but only suitable where forest is the natural vegetation; planting grassland, tundra or peat can destroy habitats or release soil carbon |
| **Forest regeneration (reforestation)** | Allowing or helping forest to regrow where it was cleared | Uses native species and existing seed banks, often cheaply; growth may be slower at first |
| **Restoring peat-forming wetlands** | Blocking drains, raising the water table, replanting *Sphagnum* moss | Stops CO₂ loss from drained peat quickly; new peat forms slowly; rewetting can briefly raise methane release |

### The plantation debate

Scientists actively debate the best approach:

- **Non-native plantations** (e.g. Sitka spruce in Britain, eucalyptus or pine elsewhere) grow fast and sequester carbon quickly, and produce timber. But they are usually **monocultures** with low biodiversity, are vulnerable to pests and fire, and much of their carbon returns to the air if the wood is harvested and burned.
- **Rewilding with native species** creates diverse, more resilient forests that support native wildlife and keep carbon stored for longer, though growth can be slower. Some argue that non-native species may be better suited to a warmer future climate.

### Where peat forms

Peat forms where dead plant matter accumulates faster than it decomposes: in **waterlogged** soils, which are **anaerobic** and often acidic, so saprotrophs work very slowly. This happens in bogs and fens of the **temperate and boreal** zones, where peat builds up about a millimetre a year, and **very rapidly** in some **tropical** peat swamp forests, such as in South-East Asia and the Congo Basin. Peatlands cover only about 3% of the land but store roughly 600 billion tonnes of carbon, more than all the world's forest vegetation.

<Figure name="d4-3-peat" caption="Peat stores carbon only while it stays waterlogged. Drainage lets air in, so the peat decomposes and becomes a carbon source; rewetting can restore the sink." />

</Section>

<Section id="phenology" title="Phenology: timing of biological events" level="AHL" covers="D4.3.9">

**Phenology** is the study of the **timing** of seasonal events in living organisms and of the environmental cues that control them. Being active at the right time of year is crucial: flowering when pollinators are about, or rearing young when food is most plentiful.

### Two main cues

| Cue | How it varies | Events it often controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Photoperiod** (day length) | Exactly the same each year at a given latitude; not affected by climate change | **bud set** and leaf fall in many deciduous trees (shortening days in late summer); **flowering** in photoperiod-sensitive plants; **migration** and the start of **nesting** in many birds |
| **Temperature** | Varies from year to year, and rises with climate change | **budburst** in deciduous trees (after a cold winter, warm spring days trigger it); insect emergence and development; flowering in many spring plants; fine-tuning of egg-laying date in some birds |

Many events use both cues. In some trees, for example, budburst needs a period of winter chilling, then a certain amount of spring warmth, and in beech a long enough day as well.

### Phenology as evidence of climate change

Because photoperiod does not change, any trend in the dates of temperature-cued events reflects climate. Long records show it clearly: the first leaves of an official horse-chestnut tree in Geneva have been recorded since 1808, and the typical date has moved from around early April to around March, with some years far earlier. Across hundreds of species, spring events in the Northern Hemisphere have advanced on average by about **2–3 days per decade**.

<Callout type="exam">

Name the cue and the event precisely: "budburst in oaks is triggered by **rising spring temperature**", "bud set is triggered by **shortening photoperiod**". Remember that photoperiod is the same every year, so climate change cannot shift it.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="synchrony" title="Climate change disrupts phenological synchrony" level="AHL" covers="D4.3.10">

Within an ecosystem, interacting species may respond to **different cues**. If one population uses **temperature** and another uses **photoperiod**, warming shifts one event but not the other, and species that were **synchronized** fall out of step.

### Reindeer and Arctic mouse-ear chickweed

In West Greenland, **reindeer** (caribou, *Rangifer tarandus*) migrate to their calving grounds in spring, timed largely by **photoperiod**. Calving coincides with the spring growth of food plants such as **Arctic mouse-ear chickweed** (*Cerastium arcticum*), whose growth is triggered by **temperature**. As springs have warmed, plant growth has started earlier, but the reindeer have not arrived earlier. Females therefore meet plants past their most nutritious stage when they most need food to produce milk, and fewer calves survive.

### Great tits and caterpillars

**Great tits** (*Parus major*) feed their chicks mainly on **winter moth caterpillars**, which are abundant on oak leaves for only a few weeks in spring. The caterpillar peak is driven by **temperature** (eggs hatch as oak buds open). The birds' laying date is set largely by **photoperiod**, with only some response to temperature. At **Hoge Veluwe** in the Netherlands, warming since the 1980s has advanced the caterpillar peak much more than the laying date, so in many years the chicks need most food after the caterpillar peak has passed, and selection favours females that lay earlier.

<Sim topic="d4-3" id="phenology" />

Not every population is mismatched. In **Wytham Woods**, near Oxford, great tits have advanced their laying by about two weeks over five decades through **phenotypic plasticity** (responding to spring temperature) and have largely kept pace with the caterpillars.

<Callout type="exam">

For a mismatch question, state **which species uses which cue**, **which event moves earlier with warming**, and the **consequence** (less food when young need it most, fewer offspring surviving).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="insects" title="More insect life cycles each year" level="AHL" covers="D4.3.11">

Insects are **ectotherms**: their rate of development depends on temperature. A life cycle needs a certain amount of accumulated warmth, so in warmer years each generation is completed faster, and the active season starts earlier and ends later. Some insects can therefore fit **more generations into one year**, greatly increasing their population growth rate.

### Spruce bark beetle (*Ips typographus*)

The European spruce bark beetle bores into spruce trees and breeds under the bark. Its larvae eat the phloem, cutting off the flow of sugars, and the beetles carry blue-stain fungi; mass attacks kill the tree.

<Figure name="d4-3-beetle" level="AHL" caption="Schematic timing of the spruce bark beetle. Warmth brings the spring flight forward and speeds up development, allowing a second generation in the same year." />

- Adults **overwinter** and fly to new trees in spring when the air warms to about 16–17 °C.
- One generation, from egg to adult, needs roughly **550 degree-days** above about 8 °C.
- In cool northern or upland forests this allowed only **one generation** a year. In warm summers, populations in southern Scandinavia now complete **two**, and in lowland central Europe sometimes three.

At the same time, **drought** weakens spruce trees, which produce less resin to defend themselves. More beetles attacking weaker trees caused huge outbreaks: after the hot, dry summers of 2018–2020, beetles killed spruce over vast areas of central Europe, especially in the Czech Republic and Germany. Dead forest stores less carbon, linking back to positive feedback.

The **great spruce bark beetle** (*Dendroctonus micans*) normally takes one to two years per generation; warmer conditions shorten its development too. In North America, the related spruce beetle (*Dendroctonus rufipennis*) switched from a two-year to a one-year life cycle in warm years, contributing to outbreaks in Alaska.

</Section>

<Section id="evolution" title="Evolution as a consequence of climate change" level="AHL" covers="D4.3.12">

Climate change alters the environment, so it can alter which **heritable variants** survive and reproduce best. When that changes allele frequencies in a population, it is **evolution by natural selection** (<TopicLink code="A4.1" />).

### Tawny owls (*Strix aluco*) in Finland

Tawny owls occur in two main colour variants (morphs): **grey** and **brown**. Plumage colour is strongly **heritable**, and the brown allele is **dominant** over the grey allele. Finland is near the northern edge of the species' range.

- In **cold winters with deep snow**, grey owls survive better than brown ones, possibly because they are better camouflaged against snow or cope better with cold. Snow-rich winters therefore select **against** the brown variant.
- As winters in Finland have become **milder with less snow cover**, this selection has **weakened**, so the relative fitness of brown owls has risen.
- Long-term records showed the proportion of **brown owls increasing** markedly, both in a study population followed for about 28 years and in Finland as a whole over about 48 years.

| | Snowy winters (past) | Milder winters, less snow (now) |
|---|---|---|
| Grey morph | better survival | survival similar to brown |
| Brown morph | selected against | selection relaxed; frequency rising |

This is evidence that climate change is causing a **genetic** change in a wild population, not just a change in behaviour.

<Callout type="note" title="Plasticity or evolution?">

A change in a population's traits is evolution only if allele frequencies change. Earlier laying by Wytham great tits comes mostly from **plasticity**: the same birds lay earlier in warm springs. The rise of brown tawny owls is **evolution**: brown is inherited, and its frequency in the population has increased.

</Callout>
