<Section id="vessels" title="Capillaries, arteries and veins" level="SL" covers="B3.2.1 B3.2.2">

<Viewer3D topic="b3-2" id="transport" />

Blood flows from the heart through **arteries**, which branch into narrow **arterioles**, then through **capillaries**, and back to the heart through **venules** and **veins**. Each type of vessel has a wall suited to its job.

### Capillaries: adapted for exchange

Capillaries are where materials are exchanged between the blood and the tissues (or, in the lungs and gut, with the outside world). They have three key adaptations:

- **Large surface area.** Capillaries branch and rejoin repeatedly to form dense **capillary networks**, so their total surface area is enormous and every active cell is close to one. Their narrow lumen (about 5–10 µm) means red blood cells pass in single file, close to the wall.
- **Thin, permeable wall.** The wall is a single layer of very thin **endothelial cells** on a **basement membrane**, so the diffusion distance is tiny. Small gaps (**pores**) between the cells let water and small solutes leak out to form tissue fluid, while blood cells and most plasma proteins stay in.
- **Fenestrations.** **Fenestrated capillaries** also have larger pores through the endothelial cells themselves. They are found where exchange must be especially rapid, such as the glomerulus of the kidney (filtration of blood), the villi of the small intestine (absorption) and endocrine glands (release of hormones).

### Telling arteries from veins in micrographs

| Feature | Artery | Vein |
|---|---|---|
| Wall thickness relative to lumen | thick wall, narrow lumen | thin wall, wide lumen |
| Tunica media | thick: much smooth muscle and elastic tissue | thin: little muscle or elastic tissue |
| Shape in section | usually round, holds its shape | often flattened or irregular |
| Lining | often looks folded (corrugated) | smooth |
| Valves | none (except at the exit from the heart) | pocket valves in many veins |

<Callout type="exam" title="Identifying vessels in a micrograph">

Compare the **wall thickness with the lumen diameter**. A thick, muscular wall around a small, round lumen is an artery; a thin wall around a wide, often collapsed lumen is a vein. A capillary is only about as wide as a red blood cell. When drawing transverse sections, label the **tunica intima**, **tunica media** and **tunica externa**, and the **lumen**, and draw the artery's media thicker than the vein's.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="arteries" title="Arteries withstand and maintain high pressure" level="SL" covers="B3.2.3">

Each time the ventricles contract (**systole**), a surge of blood at high pressure enters the arteries; between beats (**diastole**) the heart pumps nothing into them. Artery walls are built to cope with this.

- **Collagen fibres** in the **tunica externa** (and media) are very strong in tension, so the artery does not bulge outwards (an **aneurysm**) or burst under the highest pressures.
- **Elastic fibres** (elastin) in the **tunica media** are stretched by each surge, storing potential energy. When the ventricles relax, the fibres **recoil**, squeezing the blood onwards. This keeps arterial pressure from falling to zero between beats (it stays around 80 mmHg in large arteries) and makes the flow more continuous.
- **Smooth muscle** in the tunica media is arranged in circles. Its contraction narrows the lumen (**vasoconstriction**); relaxation widens it (**vasodilation**). Arterioles have the most muscle relative to their size, so they control how much blood each tissue receives.
- A thick wall and a relatively **narrow lumen** help to maintain high pressure; a smooth **endothelium** in the **tunica intima** reduces friction.

The stretch and recoil of the artery wall with every heartbeat is what you feel as the **pulse**. By the time blood reaches the capillaries, its pressure has fallen and the pulse has smoothed out.

<Callout type="tip">

Use the 3D model's labels to link each layer to its job: **collagen** resists bursting, **elastic fibres** stretch and recoil, **smooth muscle** changes the lumen diameter.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="pulse" title="Measuring pulse rate" level="SL" covers="B3.2.4">

There is one pulse for every beat of the heart, so the **pulse rate** equals the **heart rate**. You can feel it wherever an artery runs close to the skin over something firm:

- the **radial pulse**, on the palm side of the wrist below the thumb;
- the **carotid pulse**, in the neck in the soft groove beside the windpipe (press gently and on one side only).

### Method

1. Rest quietly for a few minutes first if you want a resting rate.
2. Place the tips of your **index and middle fingers** on the artery. Do not use your thumb, which has a pulse of its own.
3. Count the beats for a fixed time and convert to **beats per minute (bpm)**: ×4 for 15 s, ×2 for 30 s, ×1 for 60 s.
4. Repeat and calculate a mean.

A resting adult heart rate is usually about 60–100 bpm.

<Sim topic="b3-2" id="pulse" />

### Traditional versus digital methods

**Digital methods** include **pulse oximeters**, which clip on a fingertip, shine light through it and detect the change in light absorption as each pulse of blood arrives; **fitness watches**, which use a similar optical sensor; and chest-strap monitors, which detect the heart's electrical activity. They are quick, give continuous readings and avoid counting errors, but they can be thrown off by movement, cold fingers or a loose fit. Counting by hand needs no equipment but depends on the counter. A short count is quicker, but each missed or extra beat changes a 15 s result by 4 bpm.

<Callout type="exam">

Show your working: "18 beats in 15 s, so 18 × 4 = 72 beats per minute". When comparing methods, mention **precision** (a 15 s count can only give multiples of 4), **human error** in counting, and the effect of **repeats** on reliability.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="veins" title="Veins return blood to the heart" level="SL" covers="B3.2.5">

By the time blood has passed through capillaries, its pressure is low and steady: there is no pulse in veins. Veins are adapted to get this slow-moving blood back to the heart.

- **Valves.** Many veins, especially in the limbs, contain **pocket valves**: flaps of tissue attached to the wall like pockets. Blood flowing towards the heart pushes the flaps against the wall, so the valve is open. If blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which meet in the middle and close the vein. Blood can therefore only move one way, towards the heart.
- **Thin, flexible walls.** Veins have a thin tunica media with little muscle or elastic tissue. When nearby **skeletal muscles** contract, they bulge and press on the veins, squeezing them flatter and pushing blood along; the valves make sure it goes towards the heart. This is sometimes called the **skeletal muscle pump**, and it is one reason why walking helps prevent blood pooling in the legs.
- **Wide lumen.** A large lumen gives little resistance to flow, so the low pressure is enough to move a large volume of blood. At any one time most of the blood in the body is in the veins.

<Callout type="exam">

To explain how blood returns to the heart, link a structure to its effect: **valves prevent backflow**; the **thin, flexible wall** lets adjacent muscles compress the vein. A statement such as "veins have valves" with no function rarely earns the mark.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="coronary" title="Coronary artery occlusion and heart disease" level="SL" covers="B3.2.6">

Cardiac muscle works without stopping, so it needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose for aerobic respiration. This comes not from the blood inside the heart's chambers but from the **coronary arteries**, the first branches of the aorta, which run over the surface of the heart.

### Causes

In **atherosclerosis**, fatty deposits called **plaques** (**atheroma**), made of lipids including cholesterol, fibrous tissue and white blood cells, build up in the walls of arteries just beneath the endothelium.

1. The plaque narrows the **lumen** and makes the artery wall less elastic, so blood flow is reduced.
2. If the surface of a plaque ruptures, a **blood clot** (thrombus) forms there. The clot can block the artery completely: **occlusion**.

### Consequences

- A narrowed coronary artery may supply enough blood at rest but not during exercise, when cardiac muscle needs more oxygen. The result is chest pain called **angina** and shortness of breath on exertion.
- If a coronary artery is occluded, the cardiac muscle it supplies receives **no oxygen**. It cannot respire aerobically, stops contracting and begins to die: a **myocardial infarction** (heart attack). The heart may beat irregularly or stop.

Together these conditions are called **coronary heart disease (CHD)**.

<Sim topic="b3-2" id="occlusion" />

### Evaluating epidemiological data

**Epidemiology** studies the patterns and causes of disease in populations. It has identified **risk factors** for CHD, including high blood pressure, smoking, high blood cholesterol (especially LDL), obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age, being male and a family history of CHD.

A **correlation coefficient** (*r*) quantifies how strongly two variables are linearly related. It runs from −1 (perfect negative correlation) through 0 (no linear correlation) to +1 (perfect positive correlation).

<Figure name="b3-2-chd-data" caption="Invented country-level data for practice. Graph A shows a strong positive correlation (r close to +1); graph B shows almost no correlation (r close to 0)." />

- A **low** or zero correlation coefficient is evidence **against** a hypothesis that one variable affects the other.
- A **high** correlation coefficient supports a hypothesis but **does not prove causation**. For example, countries where people eat more saturated fat tend to have higher CHD rates, but those countries may also differ in smoking, exercise, wealth or other foods. These **confounding variables** could explain the pattern.

<Callout type="exam" title="Evaluating a study">

Comment on the **sample size**, whether **confounding variables** were controlled or matched (for example by comparing people of the same age), whether the data show **correlation or cause**, and how reliable the measurements of the variables are.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="transpiration" title="Water transport from roots to leaves" level="SL" covers="B3.2.7">

Plants move large volumes of water from the roots to the leaves in **xylem vessels**, with dissolved mineral ions. Most of this water is lost from the leaves by **transpiration**. Remarkably, the movement is entirely passive.

1. **Evaporation.** In the leaf, water evaporates from the moist **cell walls** of the spongy mesophyll cells into the air spaces. The water vapour then **diffuses out through the stomata** into the drier air outside (see <TopicLink code="B3.1" />).
2. **Capillary action in cell walls.** Cellulose in cell walls is hydrophilic. The water lost is replaced by water drawn through the narrow spaces between the cellulose fibres, by **adhesion** of water to cellulose and **cohesion** between water molecules. This water is pulled from the nearest xylem vessels in the leaf veins.
3. **Tension in the xylem.** Pulling water out of the xylem puts the water inside under **tension** (a negative pressure). Because of **cohesion** (hydrogen bonding between water molecules, see <TopicLink code="A1.1" />), the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled upwards as a continuous thread, from the roots to the leaves. Adhesion to the vessel walls also helps.
4. **Water enters the roots.** As water is pulled up, it is replaced by water moving across the root from the soil.

This is the **transpiration pull**, or cohesion–tension mechanism. The energy comes from the heat that causes evaporation, ultimately from the Sun; no ATP is used to move water, and the vessels themselves are dead.

<Anim topic="b3-2" id="transpiration" />

<Callout type="exam">

Use the key words in a logical chain: **evaporation** from cell walls → **capillary action** draws water through walls → **tension** (negative pressure) in the xylem → **cohesion** transmits it → **continuous column** moves up. Saying that water is "sucked up" or "pumped" loses marks.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="xylem" title="Xylem vessels are adapted for water transport" level="SL" covers="B3.2.8">

Xylem vessels develop from columns of cells arranged end to end. As they mature, the cells die and their structure is modified:

- **No cell contents.** The plasma membranes, cytoplasm and nuclei break down, leaving an empty tube. Nothing obstructs the flow, and the movement of water must be passive.
- **No end walls (or incomplete ones).** The end walls between the cells are removed, so each vessel is a long continuous tube in which flow of xylem sap is **unimpeded**.
- **Lignified walls.** The side walls are thickened and impregnated with **lignin**, a strong, rigid polymer. Under tension, water pulls inwards on the walls; lignin stops the vessels collapsing. In young xylem the thickening is laid down as rings or a spiral; in older xylem it covers most of the wall.
- **Pits.** Gaps in the lignified thickening, where only a thin layer of cellulose wall remains, let water move sideways into and out of the vessel, for example into neighbouring vessels or out to the cells of a leaf.

<Figure name="b3-2-xylem" caption="A xylem vessel cut lengthways: a continuous tube with no end walls or cell contents, lignified side walls and pits." />

Lignified xylem also gives the plant mechanical support: wood is mostly xylem.

</Section>

<Section id="stem-root" title="Tissues in dicot stems and roots" level="SL" covers="B3.2.9 B3.2.10">

A **plan diagram** shows the outlines of tissues in an organ, without drawing individual cells. You need to draw plan diagrams of transverse sections (TS) of the stem and root of a **dicotyledonous** plant from micrographs, and annotate them with the functions of the tissues.

### Stem

In a young dicot stem, the **vascular bundles** are arranged in a **ring** near the outside. In each bundle, **phloem** is on the outer side and **xylem** on the inner side, separated by a thin layer of **cambium**. Outside the ring is the **cortex**, covered by the **epidermis**; inside the ring is the **pith**.

<Figure name="b3-2-stem" caption="Plan diagram of a young dicot stem in transverse section, annotated with the functions of the tissues." />

### Root

In a dicot root the vascular tissue is in the **centre**. The **xylem** forms a star or cross shape, with groups of **phloem** between its arms. The vascular tissue is surrounded by a ring of **endodermis**, then a wide **cortex** and an outer **epidermis**, many of whose cells grow **root hairs**.

<Section id="tissue-fluid" title="Tissue fluid: formation, exchange and reuptake" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.11 B3.2.12">

Body cells are not bathed by blood. They are surrounded by **tissue fluid**, which forms from blood plasma in the capillaries.

### Release and reuptake

- **Arteriole end.** Blood arrives from the arterioles at relatively **high pressure** (roughly 35 mmHg). This forces plasma out through the pores of the capillary wall: **pressure filtration**. Water and small solutes leave; blood cells and most **plasma proteins** are too large and stay in the blood.
- **Venule end.** Resistance in the capillary reduces the blood pressure (to roughly 15 mmHg). The plasma proteins that stayed in the blood give it a higher solute concentration than the tissue fluid, which tends to draw water back in by osmosis. Once the blood pressure has fallen this low, tissue fluid can **drain back** into the capillary.

<Figure name="b3-2-tissue-fluid" level="AHL" caption="Plasma is forced out at the arteriole end of a capillary, where pressure is high, and tissue fluid drains back in at the venule end; the excess enters lymph vessels." />

### Exchange with cells

As tissue fluid flows between cells, substances are exchanged across the plasma membranes by diffusion, facilitated diffusion and active transport. Cells take up **oxygen**, **glucose**, **amino acids**, fatty acids and ions, and release **carbon dioxide**, **urea** and other waste products. The fluid that drains back into the capillaries is therefore lower in oxygen and nutrients and higher in wastes, which the blood carries to the lungs, liver and kidneys.

| | Blood plasma | Tissue fluid |
|---|---|---|
| Water, ions, glucose, amino acids | present | present at similar concentrations |
| Plasma proteins (e.g. albumin, fibrinogen) | high concentration | very little |
| Red blood cells and platelets | carried in blood | absent |
| Oxygen and nutrients | higher (arriving) | lower (being used by cells) |
| Carbon dioxide and urea | lower | higher (released by cells) |

<Callout type="note" title="A note on the model">

This is the classic model. Measurements suggest that in many tissues less fluid is reabsorbed at the venule end than it predicts, and more returns to the blood as lymph.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="lymph" title="Excess tissue fluid drains into lymph ducts" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.13">

More fluid leaves the capillaries than drains back into them, and if the excess stayed in the tissues they would swell (**oedema**). It is removed by the **lymphatic system**.

- Tissues contain **blind-ended lymph capillaries** with **thin walls** that have **gaps** between their cells, so tissue fluid (and any protein that has leaked out) can enter easily. Once inside, the fluid is called **lymph**.
- Lymph capillaries join into larger **lymph ducts** (lymph vessels), which contain **valves** that prevent backflow. Like veins, they rely on surrounding muscles squeezing them, and the valves make sure the lymph moves in one direction.
- The largest lymph ducts empty the lymph into veins near the heart (the subclavian veins), so the fluid is **returned to the blood circulation**.

In this way a few litres of fluid a day return to the blood that would otherwise accumulate in the tissues.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope">

For B3.2.13 you only need the **valves**, the **thin walls with gaps** and the **return of lymph to the blood**. The roles of lymph nodes in immunity are not required here.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="circulation" title="Single and double circulation" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.14">

In a **single circulation** blood passes through the heart once for each complete circuit of the body; in a **double circulation** it passes through twice.

### Bony fish: single circulation

The fish heart has one **atrium** and one **ventricle**. The ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood to the **gills**, where it is oxygenated, and the blood then flows straight on to the other **body organs** before returning to the heart. Passing through the gill capillaries lowers the pressure a lot, so blood flows on to the body organs at low pressure and more slowly.

### Mammals: double circulation

The mammalian heart is a **double pump** with two separate sides:

- **Pulmonary circulation**: the **right ventricle** pumps deoxygenated blood through the pulmonary artery to the **lungs**, and oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the **left atrium**. The pressure is relatively low, which protects the delicate capillaries of the alveoli.
- **Systemic circulation**: the **left ventricle** pumps oxygenated blood through the aorta at high pressure to **all the other organs**, and deoxygenated blood returns through the venae cavae to the **right atrium**.

Returning blood to the heart after the lungs lets it be pumped again at high pressure, so oxygen reaches the tissues quickly. This supports the high metabolic rate of mammals, and high pressure is needed for processes such as filtration in the kidneys (see <TopicLink code="D3.3" />).

<Section id="heart" title="The mammalian heart" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.15">

The heart is a muscular pump that delivers blood under pressure to the arteries. Switch the 3D model at the top of the page to **Heart, frontal section** to explore it and to step through the route blood takes.

| Structure | Adaptation for pumping |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac muscle** | contracts and relaxes rhythmically throughout life without fatigue; contraction is **myogenic** (it starts within the muscle, not from a nerve); branched cells are linked so impulses spread quickly (see <TopicLink code="B2.3" />) |
| **Pacemaker** (sinoatrial node) | a group of specialized muscle cells in the wall of the right atrium that initiates each heartbeat |
| **Atria** | thin-walled collecting chambers that fill with blood from the veins and pump it a short distance into the ventricles |
| **Ventricles** | thick muscular walls generate high pressure to pump blood into the arteries; the **left ventricle wall is thicker** than the right, because it pumps to the whole body rather than the nearby lungs |
| **Atrioventricular valves** | the tricuspid (right) and bicuspid or mitral (left) valves prevent backflow into the atria when the ventricles contract; tendons stop them inverting |
| **Semilunar valves** | the pulmonary and aortic valves prevent backflow from the arteries into the ventricles when the ventricles relax |
| **Septum** | separates the left and right sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix and each side pumps at its own pressure |
| **Coronary vessels** | coronary arteries carry oxygenated blood from the aorta to the cardiac muscle; coronary veins drain it back to the right atrium |

### Tracing the flow of blood

**Venae cavae** → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary semilunar valve → **pulmonary artery** → lungs → **pulmonary veins** → left atrium → bicuspid valve → left ventricle → aortic semilunar valve → **aorta**.

<Section id="cardiac-cycle" title="The cardiac cycle" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.16">

The **cardiac cycle** is the sequence of events in one heartbeat. At 75 beats per minute it lasts 60 ÷ 75 = **0.8 s**. Contraction is called **systole** and relaxation **diastole**. The two sides of the heart work at the same time; the description below follows the **left side**.

1. **Initiation.** The **sinoatrial node** (SAN, the pacemaker) sends out an electrical impulse that spreads through the walls of both atria.
2. **Atrial systole.** The atria contract, pushing blood through the open **atrioventricular valves** to finish filling the ventricles. The semilunar valves are closed.
3. **Ventricular systole.** After a short delay (the impulse passes to the ventricles through the atrioventricular node), the ventricles contract, starting from the apex (bottom) and spreading upwards, which pushes blood towards the arteries. Ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, so the **AV valves close** (the first heart sound). When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the **semilunar valves open** and blood is ejected.
4. **Diastole.** The ventricles relax. When their pressure falls below aortic pressure the **semilunar valves close** (the second heart sound). When it falls below atrial pressure the **AV valves open**, and blood that has collected in the atria flows into the ventricles. The aorta's elastic recoil keeps blood flowing.

<Anim topic="b3-2" id="cardiac-cycle" />

### Interpreting blood pressure

Blood pressure is usually measured in an artery of the upper arm and written as **systolic/diastolic**, for example **120/80 mmHg**.

- **Systolic pressure** is the **maximum** arterial pressure, reached during ventricular systole while the semilunar valves are open.
- **Diastolic pressure** is the **minimum** arterial pressure, reached at the end of diastole, just before the semilunar valves open again.

A resting reading persistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg is usually classed as high blood pressure (**hypertension**), a risk factor for coronary heart disease.

<Callout type="exam" title="Reading the pressure graph">

A valve is open only when the pressure **behind** it is higher than the pressure in **front** of it. Find where the curves **cross**: ventricle above atrium, AV valve closes; ventricle above aorta, semilunar valve opens; ventricle below aorta, semilunar valve closes; ventricle below atrium, AV valve opens. Between the first two crossings, and between the last two, both valves are closed.

</Callout>

<Section id="root-pressure" title="Root pressure" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.17">

Transpiration pull only works when water is evaporating from the leaves. Sometimes a plant needs to move water and minerals up the xylem when there is little or no transpiration:

- when the air is **very humid**, so little water evaporates;
- in **spring**, before the leaves of **deciduous** plants have opened, when xylem vessels must be refilled with sap and minerals delivered to the growing buds.

In these conditions plants generate **root pressure**:

1. Cells in the root, next to the xylem, **actively transport mineral ions** (such as K⁺ and nitrate) into the xylem vessels, using ATP from respiration.
2. The solute concentration of the xylem sap rises (its water potential falls; see <TopicLink code="D2.3" />).
3. Water follows by **osmosis** from the surrounding root cells into the xylem.
4. With water entering a confined space, a **positive pressure** builds up that pushes the sap upwards.

Root pressure is **positive**, whereas transpiration produces **tension** (negative pressure). It is usually much smaller than the tensions generated by transpiration. Its effects can be seen when sap oozes from the cut stump of a plant, or when droplets of water appear at leaf edges on a humid morning (**guttation**).

Step 6 of the transpiration animation above shows root pressure.

<Callout type="exam">

The energy for root pressure comes from **active transport of mineral ions**, not of water. Water always moves by **osmosis**.

</Callout>

<Section id="phloem" title="Phloem: sieve tubes and companion cells" level="AHL" covers="B3.2.18">

**Translocation** is the movement of carbon compounds, mainly **sucrose**, in **phloem sap** from **sources** to **sinks**.

- **Sources** release carbon compounds: photosynthesising leaves and green stems, and storage organs when their stores are being mobilised.
- **Sinks** use or store them: roots, growing shoots, developing fruits and seeds, and storage organs that are filling up.

### Sieve tube elements

Phloem sieve tubes are columns of living cells, **sieve tube elements**, joined end to end.

- **Sieve plates**: the end walls between elements are perforated by large pores, so sap flows easily from one element to the next.
- **Reduced cytoplasm and organelles**, and **no nucleus**: almost the whole cell is open to the flow of sap.
- A **plasma membrane** is kept, so the cell stays alive and can control what enters and leaves.

### Companion cells

Each sieve tube element has one or more **companion cells** beside it.

- They have a **nucleus**, which supports the sieve tube element, and **many mitochondria**, which make the ATP for **active transport** of sucrose.
- Many **plasmodesmata** (narrow cytoplasmic channels through the shared wall) connect each companion cell to its sieve tube element, so sucrose can pass between them.

<Figure name="b3-2-phloem" level="AHL" caption="Left: a sieve tube and companion cell. Right: sucrose loaded at a source raises the pressure in the sieve tube, so sap flows to a sink where sucrose is unloaded." />

### How the adaptations work together

1. **Loading at a source.** Companion cells use ATP to pump sucrose in by active transport; it passes through plasmodesmata into the sieve tube.
2. The high solute concentration draws water in from nearby xylem by **osmosis**, raising the **hydrostatic pressure** in the sieve tube.
3. **Unloading at a sink.** Sucrose is removed from the sieve tube for use or storage, water follows it out by osmosis and the pressure falls.
4. Sap flows along the **pressure gradient** from source to sink. Different sieve tubes can carry sap in different directions, but the sap in any one tube flows in only one direction at a time.

</Section>
