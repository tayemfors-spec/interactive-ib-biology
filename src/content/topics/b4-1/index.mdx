<Section id="habitat" title="Habitats" level="SL" covers="B4.1.1">

A **habitat** is the place where an organism lives. The word is used most often for a species, but you can also describe the habitat of a single organism, a **population** or a whole **community**.

A good description of a habitat answers three questions:

| Part of the description | Question it answers | Marram grass (*Ammophila arenaria*) |
|---|---|---|
| **Geographical location** | Where in the world? | Coasts of Europe and North Africa (it has also been introduced to other continents) |
| **Physical location** | Where within that area? | Mobile dunes just inland of the beach, where wind-blown sand is still building up |
| **Type of ecosystem** | What kind of ecosystem? | Coastal sand dune |

A second example is the **red mangrove** (*Rhizophora mangle*). It lives on tropical and subtropical coasts of the Americas and West Africa (geographical location), in the **intertidal zone** of sheltered shores and estuaries, which is flooded by seawater at every high tide (physical location), in a **mangrove swamp** (type of ecosystem).

### Physical and chemical conditions

A fuller description of a habitat adds the conditions found there. Marram grass grows in loose, dry sand that is low in nutrients and exposed to strong wind and salt spray. The red mangrove grows in soft, waterlogged mud that is salty and has almost no oxygen. These conditions are what the species' adaptations must cope with (next section).

### Habitat of a community

The habitat of a community is the area that the whole group of interacting species occupies. For example, the habitat of a coral reef community is warm, shallow, clear tropical sea.

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to describe a habitat, give the **geographical location**, the **physical location** within it and the **type of ecosystem**. Do not confuse habitat with **niche**: the niche (<TopicLink code="B4.2" />) is the species' whole role, of which the habitat is only one part.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="adaptations" title="Adaptations to the abiotic environment" level="SL" covers="B4.1.2">

The **environment** of an organism includes **biotic factors** (other living things) and **abiotic factors** (non-living conditions such as temperature, water, light and salinity). An **adaptation** is a heritable characteristic that suits an organism to its environment; adaptations arise by **natural selection** (<TopicLink code="A4.1" />). They are easiest to see in plants of extreme habitats.

### Marram grass on sand dunes

A coastal dune is hard to live in: sand drains quickly and holds little water, the wind is strong and dries leaves, there is salt spray, and moving sand can bury plants. **Marram grass** (*Ammophila arenaria*) is a **xerophyte** (a plant adapted to dry conditions).

- **Rolled leaves**: in dry weather **hinge cells** on the inner surface lose turgor and the leaf rolls into a tube, enclosing a pocket of humid air.
- **Stomata on the inner surface only**, sunken in grooves between ridges, so water vapour diffuses out slowly.
- **Hairs** on the ridges trap moist air and reduce air movement next to the stomata.
- **Thick waxy cuticle** on the exposed outer surface, which has no stomata.
- **Sclerenchyma** fibres keep the leaf stiff when it is short of water.
- **Rhizomes** (underground stems) grow sideways and upwards, and burial by sand stimulates new shoots to grow up through it; **long roots** reach water deep in the dune.

<Figure name="b4-1-marram" caption="Marram grass: the rolled leaf reduces transpiration, and the rhizome and root system copes with burial and dry sand." />

### Mangrove trees in tidal swamps

Mangrove mud is **waterlogged** and **anaerobic** (it contains almost no oxygen), very **salty**, soft and regularly flooded by the tide.

- The **red mangrove** (*Rhizophora mangle*) has arching **prop (stilt) roots** that anchor it in soft mud. **Lenticels** on the roots above the mud let air in, and air spaces in the roots carry oxygen to the parts buried in mud. Its roots **exclude most of the salt** as they take up water. Its seeds germinate on the tree (**vivipary**); the seedlings drop, float and root in the mud.
- The **grey mangrove** (*Avicennia marina*) has **pneumatophores**: vertical roots that grow up out of the mud from shallow cable roots and take in air at low tide. **Salt glands** in its leaves secrete excess salt.
- In both, cells hold high concentrations of solutes, so their water potential is lower than that of seawater and water still enters the roots by **osmosis**.

<Figure name="b4-1-mangrove" caption="Two mangroves, two solutions: prop roots, salt exclusion and vivipary in the red mangrove; pneumatophores and salt glands in the grey mangrove." />

<Callout type="exam">

Link each adaptation to the **abiotic problem** it solves and **how** it helps: "stomata sunken in grooves on the inner surface of the rolled leaf, so humid air is trapped around them and less water is lost by transpiration".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="abiotic-distribution" title="Abiotic variables and species distribution" level="SL" covers="B4.1.3">

The **distribution** of a species is where it is found. Each species is found only where abiotic conditions suit its adaptations. Examples of abiotic variables that limit distribution:

| Plants | Animals |
|---|---|
| **Light intensity**: shade-tolerant woodland herbs versus light-demanding grassland plants | **Temperature**: reef-building corals only in warm seas |
| **Water availability**: xerophytes such as cacti in deserts | **Water availability**: desert animals must conserve water |
| **Temperature**: no trees can grow in the tundra | **Dissolved oxygen** in water: many mayfly nymphs need well-oxygenated streams |
| **Soil pH**: heather (*Calluna vulgaris*) grows on acidic soils | **Salinity**: most freshwater fish cannot survive in seawater |
| **Soil salinity**: only salt-tolerant plants such as mangroves grow in salty mud | **pH** of water: acidified lakes lose many fish and invertebrates |
| **Mineral nutrients** in the soil | **Substrate**, e.g. sand, rock or mud on a shore |

### Adaptations give a range of tolerance

A species' adaptations work only over a certain range of each factor. Its **range of tolerance** is the range of values in which it can survive.

- In the **optimum range**, individuals grow, survive and reproduce best, so populations are largest.
- On each side is a **zone of physiological stress**, where individuals survive but grow and reproduce poorly, so they are few.
- Beyond the **limits of tolerance** are the **zones of intolerance**, where the species cannot survive at all.

A factor that is outside, or close to the limits of, a species' range of tolerance acts as a **limiting factor**, restricting where the species is found or how well it grows there. Species with a wide range of tolerance can live in many habitats; species with a narrow range are restricted to a few.

### Biotic factors matter too

Abiotic factors set where a species **could** live. Whether it actually lives there also depends on biotic factors. On rocky shores, for example, the upper limit of many seaweeds is set by drying out at low tide (abiotic), but their lower limit is often set by competition with other seaweeds (biotic).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="tolerance-transects" title="Range of tolerance and transect data" level="SL" covers="B4.1.4">

The range of tolerance of a species can be investigated by experiment (growing it at different levels of a factor) or by looking for **correlations** in the field. In the field the usual method is a **transect**: a line across a habitat along which samples are taken, chosen so that it crosses a **gradient** in an abiotic variable, for example from a woodland edge into the wood, up a sand dune system or down a rocky shore.

### Collecting the data

You need two sets of data at each sampling point:

1. **The abiotic variable.** Electronic **sensors** with **data loggers** measure light intensity, temperature, soil moisture or pH quickly and precisely, can record repeatedly or over long periods, and store the readings digitally for analysis.
2. **The distribution of the species.** In a **belt transect**, **quadrats** are placed at regular intervals and abundance is recorded as percentage cover or number of individuals. In a **line transect**, organisms touching the line are recorded at intervals.

The IB guide expects you to collect transect data yourself in a **natural** or **semi-natural** habitat: one influenced by humans but dominated by wild species, such as a grazed meadow or managed woodland.

<Sim topic="b4-1" id="tolerance" />

### Analysing the data

Plot a **scatter graph** of abundance against the abiotic variable and describe the trend. A correlation coefficient such as Pearson's *r* measures how close the points are to a straight line.

- In the worked example, meadow grass shows a **strong positive correlation** with light (r = 0.99).
- The woodland herb shows a **hump**: it is absent in full light, most abundant at about 20% light and less abundant in deep shade. Its overall r (−0.69) hides this pattern; this is what a tolerance curve looks like in field data.
- Repeat the transect (or use several) so that the pattern is not an accident of one line.

<Callout type="exam">

In data questions, state the **trend** with figures from the data, say whether the relationship is **positive, negative or hump-shaped**, and add that **correlation does not prove causation**: soil moisture, temperature and competition also change along a transect.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="coral-reefs" title="Conditions for coral reef formation" level="SL" covers="B4.1.5">

**Coral reefs** are marine ecosystems built by **reef-building (hard) corals**. A coral is a colony of small animals (polyps, which are cnidarians) that deposit a skeleton of **calcium carbonate**. Over centuries, these skeletons build up the rock of the reef. Inside the cells of the polyps live **zooxanthellae**, single-celled photosynthetic algae. The relationship is a mutualism: the zooxanthellae supply most of the coral's food as sugars from photosynthesis, and the coral provides carbon dioxide, nutrients and a protected place in the light (see <TopicLink code="C4.1" />).

Because of this partnership, reefs only form where five conditions are met:

| Factor | Approximate requirement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Water depth** | Shallow: mostly less than about 50 m, fastest growth in the top 20–30 m | Light, needed by the zooxanthellae, fades rapidly with depth |
| **Clarity** | Clear water with little suspended sediment | Turbid water blocks light, and sediment settling on polyps smothers them |
| **Temperature** | About 23–29 °C | Cooler water slows growth and calcification; water only 1–2 °C above the usual summer maximum for several weeks causes **bleaching**, when corals expel their zooxanthellae |
| **Salinity** | About 32–42 parts per thousand (normal seawater is about 35) | Fresher or saltier water causes osmotic stress, so reefs are absent near the mouths of large rivers |
| **pH** | Slightly alkaline, about 8.0–8.3 | Lower pH means fewer carbonate ions, so corals deposit calcium carbonate more slowly |

<Sim topic="b4-1" id="coral" />

These conditions explain where reefs are found: in shallow, clear, warm tropical seas, mostly between about 30° N and 30° S. They also explain why reefs are threatened. The average pH of the surface ocean has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1 since pre-industrial times, as the oceans absorb carbon dioxide (**ocean acidification**), and marine heatwaves are causing more frequent mass bleaching.

<Callout type="exam">

For each factor, give the condition **and** the reason, linking it to the zooxanthellae (light for photosynthesis) or to the calcium carbonate skeleton (pH, carbonate ions). The five factors named in the guide are **depth, pH, salinity, clarity and temperature**.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="biome-distribution" title="Temperature and rainfall determine biomes" level="SL" covers="B4.1.6">

On land, the type of ecosystem that develops depends above all on two abiotic factors: **temperature** and **precipitation** (rainfall and snow). For any combination of mean annual temperature and annual precipitation, **one natural type of ecosystem is likely to develop**. This is shown on a **biome graph** (often called a Whittaker diagram), with temperature on one axis and precipitation on the other.

<Sim topic="b4-1" id="biomes" />

### How to read the graph

- Find the temperature on the x-axis and the precipitation on the y-axis; the region where they meet is the most likely biome.
- **Temperature** sets the main sequence from left to right: tundra, taiga, temperate biomes, tropical biomes.
- **Precipitation** decides, at each temperature, whether the result is desert, grassland or forest.
- The boundaries **slope upwards** to the right. Warmer air evaporates more water, so a hot climate needs more rain than a cool one to support the same vegetation. About 100 cm of rain supports temperate forest at 10 °C, but only savanna at 25 °C.
- The graph has an **upper envelope**: very cold climates are never very wet, because cold air holds little water vapour.

### Why the graph is only a guide

The boundaries are not sharp lines. Real biomes merge into each other over wide transition zones, and other factors also matter: how rainfall is spread through the year (a long dry season favours savanna over forest), soil type, fire, grazing animals, altitude and human activity.

<Callout type="exam">

To use a biome graph, read **both** values and name the biome. To explain a boundary, refer to **water availability** (precipitation versus evaporation) or **temperature** (length of the growing season, frost).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="biomes" title="Biomes and convergent evolution" level="SL" covers="B4.1.7">

A **biome** is a group of ecosystems that resemble each other because they have similar abiotic conditions, even though they may be on different continents. Tropical rainforests in the Amazon, the Congo basin and Borneo, for example, are separate ecosystems with mostly different species, but they form one biome.

### Why distant ecosystems look alike

Similar climates exert similar **selection pressures**. Unrelated organisms in each region have therefore evolved similar adaptations: **convergent evolution**, producing **analogous** structures (<TopicLink code="A4.1" />).

- **Cacti** in American deserts and **euphorbias** in African deserts belong to unrelated families, but both have swollen, green, water-storing stems with spines and use CAM photosynthesis.
- **Kangaroo rats** (North America) and **jerboas** (Africa and Asia) are desert rodents from different families that both hop on long hind legs, live in burrows and produce very concentrated urine.

### The six biomes to know

| Biome | Temperature | Precipitation | Other features | Typical vegetation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tropical forest** | High all year, no cold season | High; in rainforest it falls every month | Light intense at the canopy, dim below | Tall evergreen trees in layers |
| **Temperate forest** | Moderate; warm summers, cold winters | Medium to high, spread through the year | Clear seasons; moderate light | Mostly deciduous broadleaved trees |
| **Taiga** (boreal forest) | Low; long cold winters, short summers | Low to medium, much as snow | Light low to medium | Evergreen conifers |
| **Grassland** | Temperate: hot summers, cold winters; savanna: warm all year | Moderate, with a dry season | Fire and grazing common | Grasses, few trees (savanna has scattered trees) |
| **Tundra** | Very low; very short summer | Low, mostly as snow | Permafrost; light low for much of the year | Low shrubs, sedges, mosses, lichens; no trees |
| **Hot desert** | Very hot days, cold nights | Very low (usually under about 25 cm a year) | High light intensity; thin soils | Sparse succulents, shrubs and short-lived annuals |

<Section id="desert-rainforest" title="Adaptations to hot deserts and tropical rainforests" level="SL" covers="B4.1.8">

### Hot deserts

**Hot deserts** get very little rain, often with long droughts. Days are very hot and sunny, but nights can be cold because clear skies let heat escape. Soils are thin and contain little organic matter.

**Saguaro cactus** (*Carnegiea gigantea*), Sonoran Desert of the USA and Mexico:

- A **pleated stem** that swells to store water after rain and shrinks during drought; the green stem carries out photosynthesis.
- **Leaves reduced to spines**, which have almost no surface for water loss, shade the stem and deter animals that would eat it for its water.
- A **thick waxy cuticle** and **CAM** photosynthesis: stomata open at night, when it is cooler and more humid, and stay closed in the heat of the day.
- **Shallow roots** spreading widely just below the surface absorb rain quickly before it evaporates; a **taproot** anchors the plant.

**Fennec fox** (*Vulpes zerda*), Sahara:

- **Very large ears** with many blood vessels radiate heat; they also detect prey moving under the sand.
- **Nocturnal**: it spends the hot day in a cool **burrow** dug in the sand.
- **Pale, sandy fur** reflects sunlight; thick fur insulates against the cold nights; **furry soles** protect the feet from hot sand.
- It can go for long periods without drinking, getting most of its water from food and producing concentrated urine.

<Figure name="b4-1-desert" caption="Hot desert adaptations: the saguaro stores and conserves water; the fennec fox avoids the heat and loses heat through its ears." />

### Tropical rainforests

**Tropical rainforests** are hot and wet all year, with no cold season and intense light at the top of the canopy. Most nutrients are held in living things, and the soils are thin and poor because heavy rain washes nutrients out (leaching).

**Yellow meranti** (*Shorea faguetiana*), Borneo and the Malay Peninsula:

- An **emergent** tree: its crown rises above the main canopy into full sunlight, so it is not shaded by neighbouring trees. One individual in Sabah, Borneo, is 100.8 m tall, the tallest tropical tree known.
- **Buttress roots** support the tall trunk in shallow soil, and shallow roots absorb nutrients from the thin topsoil.
- **Evergreen** leaves photosynthesize all year round.
- **Winged fruits** spin as they fall and drift away from the parent tree. Like many dipterocarps it fruits in **mast years**, every few years, producing so many seeds at once that seed-eaters cannot eat them all.

**Geoffroy's spider monkey** (*Ateles geoffroyi*), Central America:

- **Long arms and legs** and very **flexible shoulders** let it swing beneath branches and reach fruit at the tips.
- **Hook-like hands** with tiny thumbs hook over branches as it swings.
- A **prehensile tail** with a bare, ridged gripping pad can support its whole weight, freeing the hands for feeding.
- It eats mainly **ripe fruit**, available all year in the rainforest, and is active by day in the upper canopy.

<Figure name="b4-1-rainforest" caption="Rainforest adaptations: an emergent tree that reaches the light, and a monkey built for moving and feeding in the canopy." />
