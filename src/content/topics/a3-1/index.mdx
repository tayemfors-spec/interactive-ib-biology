<Section id="variation" title="Variation and the first idea of a species" level="SL" covers="A3.1.1 A3.1.2">

An **organism** is an individual living thing: a bacterium, a fungus, a plant, an animal. One of the defining features of life is **variation**: no two individuals are identical in all their traits. Even identical twins differ in fingerprints and in some of the mutations their cells have picked up.

Variation is not random noise. It shows **patterns**:

- members of one **species** differ from each other only slightly, for example in height or eye colour;
- members of **closely related species**, such as humans and chimpanzees, share most traits but differ in some;
- **distantly related** organisms, such as humans and oak trees, or humans and *E. coli*, differ in almost every trait.

These nested levels of similarity are the basis for **naming and classifying** organisms: groups of similar individuals are named as species, similar species are grouped together, and so on (see <TopicLink code="A3.2" />).

### Species as groups with shared traits

From the 17th century onwards naturalists used the word **species** for a group of organisms with a characteristic set of shared traits. The Swedish botanist **Carl Linnaeus** (18th century) described and named thousands of species of plants and animals in this way, by their **morphology**: their outward form and inner structure. This is the **morphological species concept**: a species is a group of organisms that resemble each other closely and are clearly different from all other groups.

The morphological concept is practical, because it only needs observation, and it is still used to describe most new species and to identify fossils. But it relies on clear-cut differences between species, and these are not always present:

- Individuals of one species can look very different: males and females (sexual dimorphism), larvae and adults, or different breeds of dog.
- Different species can look almost identical (**cryptic species**), and are only told apart by their behaviour, their songs or their DNA.

These problems led to other ways of defining a species, below.

</Section>

<Section id="binomial" title="The binomial system for naming species" level="SL" covers="A3.1.3">

Every species has many **common names**, which vary between languages and regions: the bird called a robin in Britain is a different species from the American robin. To avoid confusion, scientists give each species a single **scientific name** using the **binomial system** popularized by Linnaeus. It is agreed internationally and used by biologists everywhere, a good example of scientific cooperation.

A binomial has two parts:

1. The **genus** name comes first and starts with a **capital** letter. A genus is a group of closely related species that share many similar traits.
2. The **species** name (the specific epithet) comes second and starts with a **lower-case** letter. It distinguishes this species from the others in the genus.

The whole name is printed in *italics*, or underlined when handwritten. After the first use, the genus can be shortened to its initial: *E. coli* for *Escherichia coli*.

| Organism | Binomial | What the name shows |
|---|---|---|
| Human | *Homo sapiens* | the only living species of the genus *Homo* |
| Lion | *Panthera leo* | same genus as the tiger, *Panthera tigris*: similar traits |
| Bread wheat | *Triticum aestivum* | a plant |
| Baker's yeast | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* | a fungus |
| A gut bacterium | *Escherichia coli* | bacteria are named the same way |

Because species in the same genus are similar, a binomial carries information: *Panthera leo* and *Panthera tigris* are both big cats.

<Callout type="exam">

Marks are lost for capital letters in the wrong place. Write *Homo sapiens*: capital H, lower-case s, and underline both words when handwriting.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="species-concepts" title="The biological species concept and speciation" level="SL" covers="A3.1.4 A3.1.5">

The **biological species concept** defines a species as a group of organisms that **can interbreed and produce fertile offspring**. Interbreeding keeps mixing the genes of the group (its gene pool), so its members stay similar. A group that does not interbreed with others is free to diverge and become more and more different.

### Difficulties with the biological species concept

- **Hybrids**: some organisms that are clearly different species by morphology can still produce fertile offspring. Polar bears and grizzly bears occasionally interbreed in the wild and some of their hybrids are fertile.
- **Untestable cases**: interbreeding cannot be tested for extinct organisms known only from fossils, or for populations living far apart that never meet.
- **Organisms that do not reproduce sexually**: the concept cannot be applied to them at all (see the AHL section below).
- **Degrees of fertility**: crosses can be partly fertile, so there is no sharp yes-or-no answer.

Because of these problems, **other species definitions are also used**, for example ones based on morphology or on how closely the DNA of organisms is related (shared ancestry). Biologists have not agreed on a single definition, and the choice often depends on the group being studied.

### Populations, species and gradual speciation

A **population** is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area at the same time. **Speciation** is the splitting of one species into two or more. It usually happens when two populations stop interbreeding, for example because a mountain range or a stretch of sea separates them:

1. The populations live in different environments and change independently, so their traits gradually **diverge**.
2. Over many generations, differences in appearance, behaviour, chromosomes or breeding season build up.
3. Eventually the populations could not produce fertile offspring even if they met again: they have become separate species.

There is **no single moment** at which one species becomes two. Partway through, two populations may be partly different and partly able to interbreed, so deciding whether they are one species or two can be an **arbitrary** decision, and taxonomists often disagree. Speciation is covered fully in <TopicLink code="A4.1" />.

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to discuss the biological species concept, state the definition with the word **fertile**, then give at least two specific problems (hybrids, fossils, isolated populations, asexual reproduction) and say that other definitions exist.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="chromosome-numbers" title="Chromosome numbers differ between species" level="SL" covers="A3.1.6">

The number of chromosomes in the nucleus is a characteristic feature of a species, and it varies widely between species. Some species have a few large chromosomes; others have many small ones.

- **Humans** have **46** chromosomes in each body cell.
- **Chimpanzees** have **48**, as do our other closest relatives, gorillas and orangutans.

These are the only two numbers you need to learn. The comparison chart in the genome-size section below (switch it to **Chromosome number**) shows how wide the range is, from 2 in an Australian ant to 78 in dogs and chickens, and that the number has nothing to do with how complex an organism is.

### Why diploid numbers are even

Most plant and animal cells are **diploid**: their nucleus holds **two sets** of chromosomes, one inherited from each parent. The chromosomes therefore come in **homologous pairs** with the same genes in the same order, and the diploid number (2n) is even. Human gametes are **haploid**, with one set of 23 (n = 23); fertilization restores 46. See <TopicLink code="D2.1" /> for how meiosis halves the number.

<Callout type="note" title="Rare odd numbers">

An odd count in a body cell means something unusual: a missing or extra chromosome (for example 47 in trisomy 21, below), or a sex-chromosome system without a pair, as in the males of some insects that have one X and no Y.

</Callout>

The difference between 46 and 48 in humans and chimpanzees raises a question: what happened to one pair? The next section shows how karyograms answer it.

</Section>

<Section id="karyotypes" title="Karyotypes and karyograms" level="SL" covers="A3.1.7">

A **karyotype** is the number and type of chromosomes in a cell or organism. A **karyogram** is an image in which those chromosomes are arranged in **homologous pairs**, conventionally in order of decreasing length.

To make one, cells are grown in culture and stopped in **metaphase** of mitosis, when the chromosomes are most condensed. They are stained and photographed, and a cell in which no chromosomes overlap is chosen. The image of each chromosome is then sorted into its pair, today usually with software. Three features identify each chromosome:

- **Length**: human chromosome 1 is the longest, about five times the length of chromosome 21, the shortest. Pairs are numbered roughly by size.
- **Centromere position**: the centromere is the constriction where the sister chromatids are held together. Its position is fixed for each type: near the middle (**metacentric**), off-centre (**submetacentric**) or close to one end (**acrocentric**).
- **Banding pattern**: stains such as Giemsa produce a pattern of dark and light bands that is the same on both homologues and different for every type of chromosome.

<Sim topic="a3-1" id="karyogram" />

The 23rd pair is the **sex chromosomes**. A female has two X chromosomes; a male has one X and a much smaller **Y**. Karyograms are used to find the sex of an individual and to detect chromosome abnormalities. One of the three people in the sorter has **three copies of chromosome 21** (trisomy 21), the cause of **Down syndrome**; their karyotype is written **47,XY,+21**.

### Evidence that human chromosome 2 formed by fusion

**Hypothesis**: the common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees had 48 chromosomes, and in the human lineage two of them fused end to end to form chromosome 2. The two ancestral chromosomes correspond to chimpanzee chromosomes **2A and 2B** (numbered 12 and 13 in older karyograms).

<Figure name="a3-1-chr2-fusion" caption="Human chromosome 2 compared with chimpanzee chromosomes 2A and 2B (banding schematic). The left part of human 2 matches 2A and the right part matches 2B. Telomere repeats in the middle and a second, inactive centromere are left over from the fusion." />

The hypothesis makes predictions that could have turned out to be false:

1. **Banding**: the banding of human chromosome 2 should match two ape chromosomes placed end to end. It does.
2. **Telomeres in the middle**: chromosomes normally carry **telomeres**, repeats of the sequence TTAGGG, only at their ends. If two ends fused, telomere repeats should be found inside chromosome 2. Sequencing found them in the long arm (band 2q13), arranged head to head, exactly as a fusion of two chromosome tips would leave them.
3. **A second centromere**: each ancestral chromosome had a centromere, so human 2 should carry the remains of a second one. There is an **inactive centromere** in 2q21, in the position matching the centromere of chimpanzee 2B.

<Callout type="exam" title="Testable hypotheses (NOS)">

The fusion hypothesis is **scientific** because it is **testable**: it predicts observations (banding, telomere sequences, a vestigial centromere) that could have falsified it, and all were confirmed. A statement such as "chromosome 2 is more beautiful than the ape chromosomes" is a matter of opinion and cannot be tested by any observation. Evidence supports a hypothesis; it never proves it beyond all doubt.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="genomes" title="Unity and diversity of genomes" level="SL" covers="A3.1.8 A3.1.9">

The **genome** is all the genetic information of an organism: the complete base sequence of all its DNA. In humans it is the DNA of the nuclear chromosomes (one set of 23 holds about 3.1 billion base pairs) plus the small circular DNA of the mitochondria.

### Unity within a species

Members of a species have the **same genes, in the same order, on the same chromosomes**. This shared organization is what allows homologous chromosomes to pair up and exchange sections during meiosis without genes being lost or duplicated. Any two humans have the same base at roughly 99.9% of positions in their genomes.

### Diversity within a species: SNPs

Most of the differences that remain are **single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)**: positions where one base varies between individuals, for example an A in some people and a C in others. A variant is usually counted as a SNP when the less common base is carried by at least 1% of the population. Different versions of a gene (**alleles**) usually differ by one or a few SNPs.

| Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Person 1 | A | T | G | **C** | A | T | **G** | G |
| Person 2 | A | T | G | **T** | A | T | **G** | G |
| Person 3 | A | T | G | **C** | A | T | **A** | G |

Here positions 4 and 7 are SNPs; the rest are identical. Over 100 million SNP positions are known in humans, but a typical person differs from the human reference genome at only about 4–5 million of its 3.1 billion positions.

### Diversity between species

Genomes of different species differ in two ways:

- **Size**, the total amount of DNA, which varies enormously among eukaryotes. Genes can be duplicated or deleted, whole chromosome sets can be added (polyploidy), and the amount of non-coding DNA varies greatly.
- **Base sequence**: once two species have separated, differences build up independently in each. Human and chimpanzee genomes differ at about 1.2% of aligned bases, and distantly related species differ far more. Genes with vital functions, such as the gene for cytochrome c used in respiration, change slowly and stay recognizably similar even between very distant species.

Variation **between** species is much larger than variation **within** a species.

</Section>

<Section id="genome-size" title="Comparing genome sizes" level="SL" covers="A3.1.10">

**Genome size** is the total amount of DNA in one set of chromosomes (a haploid genome). It is given either as a number of base pairs, in megabase pairs (1 Mb = 10<sup>6</sup> bp) or gigabase pairs (1 Gb = 10<sup>9</sup> bp), or as a mass of DNA in picograms, the **C-value** (1 pg is about 978 Mb). For prokaryotes, the figure is the DNA content of one cell.

<Sim topic="a3-1" id="genomes" />

### Using a database

Genome sizes for thousands of species are held in free online databases, for example the NCBI Genome database (sequenced genomes of all groups), the Kew Plant DNA C-values Database and the Animal Genome Size Database. To compare genome size with complexity:

1. Choose species from different taxonomic groups (bacteria, fungi, plants, invertebrates, vertebrates).
2. Look up each genome size and convert to the same unit.
3. Plot the values on a **log scale**, because they span more than five orders of magnitude.
4. Compare with a measure of complexity, such as the number of cell types.

### Does genome size follow complexity?

Only very loosely. Prokaryotes have the smallest genomes, and single-celled eukaryotes such as yeast have smaller genomes than most multicellular ones. But among eukaryotes there is **no simple relationship**:

- An **onion** has about five times as much DNA as a human, and the plant *Paris japonica* almost 50 times as much.
- Among animals, the axolotl (a salamander) has about ten times as much DNA as a human and the marbled lungfish about 40 times, while the pufferfish has about one-eighth.
- Much of a large genome is **non-coding DNA**, especially repeated sequences, rather than extra genes. The human genome has only about 20,000 protein-coding genes.

<Callout type="exam">

In a data question, avoid "genome size increases with complexity". A better conclusion is: prokaryotes have smaller genomes than eukaryotes, but among eukaryotes genome size does **not** correlate closely with complexity, as some plants and amphibians have far larger genomes than humans. Quote numbers from the data.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sequencing" title="Uses of whole-genome sequencing" level="SL" covers="A3.1.11">

**Whole-genome sequencing** means working out the base sequence of an organism's entire genome. The first genome of a free-living organism, the bacterium *Haemophilus influenzae*, was sequenced in 1995. The **Human Genome Project** took 13 years (1990–2003) and cost about US$3 billion.

### Faster and cheaper

Sequencing technology has improved dramatically. Machines now read millions of DNA fragments in parallel, so a human genome can be sequenced in a day or two for a few hundred US dollars. As a result, the number of species with a sequenced genome is rising very rapidly, and millions of individual human genomes have been sequenced. The Earth BioGenome Project aims to sequence every known species of eukaryote.

### Current use: evolutionary relationships

Comparing the base sequences of whole genomes shows how closely species are related: the more differences, the longer ago they shared a common ancestor. Genome comparisons have confirmed that chimpanzees and bonobos are our closest living relatives, revealed the chromosome 2 fusion, shown that some Neanderthal DNA is present in most modern humans, and are redrawing the classification of many groups (<TopicLink code="A3.2" />).

### Potential future use: personalized medicine

If a patient's genome is known, treatment could be matched to it:

- predicting a person's **risk** of particular diseases from the alleles they carry, so that screening or prevention can start early;
- choosing **drugs and doses** that suit the person's genes. Some people carry variants of a liver enzyme gene (*CYP2C19*) that stop them activating the anti-clotting drug clopidogrel, so another drug works better for them;
- sequencing the genome of a person's **cancer cells** to choose a drug that targets the mutations driving that tumour.

Personalized medicine raises questions about the privacy of genetic data and about who can afford it.

<Callout type="exam">

Keep "current" and "future" separate, as the guide does: **current** use — research into **evolutionary relationships**; **potential future** use — **personalized medicine**. Mention the falling **cost** and rising **speed** as the reason both are possible.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="asexual-bacteria" title="Asexual species and bacteria challenge the species concept" level="AHL" covers="A3.1.12">

The biological species concept depends on **interbreeding**. Two kinds of organism do not fit it.

### Organisms that reproduce asexually

In sexual reproduction, genes from two parents are mixed every generation, which keeps the members of a species similar. An organism that reproduces only **asexually** produces offspring that are clones of their single parent. Clones never interbreed, so by the strict biological definition every clone would be a separate species, which is not useful.

- Many **dandelions** (*Taraxacum*) produce seeds without fertilization. Each line breeds true, and botanists have named hundreds of these clonal lines as "microspecies" that only experts can tell apart.
- **Bdelloid rotifers**, tiny freshwater animals, appear to have reproduced without males for millions of years, yet they have diversified into hundreds of distinct forms.

For such organisms, species have to be defined in other ways, by shared morphology or by DNA similarity.

### Bacteria and horizontal gene transfer

Normally genes pass **vertically**, from parent to offspring, so once a species has split off its genes stay separate from those of other species. Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, but they also exchange DNA by **horizontal gene transfer**:

- **conjugation**: a plasmid is copied from one cell to another through a thin tube (pilus) joining them;
- **transformation**: a cell takes up DNA released by other, dead cells;
- **transduction**: a virus carries DNA from one bacterium to another.

Horizontal transfer can happen between distantly related species. It is how **antibiotic-resistance genes** on plasmids spread from harmless bacteria into pathogens. So much DNA moves around in this way that a bacterial genome may contain genes from many different lineages, and the branches of the "tree of life" become linked like a net.

Because bacteria neither interbreed in the usual sense nor keep their genes separate, the biological species concept does not apply. Bacterial species are defined in practice by **DNA similarity**: strains whose genomes are about 95% identical or more are usually treated as one species.

</Section>

<Section id="chromosome-trait" title="Chromosome number as a shared trait of a species" level="AHL" covers="A3.1.13">

Members of a species normally all have the **same chromosome number**. This matters for reproduction, because **meiosis** depends on homologous chromosomes pairing up.

Consider a cross between two closely related species with different numbers, such as a **horse (64 chromosomes)** and a **donkey (62)**:

1. The horse's gametes carry 32 chromosomes and the donkey's carry 31, so the **mule** has 63 in its body cells.
2. Mitosis does not need pairing, so the mule develops normally and is strong and healthy.
3. In **meiosis I**, homologous chromosomes must pair. The horse and donkey sets differ in number and in how genes are arranged on them, so some chromosomes have no partner and others pair only partly.
4. The chromosomes cannot be shared out evenly into two balanced haploid sets, so the gametes are missing chromosomes or have extra ones and are not viable.

<Anim topic="a3-1" id="hybrid-meiosis" />

Mules are therefore almost always **sterile**. Crosses between related species with different chromosome numbers are generally unlikely to produce fertile offspring, which is one reason why a shared chromosome number helps keep a species reproductively isolated.

Changes in chromosome number can therefore contribute to the separation of species, because individuals whose chromosomes differ in number or arrangement often have reduced fertility when they interbreed. (Halving of chromosome number in meiosis is covered in <TopicLink code="D2.1" />.)

<Callout type="exam">

"Hybrids are sterile because they are hybrids" earns nothing. Explain through meiosis: chromosomes from the two parents are **not homologous**, so they **cannot pair** in prophase/metaphase I, and **segregation fails**, giving unbalanced, non-viable gametes.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="keys" title="Constructing a dichotomous key" level="AHL" covers="A3.1.14">

A **dichotomous key** is a tool for identifying an organism that is not immediately recognized. It is a numbered series of steps (**couplets**), each with **two** alternative statements. Each statement leads either to another numbered couplet or to the name of a species.

<Sim topic="a3-1" id="key-builder" />

### How to build your own key

Collect or photograph a group of local plants or animals: leaves of trees on the school grounds, invertebrates from a leaf-litter sample, or seaweeds from a rocky shore.

1. **List the observable features** of each species: number of legs, leaf shape, leaf margin (smooth or toothed), simple or compound leaves, opposite or alternate leaves.
2. **Choose a first feature** that divides the whole group into two smaller groups, ideally of similar size.
3. Write the first couplet as **two opposite statements** about that feature: "Leaves compound" / "Leaves simple".
4. **Repeat** for each subgroup until every group contains a single species. Write the species name at the end of the lead.
5. **Test** the key on specimens, and ask someone else to try it. If two people reach different answers, a statement is unclear.

A key for **n species needs n − 1 couplets**. Here are two couplets from a key to tree leaves:

| Couplet | Statement | Leads to |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Leaf divided into separate leaflets (compound) | 2 |
| 1b | Leaf in one piece (simple) | 3 |
| 2a | Leaflets arranged like fingers from one point | horse chestnut, *Aesculus hippocastanum* |
| 2b | Leaflets in two rows along a central stalk | ash, *Fraxinus excelsior* |

<Callout type="exam">

In an exam key, use features that can be **seen** in the drawings and are **present in every individual**: not colour, size, smell or where the organism was found. Each couplet has exactly **two** statements, and they should be opposites.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="edna" title="Identifying species from environmental DNA" level="AHL" covers="A3.1.15">

Organisms constantly shed DNA into their surroundings in skin cells, mucus, faeces, urine, pollen and gametes. This **environmental DNA (eDNA)** can be collected from samples of water, soil or even air, and identified using DNA barcodes.

A **DNA barcode** is a short section of one gene (or a few genes) that is present in all members of a large group, has a similar sequence within a species, and differs enough between species to tell them apart. Different groups use different standard barcodes: part of the mitochondrial gene **COI** for most animals, mitochondrial 12S rRNA for fish, the chloroplast genes **rbcL** and **matK** for plants.

<Figure name="a3-1-edna" level="AHL" caption="Steps in an eDNA survey. The sequencing is done on a mixture of DNA from all the species in the sample, so one water or soil sample can reveal many species at once." />

### Why it is useful

- It is **rapid**: one sample can reveal dozens of species, with no need to find, catch or identify each organism by eye.
- It detects **rare or elusive species** and those that are hard to identify, such as larvae, microorganisms or animals that only visit at night. In the UK, eDNA from pond water is used routinely to survey for the protected great crested newt.
- It is **non-invasive**: organisms are not disturbed or harmed.
- Repeated samples can track changes in **biodiversity**, the spread of **invasive species** or the success of conservation (<TopicLink code="A4.2" />).

### Limitations

- A species is only identified if its barcode is already in the **reference database**.
- eDNA breaks down in days to weeks and can be carried by water currents, so a positive result shows that a species was present recently, somewhere upstream perhaps, not that it is there now. A negative result does not prove absence.
- It usually shows **which** species are present, not how many individuals.

</Section>
