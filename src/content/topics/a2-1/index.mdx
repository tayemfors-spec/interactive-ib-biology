<Section id="early-earth" title="Conditions on early Earth" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.1">

The Earth formed about **4.5 billion years ago** (more precisely, about 4.54). Its surface was at first molten, but within a few hundred million years it had cooled enough for liquid water to collect in oceans. The atmosphere came mainly from gases released by volcanoes. Conditions were very different from today.

| Pre-biotic Earth | Earth today |
|---|---|
| Little or no free oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>) | About 21% O<sub>2</sub> |
| No **ozone layer**, because ozone (O<sub>3</sub>) is made from O<sub>2</sub> | Ozone layer in the stratosphere |
| Intense **ultraviolet (UV)** radiation reached the surface | Most UV is absorbed by ozone |
| Much higher CO<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>4</sub> concentrations | Low CO<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>4</sub> |
| Strong **greenhouse effect** kept the surface warm, even though the young Sun was about 30% dimmer | Milder greenhouse effect |
| Frequent lightning, volcanic activity and meteorite impacts | Far less of each |

### Carbon compounds without life

These conditions supplied both raw materials (CO<sub>2</sub>, CH<sub>4</sub>, N<sub>2</sub>, water and other small molecules) and plenty of **energy** (UV light, lightning, volcanic heat and hydrothermal vents). Energy breaks bonds in simple molecules and lets new ones form, so a variety of **carbon compounds** could have formed **spontaneously** by chemical processes. Dissolved in rain, these could have accumulated in seas and pools — the idea of a pre-biotic "soup" proposed by Aleksandr Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane in the 1920s.

These processes **do not happen now** for two reasons:

- Free **oxygen** in today's atmosphere would oxidize and break down such molecules.
- Any organic molecules that did form would quickly be **consumed by living organisms**, mainly microbes.

UV light was a mixed blessing: it can drive useful reactions, but it also breaks molecules apart. Places shielded from UV, such as deep water, would have helped fragile molecules to survive.

<Callout type="exam">

Explain the chain of cause and effect: **no free O<sub>2</sub> → no ozone layer → UV reaches the surface**, and **more CO<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>4</sub> → stronger greenhouse effect → higher temperatures**. Marks are usually for linking the condition to its consequence, not just listing it.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="living" title="Cells: the smallest units of self-sustaining life" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.2">

Deciding exactly what counts as "living" is harder than it seems. A useful way to think about it is that living things are **self-sustaining**:

- They keep themselves in a highly **ordered** state, which needs a continual supply of **energy** from outside (light or chemical energy). This is **metabolism**.
- They take in materials (**nutrition**), get rid of waste (**excretion**), **grow**, **respond** to their surroundings and keep internal conditions within limits (**homeostasis**).
- They **repair** damage and **reproduce**, passing on the information needed to stay organized (**heredity**), so populations can **evolve**.

Non-living things may show some of these properties — a crystal grows, a fire uses fuel and spreads — but not all of them together.

### Why the cell is the smallest unit

An individual organism is clearly alive, and so is each of its cells: a single cell can carry out all the functions of life. **Unicellular organisms** are whole organisms consisting of one cell (see <TopicLink code="A2.2" />). But the parts of a cell, such as a ribosome, a mitochondrion or a molecule of DNA, cannot sustain themselves if they are removed from the cell. So the **cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life**.

### Why viruses are considered non-living

| | Cell | Virus |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Cytoplasm enclosed by a plasma membrane | Nucleic acid in a protein coat (sometimes with an envelope); no cytoplasm |
| Metabolism | Yes: makes its own ATP and enzymes | None of its own |
| Ribosomes | Yes | No: uses the host's ribosomes |
| Reproduction | Divides by itself | Replicated only **inside a host cell**, using the host's machinery |
| Outside a host | Carries on living | An inert particle; some viruses can even be crystallized |

Viruses do have genes and they evolve, which is why some biologists argue they are on the borderline of life. But because they depend completely on a host cell for every life process, they are usually classed as **non-living**.

<Callout type="exam">

To argue that viruses are non-living, give a reason based on a **function of life** they cannot carry out independently — for example "they have no metabolism of their own" or "they cannot reproduce outside a host cell". "They are very small" is not a valid reason.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="challenge" title="The challenge of explaining how cells began" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.3">

Today, cells are only ever produced by the **division of pre-existing cells** — one of the main ideas of cell theory. Pasteur's experiments in the 1860s showed that microorganisms do not appear in sterile broth unless it is exposed to living cells, ending the old idea of spontaneous generation. Yet life has not always existed on Earth, so at some point the first cells must have arisen from non-living matter. Cells are so complex that this must have happened as a long series of small chemical steps, not in a single event.

### Four things the first cells needed

| Requirement | Why it was needed | Possible explanation (later sections) |
|---|---|---|
| **Catalysis** | Selected reactions had to happen fast enough, and in preference to others | Mineral surfaces; RNA acting as a catalyst (ribozymes) |
| **Self-replication** of molecules | Information must be copied so useful features are inherited, allowing evolution by natural selection | RNA copied by complementary base pairing |
| **Self-assembly** | Large molecules and structures had to form spontaneously from smaller units | Monomers forming without life (Miller–Urey); polymers on mineral surfaces |
| **Compartmentalization** | A boundary keeps a cell's molecules together and lets its internal chemistry differ from outside | Fatty acid vesicles; mineral micropores at hydrothermal vents |

A plausible order is: simple carbon compounds → polymers → self-replicating molecules → molecules enclosed in a membrane, giving a **protocell**.

### Testing hypotheses about the origin of life

Scientific claims must be **testable**: it must be possible, at least in principle, to find evidence that would show them to be false. Hypotheses about the origin of cells are particularly hard to test:

- The exact conditions on pre-biotic Earth are unknown, so they cannot be reproduced exactly in a laboratory.
- Protocells were tiny, soft and had no hard parts, so they are very unlikely to have fossilized.
- Almost all rocks from that time have since been destroyed by erosion or altered by heat and pressure.

Scientists therefore test individual steps — can amino acids form, can a membrane assemble, can RNA catalyse a reaction — under conditions that seem plausible. A positive result shows that a step is *possible*, not that it is what actually happened.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science">

When evaluating an origin-of-life experiment, ask how closely its conditions match what is known about early Earth. Its conclusions can be no more reliable than its assumptions.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="miller-urey" title="The Miller–Urey experiment" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.4">

In 1952 Stanley Miller, working in Harold Urey's laboratory in Chicago, tested the hypothesis that the conditions on early Earth could produce carbon compounds without life. At the time, Urey argued that the early atmosphere was strongly **reducing**: rich in hydrogen-containing gases and without free oxygen.

### The apparatus

- A flask of boiling **water** represented the oceans and supplied **water vapour**.
- A second, larger flask contained **methane, ammonia and hydrogen**, representing the atmosphere.
- **Electric sparks** between two electrodes represented **lightning**, supplying energy.
- A **condenser** cooled the gases so that water and dissolved products condensed, like rain.
- The liquid collected in a **U-shaped trap**, from which samples could be taken, and then flowed back to the boiling flask, so material circulated continuously.

<Anim topic="a2-1" id="miller-urey" />

### Results

Within a day the water turned pink, and after **a week** it was deep red-brown. Analysis by chromatography showed it contained several **amino acids**, including glycine and alanine, as well as other organic compounds. Re-analysis of Miller's stored samples in 2008, with much more sensitive methods, found more than 20 amino acids.

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| A clear, testable hypothesis, investigated with controlled conditions | The early atmosphere is now thought to have been **less reducing** — mainly CO<sub>2</sub> and N<sub>2</sub> with little CH<sub>4</sub>, NH<sub>3</sub> or H<sub>2</sub> — and such mixtures give far fewer amino acids |
| Repeatable: many later experiments, with different gases and energy sources, also produced organic molecules | Only small **monomers** formed: no polymers, self-replicating molecules or cells |
| Showed that the monomers of proteins can form from inorganic substances without living organisms | Conditions were simplified: continuous sparks, no UV, a sealed glass system and products quickly removed from the energy source |
| Supported by independent evidence: meteorites such as the Murchison meteorite contain dozens of amino acids made without life | The amino acids formed as equal mixtures of L- and D-forms, whereas proteins use only L-amino acids |

Organic molecules may still have formed on early Earth in places where conditions were locally reducing, such as volcanic plumes and hydrothermal vents, and some arrived in meteorites.

<Callout type="exam">

"Evaluate" means weigh up strengths **and** limitations, then reach a conclusion. A good answer says what the experiment showed (amino acids can form spontaneously from inorganic gases with an energy source), gives at least one strength and one limitation, and concludes that it supports the **possibility** of pre-biotic synthesis rather than proving how it happened.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="vesicles" title="Fatty acid vesicles: the first compartments" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.5">

A cell needs a boundary. Without one, the products of any useful reactions would simply diffuse away, and molecules that worked well together could not stay together.

### Fatty acids assemble themselves

A **fatty acid** has a long hydrocarbon chain, which is **hydrophobic**, with a **carboxyl group** (–COOH) at one end, which is **hydrophilic**. Molecules like this, with a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part, are **amphipathic**. In water they **coalesce** (come together) spontaneously, because this keeps the hydrocarbon chains away from water:

1. Above a critical concentration they form **micelles**: tiny spheres made of a single layer of molecules, with the carboxyl heads facing the water and the tails packed together in the centre.
2. Under suitable conditions — for example a pH close to neutral or slightly alkaline, where some carboxyl groups are ionized (–COO⁻) and some are not — they pack instead into a **bilayer**: two layers with the tails facing inwards.
3. A flat sheet of bilayer still has edges where tails touch water. The sheet curves and closes into a hollow sphere, so there are no edges left. The result is a **vesicle**: a spherical bilayer enclosing water.

No enzymes and no energy input are needed. Fatty acids can form without life — they have been found in meteorites, and laboratory simulations of hydrothermal vent conditions produce them — so vesicles could have formed in the seas and pools of pre-biotic Earth.

<Anim topic="a2-1" id="vesicles" />

### Why a compartment matters

- **Internal chemistry can differ** from outside: concentrations of molecules, and conditions such as pH, can be different inside the vesicle.
- Molecules such as RNA are **kept together**, so the products of one molecule can benefit the molecules near it, and they are passed on together when the vesicle divides.
- The protocell becomes a **unit that natural selection can act on**.

In laboratory experiments, fatty acid vesicles can grow by taking in more fatty acid molecules and divide into smaller vesicles. Their membranes are leakier than phospholipid membranes, so small molecules such as nucleotides can get in while larger RNA molecules stay trapped. Phospholipids, with two tails, form tougher, less permeable bilayers and probably replaced fatty acids later (see <TopicLink code="B2.1" />).

<Callout type="exam">

Distinguish the structures: a **micelle** is one layer with tails in the centre and **no water inside**; a **vesicle** is a **bilayer** around a water-filled space. Only a vesicle can act as a compartment.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="rna-world" title="RNA as the first genetic material" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.6">

In cells today, information and catalysis depend on each other. **DNA** holds the genes, but copying DNA and expressing its genes needs **protein enzymes**; proteins, in turn, can only be made using the information in DNA. Which came first? The **RNA world hypothesis** suggests that, before DNA and proteins, **RNA did both jobs**.

### RNA can store and copy information

RNA is a nucleic acid (see <TopicLink code="A1.2" />). Its sequence of bases (A, C, G and U) can carry genetic information. A single strand can act as a **template**: free nucleotides pair with it by **complementary base pairing** (A with U, G with C) and can be joined into a new strand, so the sequence is copied. Some viruses still use RNA rather than DNA as their genetic material.

### RNA can act as a catalyst

A single RNA strand can fold back on itself, held by base pairing between different parts of the strand, into a specific three-dimensional shape. Some folded RNAs have an active site and catalyse reactions; these are **ribozymes**. They were discovered in the early 1980s by Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman. In the laboratory, ribozymes have been evolved that can join nucleotides to copy short RNA templates, though none can yet copy itself completely.

<Figure name="a2-1-rna-world" caption="RNA can carry information in its base sequence and be copied by complementary base pairing, and a folded RNA strand can act as a catalyst (a ribozyme). In cells today DNA and proteins have taken over most of these roles, but ribosomal RNA still forms peptide bonds." />

### Clues that remain in cells today

- **Ribozymes in the ribosome** are still used to catalyse **peptide bond formation** during protein synthesis. The site where peptide bonds form in the large subunit is made of ribosomal RNA, not protein.
- Many coenzymes, including ATP, NAD and coenzyme A, contain ribonucleotide parts.
- Cells make DNA nucleotides by modifying RNA nucleotides, which fits RNA coming first.

Later, **DNA** took over information storage because it is more **stable**: deoxyribose lacks the reactive –OH group on carbon 2′, and the double helix protects the bases. **Proteins**, built from 20 different amino acids, took over most catalysis because they are more versatile catalysts. The main difficulty for the hypothesis is explaining how ribonucleotides first formed and joined, although chemists have found plausible pre-biotic routes to some of them.

<Callout type="exam">

The named example is **ribozymes in the ribosome**: rRNA catalyses peptide bond formation. Use it as evidence that RNA can be both a carrier of information and a catalyst.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="luca" title="The last universal common ancestor" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.7">

All organisms alive today — bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes — are thought to be descended from a single ancestral population, the **last universal common ancestor (LUCA)**. LUCA was already a fairly complex cell, with ribosomes, a membrane and probably DNA genes. It was **not** the first form of life.

### Evidence for a common ancestor

| Evidence | Why it points to a single common ancestor |
|---|---|
| The **universal genetic code** | Almost all organisms use the same 64 codons with the same meanings, with only minor variations (for example in mitochondria). The code is largely arbitrary, so independent origins would be very unlikely to produce the same one. |
| **Shared genes** | Genes for ribosomal RNA, ribosomal proteins, some subunits of RNA polymerase and ATP synthase, and other core proteins are found in all three domains, with recognizably similar sequences. |
| **Shared biochemistry** | All cells store genes in DNA, transcribe them into RNA and translate them on ribosomes; all use the same 20 amino acids (L-forms) and ATP as their energy carrier. |

Most of these features are arbitrary or complex, so it is much more likely that they were **inherited** from one ancestor than that they evolved separately many times. See <TopicLink code="A3.2" /> for how sequence comparisons are used to classify the three domains.

<Figure name="a2-1-luca" caption="LUCA is the most recent common ancestor of all three domains, not the first cell. Earlier and contemporary lineages left no descendants: they became extinct." />

### Other forms of life became extinct

Life may have originated more than once, or early life may have diversified into many lineages, perhaps even with different genetic codes. If so, all but one lineage became **extinct**, probably because they were **outcompeted** by LUCA and its descendants, which were better at obtaining resources. Only LUCA's line has descendants today, which is why only one genetic code survives.

<Callout type="exam">

Do not confuse LUCA with the first cell. LUCA is the **last** ancestor **shared by all** living organisms; the strongest evidence for it is the **universal genetic code** and **genes shared** by all organisms.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="dating" title="Estimating when the first cells and LUCA lived" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.8">

No one can observe the origin of life, so its date has to be estimated from indirect evidence. Each method has uncertainties, which is why published dates differ.

### 1. Fossils

**Stromatolites** are layered rock mounds built by mats of microorganisms that trap sediment and cause minerals to precipitate. Fossil stromatolites in the Pilbara region of Western Australia are about **3.4–3.5 billion years old** and are the oldest widely accepted fossils. The rocks are dated by **radiometric dating**. Claims of older stromatolites and microfossils exist but are disputed, because similar structures can form without life.

### 2. Isotope ratios

The enzymes organisms use to fix carbon take up the lighter isotope, <sup>12</sup>C, slightly faster than <sup>13</sup>C. So carbon that has passed through living things has a **low <sup>13</sup>C/<sup>12</sup>C ratio** compared with carbon in CO<sub>2</sub> or carbonate rocks. Graphite with this signature in 3.7 billion-year-old rocks from Isua, Greenland, suggests life by then. A graphite flake inside a 4.1 billion-year-old zircon from Jack Hills, Western Australia, has a similar ratio, but this single sample is disputed, as some non-living processes can also produce light carbon.

### 3. Molecular clock

The number of differences between the DNA or protein sequences of two groups increases with the time since they diverged. Calibrated with fossils of known age, the rate can be used to estimate when lineages split, and so to estimate the date of LUCA. Estimates have wide error margins; a large study published in 2024 suggested about **4.2 billion years ago**, earlier than the oldest accepted fossils.

<Sim topic="a2-1" id="timeline" />

### An immense length of time

If the whole history of the Earth were squeezed into 24 hours, the oldest accepted fossils would date from before 6 a.m., oxygen would start to build up in the atmosphere at about 11 a.m., animals with hard parts would appear after 9 p.m., and modern humans would appear about 6 seconds before midnight. Life has been evolving for most of the Earth's history.

<Callout type="exam">

Keep the methods separate. **Radiometric dating** gives the **age of the rock**; a **low <sup>13</sup>C/<sup>12</sup>C ratio** or a **fossil** in that rock is the **evidence of life**; the **molecular clock** estimates divergence times from **sequence differences**.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="vents" title="Hydrothermal vents and the evolution of LUCA" level="AHL" covers="A2.1.9">

**Hydrothermal vents** are places on the seafloor where water that has seeped into the crust and been heated comes back out, carrying dissolved minerals and reduced (unoxidized) chemicals. As this fluid meets cold seawater, minerals precipitate and build chimneys.

| | Black smokers | Alkaline vents (e.g. Lost City, Atlantic Ocean) |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature of fluid | About 350–400 °C | About 40–90 °C |
| pH of fluid | Acidic | Alkaline (about pH 9–11) |
| Key chemicals | Metal sulfides (e.g. iron sulfide) | H<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>4</sub>, made as seawater reacts with iron-rich rock |
| Chimneys | Metal sulfide | Porous carbonate minerals, full of micropores |

### Why alkaline vents suit the origin of LUCA

- **Energy and raw materials**: H<sub>2</sub> from the vent fluid can react with CO<sub>2</sub> from seawater to make organic molecules, releasing energy, without light.
- **Catalysts**: iron–sulfur (FeS) minerals in the chimney walls resemble the iron–sulfur clusters at the active sites of many ancient enzymes.
- **Compartments**: networks of tiny pores in the chimneys could concentrate molecules, acting as natural compartments before membranes evolved.
- **Gradients**: thin mineral walls separate alkaline vent fluid from more acidic ocean water, creating a natural H<sup>+</sup> gradient — similar to the H<sup>+</sup> gradients that all cells use to make ATP.
- **Protection** from UV light, deep in the ocean.

<Figure name="a2-1-vents" caption="An alkaline hydrothermal vent. Warm, alkaline, H₂-rich fluid rises through a porous chimney into CO₂-rich, more acidic ocean water. Each micropore is a natural compartment with a thin, iron–sulfur-containing wall across which there is an H⁺ gradient." />

### The evidence

- **Fossils in vent deposits**: tiny tubes and filaments of iron oxide in rocks from the Nuvvuagittuq belt in Quebec, Canada, at least 3.77 billion years old, formed in seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates. They look like the structures made by modern iron-oxidizing bacteria at vents, although whether they are truly fossils is debated.
- **Conserved genes**: a 2016 study compared millions of genes from bacteria and archaea and identified 355 families of proteins likely to have been present in LUCA. They include enzymes for living **anaerobically**, using **H<sub>2</sub>**, fixing **CO<sub>2</sub>** and N<sub>2</sub>, and an enzyme found only in organisms living at high temperatures. This fits a hot, anaerobic, H<sub>2</sub>-rich environment like a hydrothermal vent. Chemoautotrophs at vents today still obtain energy in this way (see <TopicLink code="C4.2" />).

Not all scientists agree. Some favour shallow hot springs or pools on land, where repeated drying and wetting helps monomers join into polymers. Evidence that LUCA lived at vents does not prove that life *began* there.

<Callout type="exam">

Name both kinds of evidence the guide asks for: **fossilized evidence from ancient seafloor vent precipitates** (Nuvvuagittuq, Canada) and **conserved gene sequences** from genome comparisons, which suggest LUCA was anaerobic, used H<sub>2</sub> and fixed CO<sub>2</sub>.

</Callout>
