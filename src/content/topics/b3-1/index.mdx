<Section id="exchange" title="Why organisms need gas-exchange surfaces" level="SL" covers="B3.1.1 B3.1.2">

Every organism exchanges gases with its environment. Aerobic cells take in **oxygen** for cell respiration and release **carbon dioxide**; photosynthesising cells take in CO₂ and release O₂ (see <TopicLink code="C1.2" />). This **gas exchange** happens by **diffusion**, which is passive but slow over anything more than a fraction of a millimetre.

### The problem of size

A unicellular organism such as *Amoeba* can exchange gases across its whole cell surface. As organisms get bigger this stops working, for two reasons:

- The **surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) falls** (see <TopicLink code="B2.3" />). Volume, and so the demand for O₂, rises with the cube of length, but surface area rises only with the square.
- The **distance from the centre to the outside increases**, so diffusion alone would take far too long to supply the inner cells.

| Side of cube | Surface area | Volume | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mm | 6 mm² | 1 mm³ | 6 : 1 |
| 2 mm | 24 mm² | 8 mm³ | 3 : 1 |
| 4 mm | 96 mm² | 64 mm³ | 1.5 : 1 |

Large organisms therefore have a **specialized gas-exchange surface** with a much larger area than their outer surface, such as the alveoli of mammalian lungs, the gills of fish or the spongy mesophyll of a leaf, often combined with a transport system.

### Properties of gas-exchange surfaces

Rate of diffusion depends on area, concentration gradient and distance, so efficient exchange surfaces share four properties:

1. **Permeable**: O₂ and CO₂ pass through freely. They are small and non-polar, so they diffuse through cell membranes.
2. **Thin**: often only one layer of flattened cells, giving a short diffusion distance.
3. **Moist**: gases dissolve in a film of water before they diffuse through the cells. On land this moisture is also a source of water loss.
4. **Large surface area** relative to the volume of the organism.

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to explain an adaptation, link the **structure** to the **rate of diffusion**: "the wall is one thin cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short and O₂ diffuses into the blood faster". A list of features without the link to diffusion rarely earns full marks.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="gradients" title="Keeping concentration gradients steep" level="SL" covers="B3.1.3">

Diffusion produces a **net movement** only while there is a **concentration gradient**. Left alone, gases would diffuse until the concentrations on both sides of the surface were equal, and exchange would stop. Small organisms keep their gradients simply by respiring: O₂ is used up inside and CO₂ is produced. Large animals with lungs or gills need more:

- **Dense networks of blood vessels**: capillaries cover the exchange surface, so blood is close to every part of it.
- **Continuous blood flow**: blood that has picked up O₂ and lost CO₂ is carried away at once and replaced by blood arriving from the body, which is low in O₂ and high in CO₂.
- **Ventilation**: the air or water next to the surface is renewed, keeping its O₂ concentration high and its CO₂ concentration low.

### Ventilation with air and with water

- **Lungs** of mammals are ventilated with **air** by breathing (next sections). The flow is tidal: air moves in and out through the same airways.
- **Gills** of bony fish are ventilated with **water**. Water is pumped in through the mouth, over the gill filaments and out through the gill openings, so the flow is **one-way**. Blood flows through the gill lamellae in the **opposite direction** to the water (a **countercurrent**), so the blood always meets water with a higher O₂ concentration and a gradient is kept along the whole length of the lamella. This matters because water holds far less dissolved O₂ than the same volume of air.

<Callout type="note" title="Why ventilation and blood flow work together">

Ventilation keeps the concentration of O₂ **outside** the exchange surface high; blood flow keeps the concentration **inside** low. Either on its own would soon let the gradient run down.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="lungs" title="Adaptations of the mammalian lung" level="SL" covers="B3.1.4">

<Viewer3D topic="b3-1" id="lungs" />

Air reaches the lungs through the **trachea**, which divides into two **bronchi**. Each bronchus branches again and again into narrower **bronchioles**, which end in **alveolar ducts** opening into clusters of **alveoli**. The **mammalian alveolar lung** is adapted for gas exchange in four main ways:

- **Branched network of bronchioles**: the repeated branching carries air to every part of each lung, so ventilation reaches hundreds of millions of alveoli.
- **Very large surface area**: an adult's lungs contain roughly 300–500 million alveoli, each only about 0.2–0.3 mm across, with a total surface area of around 50–100 m², dozens of times the area of the skin.
- **Extensive capillary beds**: each alveolus is covered by a dense network of capillaries, so the area of blood in contact with the alveoli is almost as large as the alveolar surface itself.
- **Surfactant**: **type II pneumocytes** secrete surfactant (phospholipids and proteins) into the film of moisture lining the alveoli. It lowers the surface tension of the water, so the moist walls do not stick together and the alveoli do not collapse when air is breathed out.

### A very short diffusion distance

The alveolar wall is mostly made of **type I pneumocytes**: extremely thin, flattened cells covering about 95 % of the alveolar surface. The capillary wall is a single layer of thin **endothelium**. Oxygen diffusing into the blood crosses only these two thin cells (sharing a basement membrane), a total of about **0.5 µm**. Red blood cells squeeze through the capillaries in single file, close to the wall. (Type I and type II pneumocytes as two cell types in one tissue are also part of <TopicLink code="B2.3" />.)

| Feature | How it speeds up gas exchange |
|---|---|
| Many alveoli | Very large total surface area |
| Type I pneumocytes and endothelium, each one thin cell | Short diffusion distance (≈ 0.5 µm) |
| Moisture film | Gases dissolve before diffusing |
| Surfactant from type II pneumocytes | Alveoli stay open, so their area stays available |
| Dense capillary network and blood flow | Steep concentration gradients maintained |

<Section id="ventilation" title="Ventilation of the lungs" level="SL" covers="B3.1.5">

Air can only enter or leave the thorax through the airways. Gases flow from **higher to lower pressure**, so to move air the body changes the **volume** of the thorax: increasing a volume lowers the pressure inside it, and decreasing it raises the pressure. The lungs follow the movements of the chest wall and diaphragm. Two pairs of **antagonistic** muscles do this work: the external and internal **intercostal muscles** between the ribs, and the **diaphragm** and the **abdominal muscles** in the front wall of the abdomen.

<Anim topic="b3-1" id="ventilation" />

| | Inspiration (breathing in) | Expiration (breathing out) |
|---|---|---|
| Intercostal muscles | External intercostals contract | Quiet: external intercostals relax. Forced: internal intercostals contract |
| Ribs | Move up and out | Move down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts, flattens and moves down | Relaxes and is pushed up into a dome (by the abdominal muscles in forced expiration) |
| Abdominal muscles | Relax, so the abdominal organs can move down | Quiet: relaxed. Forced: contract, pushing the organs and diaphragm up |
| Volume of thorax | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure in lungs | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air flow | In, until the pressure equals atmospheric | Out, until the pressure equals atmospheric |

At rest, **expiration is passive**. When the external intercostals and diaphragm relax, the lungs, which were stretched during inspiration, **recoil elastically**, and the volume of the thorax decreases without any muscle contracting. During exercise, **forced expiration** uses the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles to push out more air, quickly. How the brainstem adjusts the ventilation rate when blood CO₂ rises is part of <TopicLink code="C3.1" />.

<Callout type="exam">

Write the sequence in order, with cause and effect: **muscles contract → volume of thorax increases → pressure falls below atmospheric → air flows in**. Say which muscles contract and what they do (external intercostals move the ribs up and out; the diaphragm flattens and moves down).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="lung-volumes" title="Measuring lung volumes" level="SL" covers="B3.1.6">

A **spirometer** measures the volume of air breathed in and out. A simple one is a graduated container of air over water, connected to a mouthpiece by a tube; electronic spirometers measure the flow of air and use software to calculate volumes. A nose clip makes sure all the air passes through the mouthpiece. Each measurement is repeated (at least three times) to check that the readings are consistent.

From a spirometer trace you can find:

- **Tidal volume (TV)**: the volume breathed in or out in one normal breath. Measure from the bottom to the top of a normal breath (about 0.5 dm³ at rest).
- **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)**: the extra volume that can be breathed in by forcing inhalation after a normal breath in (about 3 dm³).
- **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)**: the extra volume that can be breathed out by forcing exhalation after a normal breath out (about 1 dm³).
- **Vital capacity (VC)**: the largest volume that can be breathed out after the deepest possible breath in: **VC = IRV + TV + ERV** (about 4–5 dm³ in a young adult; it varies with sex, height, age and fitness).
- **Ventilation rate**: breaths per minute, counted from the peaks in a known time (about 12–16 at rest).

Some air, the **residual volume** (about 1.2 dm³), always stays in the lungs and airways, so a spirometer cannot measure it. Multiplying ventilation rate by tidal volume gives the volume of air breathed per minute.

<Sim topic="b3-1" id="spirometer" />

During **exercise**, both tidal volume and ventilation rate increase. The extra tidal volume uses part of the inspiratory and expiratory reserves, so the IRV and ERV get smaller while vital capacity stays the same.

<Callout type="exam" title="Reading a trace">

Check the units on the volume axis (dm³ or cm³; 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³) and read from gridlines, not from the edge of the trace line. Vital capacity is measured from the highest to the lowest point of the **deep breath**, not from zero on the axis.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="leaves" title="Gas exchange in leaves" level="SL" covers="B3.1.7 B3.1.8">

Leaves exchange gases for photosynthesis: chloroplasts in the mesophyll need a supply of CO₂, and the O₂ they produce must be removed (at night, when respiration continues without photosynthesis, the direction is reversed). Like an alveolus, the leaf needs a large, moist surface for exchange, but on land a moist surface loses water. The **dicotyledonous leaf** balances these needs.

<Figure name="b3-1-leaf-section" caption="Transverse section through part of a dicotyledonous leaf, showing cells. Carbon dioxide diffuses in through a stoma and the air spaces to the mesophyll cells; oxygen and water vapour diffuse out the same way." />

### Adaptations for gas exchange

- **Waxy cuticle**: a waterproof layer of wax secreted by the epidermis on both surfaces. It reduces water loss, but it is also almost impermeable to gases, so gas exchange happens mainly through the stomata.
- **Epidermis**: a single layer of tightly fitting cells that protects the leaf, secretes the cuticle and contains the guard cells. Upper epidermis cells are transparent, so light reaches the palisade mesophyll.
- **Stomata and guard cells**: each **stoma** is a pore between a pair of **guard cells**, which change shape to open or close it. Stomata are open when gas exchange is needed for photosynthesis, and close at night and when the plant is short of water. In many dicots most stomata are in the lower epidermis.
- **Air spaces**: the stomata open into a network of air spaces that runs between the mesophyll cells, so CO₂ and O₂ can diffuse quickly through the inside of the leaf.
- **Spongy mesophyll**: loosely packed, rounded cells with large air spaces between them. Their **moist cell walls** provide a very large surface area where CO₂ dissolves and diffuses into the cells. Photosynthesis using CO₂ keeps its concentration in the cells low, maintaining the gradient.
- **Veins**: water evaporates from the moist mesophyll cell walls and is lost, so **xylem** in the veins brings water to replace it; **phloem** carries away the sugars made in photosynthesis (see <TopicLink code="B3.2" />).

Most photosynthesis happens in the **palisade mesophyll**, tall closely packed cells with many chloroplasts just below the upper epidermis.

### A plan diagram of the tissues

A **plan diagram** shows where the tissues are, as outlined areas, without drawing individual cells.

<Figure name="b3-1-leaf-plan" caption="Plan diagram of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf. Lines mark the boundaries between tissues; no cells are drawn. In the vein, xylem is towards the upper surface and phloem towards the lower surface." />

<Callout type="exam" title="Drawing the plan diagram">

Your plan diagram must:

- show only **tissue boundaries** as clear, continuous lines, with **no individual cells**;
- show the layers in the right order and rough proportions: **upper epidermis**, **palisade mesophyll** (a thick band), **spongy mesophyll**, **lower epidermis** (the cuticle may be shown as a line on each surface);

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="transpiration" title="Transpiration: a consequence of gas exchange" level="SL" covers="B3.1.9">

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves and stems of a plant. It is a **side effect of gas exchange**, not a process the plant carries out for its own sake:

1. Water **evaporates** from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which are usually saturated, or nearly saturated, with water vapour.
2. When the stomata are open for CO₂ to enter, **water vapour diffuses out** through them, down a concentration gradient to the drier air outside.
3. More water then evaporates from the cell walls, and is replaced by water from the xylem.

A leaf cannot take in CO₂ without opening its stomata, and it cannot have moist exchange surfaces without evaporation. Closing the stomata saves water but also stops CO₂ entering, so photosynthesis slows.

### Factors affecting the rate of transpiration

| Factor | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature ↑ | Increases | More energy for evaporation; faster diffusion of water vapour |
| Humidity ↑ | Decreases | Smaller concentration gradient of water vapour between the air spaces and the air outside |
| Wind speed ↑ | Increases | Moving air removes the layer of humid air near the stomata (very strong wind can make stomata close) |
| Light intensity ↑ | Increases | Stomata open in the light to supply CO₂ for photosynthesis |
| Shortage of soil water | Decreases | Guard cells close the stomata to prevent dehydration |

The rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot, which is close to the rate of transpiration, can be measured with a **potometer**.

<Callout type="exam">

For each factor, explain its effect with **evaporation**, the **water vapour concentration gradient** or **stomatal opening**. For example, "higher humidity reduces the concentration gradient of water vapour between the air spaces and the air, so water vapour diffuses out more slowly".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="stomatal-density" title="Measuring stomatal density" level="SL" covers="B3.1.10">

**Stomatal density** is the number of stomata per unit area of leaf surface, usually in stomata per mm² (mm⁻²). To see the stomata, use one of these methods:

- **Epidermal peel**: peel a thin strip of lower epidermis from the leaf and mount it in water.
- **Leaf cast**: paint a small area of the leaf with clear nail varnish, let it dry, lift it off with clear tape and mount it; the varnish holds an impression of the epidermis.
- **Micrograph**: photograph the surface through a microscope and count from the image.

### Calculating the density

1. Measure the **diameter of the field of view** at high power with a stage micrometer (for example 0.40 mm).
2. Calculate the **area of the field**: area = π r² = π × (0.40 ÷ 2)² = 0.126 mm².
3. Count the stomata in the field. Count those that are more than half inside the circle, and leave out the rest.
4. Repeat in several **randomly chosen** fields of view, away from veins and the leaf edge, and calculate the **mean**.
5. **Stomatal density = mean number of stomata ÷ area of field**. For a mean of 19 stomata: 19 ÷ 0.126 = 151 stomata per mm².

<Sim topic="b3-1" id="stomata" />

### Reliability (nature of science)

Biological material is **variable**: the number of stomata differs from one field of view to the next, between leaves and between plants. A single count can give a misleading result, so **repeated counts** are needed. The mean of several counts is a more **reliable** estimate, and the spread of the counts (the range or standard deviation) shows how variable the leaf is. Repeating the whole procedure on other leaves (replicate trials) shows whether the result is typical of the plant.

<Callout type="exam">

Show every step of the calculation with units: the radius (half the diameter), the area in mm², the mean count, then the density in mm⁻². The most common error is using the diameter instead of the radius in π r².

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="haemoglobin" title="Foetal and adult haemoglobin" level="AHL" covers="B3.1.11">

Oxygen is carried in the blood bound to **haemoglobin** in red blood cells. Haemoglobin is a protein with **quaternary structure** (see <TopicLink code="B1.2" />): four polypeptide chains, each with a **haem group** containing an iron ion (Fe²⁺). Each haem binds **one O₂ molecule** reversibly, so one haemoglobin molecule can carry up to **four** O₂.

### Cooperative binding

Binding of O₂ by the four haem groups is **cooperative**:

- Binding of the first O₂ is relatively difficult. When it binds, it causes a **conformational change** in the whole molecule that **increases the affinity** of the remaining haem groups for O₂, so the second, third and fourth O₂ bind more easily.
- In the same way, when one O₂ is released the affinity of the others falls, so they are released more readily.

This is why haemoglobin is mostly either fully loaded or nearly empty, and why its dissociation curve is S-shaped (next section).

### Allosteric binding of carbon dioxide

CO₂ can bind to haemoglobin, but not at the haem groups. It binds to the **globin polypeptides** at other sites, which is **allosteric** binding, forming **carbaminohaemoglobin**. This changes the shape of the protein and **reduces its affinity for O₂**. In respiring tissues, where the CO₂ concentration is high, CO₂ binding therefore promotes release of O₂. In the lungs, CO₂ leaves the haemoglobin and its affinity for O₂ rises again.

### Foetal haemoglobin

Before birth, a foetus gets its oxygen from its mother's blood in the **placenta**; the two bloods do not mix. **Foetal haemoglobin** (two α and two γ chains) differs from **adult haemoglobin** (two α and two β chains), and it has a **higher affinity for oxygen**. At the partial pressures of oxygen in the placenta, foetal haemoglobin is more saturated than the mother's haemoglobin, so O₂ dissociates from maternal haemoglobin, diffuses across the placenta and binds to foetal haemoglobin. After birth, foetal haemoglobin is gradually replaced by adult haemoglobin over the first months of life.

<Callout type="note" title="Why does foetal haemoglobin have a higher affinity?">

The γ chains bind a regulatory molecule in red blood cells (2,3-bisphosphoglycerate) less strongly than β chains do, and this molecule reduces oxygen affinity. You do not need this detail for the exam, but it explains the difference.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="dissociation" title="Dissociation curves and the Bohr shift" level="AHL" covers="B3.1.12 B3.1.13">

An **oxygen dissociation curve** shows the **percentage saturation** of haemoglobin with oxygen (y-axis) at different **partial pressures of oxygen**, pO₂, measured in kilopascals (kPa) (x-axis). It shows the **affinity** of haemoglobin for oxygen: a curve further to the **left** means a higher affinity.

<Sim topic="b3-1" id="dissociation" />

### Why the curve is S-shaped

The adult haemoglobin curve is **sigmoid** because of **cooperative binding**:

- At **low pO₂** the curve is shallow: binding the first O₂ to each molecule is difficult.
- In the **middle** (about 2–6 kPa) it is very steep: once one O₂ has bound, the affinity of the other haem groups rises, so saturation increases sharply with only a small rise in pO₂.
- At **high pO₂** it levels off as almost all haem groups are occupied.

The steep part matches the pO₂ of respiring tissues. In the lungs, where alveolar pO₂ is about 13 kPa, haemoglobin is about 97–98 % saturated. In resting tissues (about 5 kPa) it falls to about 75 %, and in actively respiring muscle (2–3 kPa) to well under 50 %. A small fall in pO₂ therefore releases a large amount of oxygen exactly where it is needed. The pO₂ at which haemoglobin is 50 % saturated (P50) is about 3.5 kPa for adult haemoglobin.

### The Bohr shift

In actively respiring tissues the **CO₂ concentration is higher**. This **reduces the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen**, in two ways:

1. CO₂ reacts with water in red blood cells (catalysed by carbonic anhydrase) to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻. The **lower pH** changes the shape of haemoglobin and reduces its affinity for O₂.
2. CO₂ binds **allosterically** to the globin chains, forming carbaminohaemoglobin, which has a lower affinity for O₂.

The curve moves to the **right**: this is the **Bohr shift**. At the same pO₂, haemoglobin is less saturated, so **more O₂ is released**. The more actively a tissue respires, the more CO₂ it produces and the more O₂ it receives. In the lungs the CO₂ concentration is low, so the curve moves back to the left and haemoglobin loads fully; because the curve is flat at high pO₂, loading is hardly affected.

### Foetal haemoglobin and myoglobin

The **foetal** curve lies to the **left** of the adult curve, showing its higher affinity. **Myoglobin**, an oxygen-storing protein in muscle with only one haem group, cannot bind cooperatively, so its curve is a hyperbola far to the left. It releases its O₂ only when the pO₂ in muscle falls very low, during intense exercise.

<Callout type="exam" title="Interpreting dissociation curves">

- Read saturation at **two** partial pressures (lungs and tissue) and subtract to find how much O₂ is released.
- **Left** = higher affinity (foetal haemoglobin, myoglobin, low CO₂); **right** = lower affinity (more CO₂, lower pH).
- Explain the S-shape with **cooperative binding** and **conformational change**, not just "it is S-shaped".
