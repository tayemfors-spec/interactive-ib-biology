<Section id="genetic-material" title="DNA is the genetic material of life" level="SL" covers="A1.2.1">

Every living organism, from bacteria to whales and oak trees, stores its genetic information in **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid). Genes are sections of DNA that carry the instructions a cell uses to build and run itself, and DNA is copied and passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce. This shared use of DNA is one of the clearest examples of the **unity of life**.

DNA and **RNA** (ribonucleic acid) are the two types of **nucleic acid**. Both are polymers of nucleotides, and both can store information as a sequence of bases.

### What about viruses?

Some viruses use RNA rather than DNA as their genetic material. These **RNA viruses** include coronaviruses (such as SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19), influenza viruses and HIV. Viruses are not usually considered to be living, however:

- they are not made of cells;
- they cannot carry out metabolism or reproduce on their own, only inside a host cell, using the host's ribosomes and enzymes.

So the statement "DNA is the genetic material of all living organisms" still holds. Other viruses, such as the T2 bacteriophage used by Hershey and Chase (see below), use DNA.

<Callout type="exam">

If a question asks about the genetic material of "living organisms", the answer is DNA. Mention RNA only as the genetic material of **some viruses**, and add that viruses are not considered living.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="nucleotides" title="Nucleotides and the sugar–phosphate backbone" level="SL" covers="A1.2.2 A1.2.3 A1.2.4">

<Viewer3D topic="a1-2" id="dna" />

### Parts of a nucleotide

A **nucleotide** has three parts:

- a **phosphate** group, which is negatively charged and makes nucleic acids acidic;
- a **pentose sugar**, with five carbon atoms, four of which are in a ring with an oxygen atom — **deoxyribose** in DNA and **ribose** in RNA;
- a **nitrogenous base**, a molecule containing nitrogen with one or two rings.

The carbons of the sugar are numbered 1′ to 5′ ("one prime" to "five prime"). The base is attached to **carbon 1′** and the phosphate to **carbon 5′**, which projects from the ring. In simple diagrams the phosphate is drawn as a **circle**, the sugar as a **pentagon** and the base as a **rectangle**.

### The sugar–phosphate backbone

Nucleotides link when the phosphate on carbon 5′ of one nucleotide bonds covalently to carbon 3′ of the sugar of the adjacent nucleotide. Repeating this gives a chain in which sugar and phosphate alternate: the **sugar–phosphate backbone**. Every link is a strong **covalent bond**, so the backbone is a continuous chain of atoms. This makes each strand stable, so the information it carries is not easily lost, and a nucleotide can always be added to the free end, so a strand can have any length.

### The bases

There are four bases in each nucleic acid:

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Adenine (A) | ✓ | ✓ |
| Guanine (G) | ✓ | ✓ |
| Cytosine (C) | ✓ | ✓ |
| Thymine (T) | ✓ | — |
| Uracil (U) | — | ✓ |

The bases project from the backbone, and the order in which they occur, the **base sequence**, is what carries genetic information, just as the order of letters carries the meaning of a sentence.

<Callout type="exam" title="Drawing a nucleotide: must include">

- a **circle** (phosphate) joined by a line to the carbon that sticks out of the pentagon (**C5′**);
- a **pentagon** (pentose), drawn with its ring oxygen at the top;
- a **rectangle** (base) joined to the carbon to the right of the ring oxygen (**C1′**), on the opposite side from the phosphate;
- labels: phosphate, pentose sugar (deoxyribose or ribose), base.

<Section id="rna" title="RNA: a polymer built by condensation" level="SL" covers="A1.2.5">

RNA is a **polymer** made from nucleotide **monomers**. Each RNA nucleotide contains ribose, a phosphate and one of the bases A, U, G or C.

### Condensation

Nucleotides are joined by **condensation** reactions:

1. The phosphate group of a free nucleotide approaches the **–OH on carbon 3′** of the last nucleotide in the chain.
2. A covalent bond forms between them, linking the two sugars through the phosphate.
3. The atoms of a molecule of **water** are removed, so this is a condensation reaction. (Strictly, in cells each incoming nucleotide carries two extra phosphate groups, as ATP does; these are split off as the bond forms and supply the energy for it.)

Repeating this adds one nucleotide at a time, building a single strand with a sugar–phosphate backbone and bases projecting to one side. The enzyme that does this during transcription is **RNA polymerase** (see <TopicLink code="D1.2" />).

### Drawing an RNA polymer

A short RNA strand is drawn as a row of pentagons linked by circles: each circle joins carbon 3′ of one pentagon to carbon 5′ of the next. Every pentagon carries a rectangle labelled A, U, G or C, all on the same side, and there is no second strand. Breaking the bonds between nucleotides needs water: this is **hydrolysis**, the reverse of condensation.

</Callout>

<Section id="double-helix" title="DNA: a double helix of antiparallel strands" level="SL" covers="A1.2.6">

DNA usually consists of **two strands** of nucleotides. The bases of one strand form **hydrogen bonds** with the bases of the other, linking the strands like the rungs of a ladder, with the two sugar–phosphate backbones as the sides.

### Complementary base pairs

Only two combinations fit and form hydrogen bonds properly:

- **adenine (A) with thymine (T)**;
- **guanine (G) with cytosine (C)**.

These are **complementary base pairs**. (A–T pairs are held by two hydrogen bonds and G–C pairs by three, as the 3D model shows, but you do not need to memorize these numbers.)

### Antiparallel strands

The two strands run in **opposite directions**: they are **antiparallel**. In a diagram this means one strand is drawn the right way up and the other upside down, so at each end of the molecule the end of one strand faces the opposite kind of end on the other. (These are called the 5′ and 3′ ends; see the higher level section on directionality.)

### The double helix

The two strands are wound round each other to form a **double helix**, the overall shape of a DNA molecule. The backbones spiral round the outside and the base pairs are stacked inside, protected from the surrounding water. The twisted backbones leave two spiral grooves of different widths, the **major groove** and **minor groove**.

<Callout type="exam" title="Drawing DNA: must include">

- two strands, each with alternating circles (phosphate) and pentagons (deoxyribose);
- the strands **antiparallel**: one drawn upside down, with 5′ and 3′ ends labelled;
- bases as rectangles paired **A–T** and **G–C**, with the hydrogen bonds shown as dashed lines and labelled;
- labels: sugar–phosphate backbone, base pair, hydrogen bond.

You do **not** need to draw the helix. Panel 3 of the **Exam diagram** shows a suitable drawing.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="dna-vs-rna" title="Comparing DNA and RNA" level="SL" covers="A1.2.7">

DNA and RNA are built in the same way, but differ in three ways.

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Number of strands | Usually two (double helix) | Usually one |
| Pentose sugar | **Deoxyribose** | **Ribose** |
| Bases | A, T, G, C | A, **U**, G, C |

### Ribose and deoxyribose

Both sugars have five carbon atoms, four of them in a five-membered ring with one oxygen atom. The difference is on **carbon 2′**:

- **ribose** has an **–OH** group on carbon 2′;
- **deoxyribose** has just an **–H** there. "Deoxy" means one oxygen atom fewer.

The extra –OH makes RNA more reactive and less stable than DNA, which suits RNA's short-term roles and DNA's role as a long-term store.

<Figure name="a1-2-ribose" caption="Ribose (left) and deoxyribose (right). Both have an oxygen in the ring and –OH on carbon 3′; only ribose has –OH on carbon 2′." />

### Examples of nucleic acids

- **DNA**: chromosomes in the nucleus; also the circular DNA of prokaryotes, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
- **Messenger RNA (mRNA)**: a copy of a gene's base sequence, carried to ribosomes.
- **Transfer RNA (tRNA)**: carries amino acids to the ribosome and pairs with codons.
- **Ribosomal RNA (rRNA)**: part of the structure of ribosomes, where it catalyses peptide bond formation.
- **Viral genomes**: DNA in some viruses, RNA in others.

</Section>

<Section id="complementarity" title="Complementary pairing: copying and expressing information" level="SL" covers="A1.2.8">

Complementary base pairing depends on **hydrogen bonding**. The bases A and T (and G and C) have hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors arranged so that they line up with each other, while other combinations do not. Hydrogen bonds are individually weak, so strands can be separated and re-paired without breaking the covalent backbones.

Pairing lets the sequence of one strand specify the sequence of another, which is used in three processes.

### Replication

The two strands of DNA separate, and each acts as a **template**. Free nucleotides pair with the exposed bases (A with T, G with C), so each new strand is complementary to its template, and two identical copies of the DNA are produced. This lets a cell pass its genetic information accurately to its daughter cells (see <TopicLink code="D1.1" />).

### Transcription

A section of DNA is unzipped and one strand is used as a template for **RNA**. RNA nucleotides pair with the DNA bases (A with T, U with A, G with C, C with G), so the mRNA carries a copy of the gene's base sequence.

### Translation

At a ribosome, each three-base **codon** on mRNA pairs with the complementary **anticodon** of a tRNA molecule, which carries a specific amino acid. In this way the base sequence decides the order of amino acids in a polypeptide (see <TopicLink code="D1.2" />).

<Section id="information" title="Storing information: sequence diversity and a universal code" level="SL" covers="A1.2.9 A1.2.10">

### A limitless store

Any base can follow any other along a DNA strand: there are no rules restricting the sequence, and pairing only fixes the partner strand. A strand of *n* nucleotides can therefore have **4<sup>n</sup>** different sequences:

| Length (bases) | Possible sequences |
|---|---|
| 1 | 4 |
| 3 | 64 |
| 10 | 1 048 576 (over a million) |
| 100 | about 1.6 × 10<sup>60</sup> |

A typical gene is over a thousand bases long and the human genome has about 3 billion base pairs, so the number of possible sequences is effectively **limitless**. DNA molecules can also be any length. This diversity of base sequences is why DNA can store all the information needed to build any organism.

DNA stores this information with great **economy**: a diploid human cell holds about 6.4 billion base pairs, and at about 0.34 nm per base pair that is roughly 2 m of DNA (6.4 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 0.34 nm ≈ 2.2 m), yet it is packed into a nucleus only about 6 µm across. Experiments storing computer files in synthetic DNA have reached densities of about 200 million gigabytes per gram, far beyond any electronic medium.

### A code shared by all life

Information in DNA is read in triplets (codons). The **genetic code** is the set of rules linking each of the 64 codons to one of the 20 amino acids or to "stop". There is no chemical reason why a particular codon must code for a particular amino acid, so a huge number of different codes would be possible. Yet almost all organisms use **the same code**, with only a few minor variations (for example in mitochondria).

The best explanation is that the code was established in a **common ancestor** of all living things and has been **conserved** ever since, because any change to it would alter many proteins at once and would almost always be harmful. The universality of the code is therefore strong evidence for **universal common ancestry**. It also explains why a human gene can be expressed in a bacterium in genetic engineering.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="directionality" title="Directionality: 5′ and 3′ ends" level="AHL" covers="A1.2.11">

The backbone of each strand has a direction, because each sugar is linked to its neighbours through two different carbons: carbon 3′ on one side and carbon 5′ on the other. Every phosphate in the backbone joins carbon 3′ of one sugar to carbon 5′ of the next, a **5′→3′ linkage**.

- The **5′ end** of a strand has a phosphate group attached to carbon 5′ of the last sugar.
- The **3′ end** has a free **–OH** on carbon 3′ of the last sugar.

In a double helix the strands are **antiparallel**, so each end of the molecule has the 5′ end of one strand and the 3′ end of the other. In the 3D model, strand 1 runs 5′→3′ from left to right and strand 2 runs 5′→3′ from right to left.

### Why direction matters

Nucleotides can only be added to the free –OH at the **3′ end** of a chain: the phosphate of the incoming nucleotide bonds to carbon 3′. Strands therefore always grow in the **5′→3′ direction**. This has consequences for each process:

- **Replication**: DNA polymerase adds nucleotides only to the 3′ end of a new strand, and the new strand is antiparallel to its template. One new strand can be made continuously, but the other has to be made in short fragments (see <TopicLink code="D1.1" />).
- **Transcription**: RNA polymerase builds mRNA 5′→3′, moving along the template strand from its 3′ end towards its 5′ end.
- **Translation**: ribosomes read mRNA from its 5′ end towards its 3′ end, so the start codon is near the 5′ end, and codons are read in order.

<Callout type="exam">

When writing a complementary strand, label both ends: a template written 5′-ATGC-3′ pairs with 3′-TACG-5′. Written the usual way, 5′→3′, the complementary strand is 5′-GCAT-3′.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="purine-pyrimidine" title="Purine–pyrimidine pairs and helix stability" level="AHL" covers="A1.2.12">

The four DNA bases fall into two chemical groups:

- **purines**, adenine and guanine, have **two rings** (a six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring);
- **pyrimidines**, cytosine and thymine (and uracil in RNA), have **one ring**.

Each base pair in DNA consists of **one purine and one pyrimidine**: A (purine) with T (pyrimidine), and G (purine) with C (pyrimidine). Two purines together would be too wide to fit between the backbones, and two pyrimidines would be too narrow to reach each other and form hydrogen bonds.

As a result, **A–T and C–G pairs are the same length**. The distance between the two sugar–phosphate backbones is the same at every point, so the double helix keeps **the same shape and width along its whole length, regardless of which bases are present**. This matters because:

- the helix is **regular and stable**, with base pairs stacked neatly on top of each other;
- **any sequence** of bases can be accommodated, so the structure places no limit on the information that can be stored;
- enzymes such as DNA polymerase can work on any part of any DNA molecule.

In the 3D model, purines are drawn as a pentagon fused to a hexagon and pyrimidines as a hexagon: every rung is the same length, but in some the double-ring base is on one strand and in others on the other.

</Section>

<Section id="nucleosomes" title="Nucleosomes: packaging DNA" level="AHL" covers="A1.2.13">

The DNA molecule in a human chromosome can be several centimetres long, yet it must fit into a nucleus only a few micrometres across and still be accessible for replication and transcription. In eukaryotes, DNA is packaged with proteins called **histones** into **nucleosomes**.

### Structure

- A **core of eight histone proteins**, the **histone octamer**, made of two copies each of four histones (H2A, H2B, H3 and H4).
- **DNA wound round the core**: about 147 base pairs of double helix, in about 1.65 turns.
- An additional histone, **H1**, attached to the **linker DNA** where it enters and leaves the nucleosome, holding the DNA in place.
- **Linker DNA**, of variable length, connecting each nucleosome to the next.

In electron micrographs, chromatin that has been spread out looks like **beads on a string**: the beads are nucleosomes and the string is linker DNA. Nucleosomes can be packed together into thicker fibres and further coiled during cell division, when chromosomes condense.

### How the DNA and proteins associate

Histones contain many **positively charged** amino acids (lysine and arginine). DNA is **negatively charged** because of its phosphate groups. The attraction between these opposite charges holds the DNA on the octamer. Because the contacts are with the backbone, not the bases, histones can bind DNA of **any sequence**. Flexible **histone tails** project out from the core; chemical changes to them (such as acetylation) loosen or tighten packing and so help regulate gene expression (see <TopicLink code="D2.2" />).

Things to look for: the DNA forms a left-handed coil of about 1.65 turns round the outside of the octamer; positively charged lysine and arginine side chains point towards the DNA phosphates; the minor groove faces the histones about every 10 base pairs; and histone tails pass between the turns of DNA.

</Callout>

<Section id="hershey-chase" title="Hershey–Chase: DNA is the genetic material" level="AHL" covers="A1.2.14">

In the early 1950s it was still unclear whether genes were made of protein or DNA. Proteins seemed a good candidate, because there are 20 amino acids and so far more possible sequences than with four bases. In 1952, **Alfred Hershey** and **Martha Chase** tested this using **T2 bacteriophage**, a virus that infects the bacterium *E. coli*. T2 consists only of DNA inside a protein coat, so its genes must be made of one or the other.

### The key idea: label each molecule differently

- **Protein** contains sulfur (in the amino acids cysteine and methionine) but almost no phosphorus.
- **DNA** contains phosphorus (in its phosphate groups) but no sulfur.

So one batch of phage was grown with radioactive sulfur, **³⁵S**, to label the protein coats, and another with radioactive phosphorus, **³²P**, to label the DNA.

<Anim topic="a1-2" id="hershey-chase" />

### Results and conclusion

| Label | Labels | Mostly found in | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ³⁵S | Protein coat | **Supernatant** (about 80%) | Protein stayed outside the bacteria |
| ³²P | DNA | **Pellet** (roughly 70%) | DNA entered the bacteria |

The bacteria in the pellet went on to produce new phages, so whatever entered them carried the genetic information: that was **DNA**. In addition, some ³²P, but almost no ³⁵S, was found in the next generation of phages. The small amount of ³⁵S in the pellet came from coats that were not shaken off.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: technology opens up new experiments">

Isotopes of an element behave the same way chemically, but radioisotopes can be detected by the radiation they emit. Once radioisotopes such as ³²P and ³⁵S became available to biologists as research tools in the 1940s, it became possible to follow specific molecules through an experiment. Without them, the Hershey–Chase experiment could not have been done.

</Callout>

</Callout>

<Section id="chargaff" title="Chargaff's data and the tetranucleotide hypothesis" level="AHL" covers="A1.2.15">

In the early twentieth century, the **tetranucleotide hypothesis** (proposed by Phoebus Levene) suggested that DNA was a simple repeating chain of the four nucleotides, such as ...ATGC ATGC ATGC.... If so, DNA would contain **equal amounts (25%) of each base** and could not carry varied information, which was one reason why many scientists thought genes were made of protein.

### What Chargaff found

From the late 1940s **Erwin Chargaff** measured the proportions of the bases in DNA from many species and tissues. He found that:

- the amounts of the four bases are **not equal**;
- the proportions are the same in different tissues of one species but **vary between species**;
- in every species, **A = T** and **G = C** (within experimental error), so **purines = pyrimidines** (A + G = T + C).

<Sim topic="a1-2" id="chargaff" />

The equalities A = T and G = C were explained in 1953 by Watson and Crick's model: every A pairs with a T and every G with a C, a purine always pairing with a pyrimidine. The single-stranded DNA of the virus φX174 does not follow the rule, because its bases are not paired.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: falsification and the problem of induction">

No number of observations that agree with a hypothesis can prove it true, because the next observation might disagree: this is the **problem of induction**. But a single reliable result that contradicts a prediction is enough to show the hypothesis is false. The tetranucleotide hypothesis predicted 25% of each base; Chargaff's data showed unequal amounts, so the hypothesis was **falsified** with certainty. It opened the way to the idea that base sequences vary and so could carry genetic information.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

In data questions, calculate the ratios: if A ÷ T and G ÷ C are both close to 1, the DNA is double-stranded with complementary pairing. If you know one base percentage, you can work out the others: for example, if A = 30%, then T = 30% and G = C = 20%.

</Callout>
