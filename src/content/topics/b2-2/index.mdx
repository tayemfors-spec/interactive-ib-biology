<Section id="organelles" title="What counts as an organelle" level="SL" covers="B2.2.1">

An **organelle** is a **discrete** subunit of a cell (a separate, distinct structure) that is adapted, by its structure, to carry out one or more **specific functions**. Many organelles are bounded by membranes, but a membrane is not required: ribosomes have none.

| | Structure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Organelle | **Nucleus** | Discrete, enclosed by the nuclear envelope; holds the chromosomes and is where transcription happens |
| Organelle | Mitochondria, chloroplasts, ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles | Discrete compartments, each bounded by membrane and with its own functions |
| Organelle | **Vesicles** | Small, separate sacs of membrane that carry materials |
| Organelle | **Ribosomes** | Discrete particles of RNA and protein, adapted for translation |
| Organelle | **Plasma membrane** | A discrete structure with specific functions, such as controlling what enters and leaves |
| Not an organelle | **Cell wall** | Lies outside the plasma membrane, so it is extracellular |
| Not an organelle | **Cytoskeleton** | A network running through the whole cytoplasm, not a discrete unit |
| Not an organelle | **Cytoplasm** | The whole region inside the plasma membrane, with many functions rather than one specific one; organelles are found within it |

### How organelles were studied: cell fractionation

To find out what each organelle does, biologists needed to collect large numbers of one type, separate from the rest. This became possible with **cell fractionation**.

1. Tissue is cut up in a solution that is **cold** (slowing enzymes that would digest the organelles), **isotonic** (so organelles do not gain or lose water by osmosis and burst or shrivel) and **buffered** (keeping the pH constant so proteins are not denatured).
2. The tissue is **homogenized** (broken up in a blender or homogenizer), which bursts the cells and releases their organelles. The homogenate is filtered to remove debris.
3. **Differential centrifugation**: the homogenate is spun, and the sediment (pellet) collected; the liquid above it (supernatant) is spun again, faster and for longer, and so on.

| Typical spin | Main contents of the pellet |
|---|---|
| ≈ 1,000 × g for 10 min | Nuclei |
| ≈ 20,000 × g for 20 min | Mitochondria (and chloroplasts from plant tissue) |
| ≈ 80,000 × g for 60 min | Small fragments of ER and plasma membrane |
| ≈ 150,000 × g for 3 h | Ribosomes |

The highest speeds need an **ultracentrifuge**, a machine first developed in the 1920s that spins samples fast enough to produce forces many thousands of times gravity.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: new techniques drive progress">

Organelles had been seen with microscopes, but their functions could only be tested once ultracentrifuges and fractionation methods existed. Testing each fraction for enzyme activity showed, for example, that mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration, and in the 1950s it led to the discovery of lysosomes: an enzyme in one fraction became more active when the fraction's membranes were damaged, revealing that it had been enclosed in a previously unknown membrane-bound organelle.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

To justify whether a structure is an organelle, use both ideas in the definition: it must be **discrete** and adapted for a **specific function**. For fractionation, explain each condition of the solution (cold, isotonic, buffered) and state that larger, denser organelles sediment at lower speeds.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="nucleus-cytoplasm" title="Separating the nucleus from the cytoplasm" level="SL" covers="B2.2.2">

<Viewer3D topic="b2-2" id="organelles" />

In a eukaryotic cell the chromosomes are enclosed in the **nucleus**, bounded by the **nuclear envelope**. This divides the cell into two compartments with different jobs:

- **Transcription** (making RNA from DNA) happens in the **nucleus**.
- **Translation** (making polypeptides on ribosomes) happens in the **cytoplasm**. No ribosomes translate mRNA inside the nucleus.

### The advantage: mRNA is modified before it meets ribosomes

The RNA first made by transcription is **pre-mRNA**. While it is still in the nucleus it undergoes **post-transcriptional modification**. Most importantly, non-coding sequences called **introns** are cut out and the remaining **exons** are joined together; the two ends of the molecule are also modified. Only this **mature mRNA** is exported through the **nuclear pores** to the cytoplasm.

Because the ribosomes are on the other side of the envelope, they cannot start translating pre-mRNA. If they did, the introns would be translated, giving a polypeptide with the wrong amino acid sequence that would usually not function. Keeping the two processes apart makes sure modification is finished first. (The details of splicing are in <TopicLink code="D1.2" />.)

### Prokaryotes: no separation

Prokaryotes have no nucleus: their DNA lies in the cytoplasm. Ribosomes can attach to an mRNA molecule and begin translating it **while it is still being transcribed**, so there is no opportunity to modify the mRNA first. Most bacterial genes have no introns, so this does not cause errors for them, and it lets bacteria make new proteins very quickly.

<Section id="compartments" title="Advantages of compartments in the cytoplasm" level="SL" covers="B2.2.3">

The membranes of organelles divide the cytoplasm into many separate **compartments**. Each compartment can have its own set of enzymes and its own internal conditions. This **compartmentalization** has three main advantages.

1. **Enzymes and metabolites are concentrated.** If the enzymes and substrates of a pathway are packed into a small organelle rather than spread through the whole cell, their concentrations are higher, they collide more often and the reactions are faster. The product of one enzyme is also made close to the next enzyme in the pathway.
2. **Incompatible processes are kept apart.** Some processes would interfere with each other if they shared a space. Hydrolytic enzymes would digest the cell's own proteins and nucleic acids; fatty acids are built up in the cytosol but broken down inside mitochondria, so the two pathways do not undo each other.
3. **Conditions can be optimized.** Each compartment can be kept at conditions that suit its enzymes, such as a particular pH.

### Lysosomes

**Lysosomes** are vesicles bounded by a single membrane and filled with dozens of different **hydrolytic enzymes** (proteases, lipases, nucleases and carbohydrases). Proton pumps in the membrane use ATP to keep the inside at about **pH 4.5–5**, the optimum for these enzymes, while the cytosol is about pH 7.2. Lysosomes digest material taken into the cell by endocytosis and break down worn-out organelles, so their contents can be recycled. If a lysosome leaks, its enzymes are much less active at the pH of the cytosol, which protects the cell further.

### Phagocytic vacuoles

White blood cells such as **macrophages** and **neutrophils** engulf bacteria by **endocytosis**, enclosing each in a **phagocytic vacuole** (see <TopicLink code="B2.1" />). Lysosomes fuse with the vacuole and release their enzymes into it. The bacterium is digested **inside the vacuole**, and the small products, such as amino acids and sugars, are absorbed into the cytoplasm. Unicellular organisms such as *Amoeba* digest their food in the same way, in food vacuoles.

<Figure name="b2-2-compartments" caption="Three advantages of compartmentalization: enzymes and substrates concentrated in a small volume; a lysosome keeping its hydrolases, at their low optimum pH, apart from the rest of the cell; and a bacterium digested inside a phagocytic vacuole after lysosomes fuse with it." />

| Advantage | How the membrane helps | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration of enzymes and substrates | Small volume, so collisions are more frequent | Krebs cycle enzymes in the mitochondrial matrix |
| Separation of incompatible processes | Enzymes kept away from molecules they would destroy | Hydrolases in lysosomes |
| Optimal conditions | Pumps keep the inside different from the cytosol | pH 4.5–5 inside lysosomes |
| Safe digestion of engulfed material | Digestion is confined to a membrane-bound sac | Bacteria digested in phagocytic vacuoles |

<Callout type="exam">

Name the advantage **and** give an example: "lysosomes keep hydrolytic enzymes separate from the rest of the cell, which they would otherwise digest" earns more credit than "compartments keep things separate".

</Callout>

<Section id="mitochondrion" title="The mitochondrion: adapted for ATP production" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.4">

Mitochondria carry out the aerobic stages of cell respiration. Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm; the pyruvate it produces enters the mitochondrion, where the **link reaction** and **Krebs cycle** take place in the matrix, and ATP is made by the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis in the inner membrane (see <TopicLink code="C1.2" />). The structure of the mitochondrion is closely related to these functions, an example of adaptation produced by natural selection.

| Structure | Adaptation | How it helps ATP production |
|---|---|---|
| **Double membrane** | The outer membrane encloses a separate compartment; the inner membrane holds the electron transport chains and **ATP synthase** | Conditions inside can suit respiration; the inner membrane is impermeable to protons, so they return to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase |
| **Small intermembrane space** | A very small volume between the two membranes | Relatively few protons pumped into it give a high concentration, so a steep proton gradient builds up quickly |
| **Cristae** | Inner membrane folded into shelves or tubes | A large surface area for electron transport chains and ATP synthase, so more ATP can be made |
| **Matrix** | Enzymes and substrates of the link reaction and Krebs cycle are compartmentalized in it | High concentrations give fast reactions, separate from glycolysis in the cytoplasm |

The electron transport chains use energy from electrons to pump protons **from the matrix into the intermembrane space**. Protons then diffuse back down their concentration gradient through ATP synthase, whose head projects into the matrix, and ATP is made **in the matrix**.

Cells that use a lot of ATP, such as muscle cells, have many mitochondria with densely packed cristae. Mitochondria also contain **70S ribosomes** and a loop of naked **DNA**, so they can make some of their own proteins.

</Callout>

<Section id="chloroplast" title="The chloroplast: adapted for photosynthesis" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.5">

A chloroplast is bounded by a **double membrane** (the chloroplast envelope) around the fluid **stroma**. Inside is a third, separate system of membranes: the **thylakoids**, flattened sacs that are stacked into **grana** and joined by single **stroma lamellae**. The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes and the light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) in the stroma (see <TopicLink code="C1.3" />).

| Structure | Adaptation | How it helps photosynthesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Thylakoid membranes** | A very large total surface area, packed into grana | Room for many **photosystems**, electron carriers and ATP synthase molecules, so light is absorbed and ATP made at a high rate |
| **Thylakoid lumen** | A very small volume of fluid inside each thylakoid | A high proton concentration builds up after relatively few photons have been absorbed, driving ATP synthesis |
| **Stroma** | The enzymes of the Calvin cycle, including **Rubisco**, and their substrates are compartmentalized here | High concentrations give fast reactions, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and NADPH |
| **Double membrane** | Encloses a compartment separate from the cytoplasm | Conditions inside can be kept suitable for photosynthesis |

Protons are moved **into the thylakoid lumen** during the light-dependent reactions. They diffuse back into the **stroma** through ATP synthase, so ATP is made in the stroma, exactly where the Calvin cycle uses it. Chloroplasts also contain **starch grains** (a store of the sugar they make), **70S ribosomes** and naked, circular **DNA**.

### Mitochondrion and chloroplast compared

| | Mitochondrion | Chloroplast |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane with the ATP synthase | Inner membrane (cristae) | Thylakoid membrane |
| Small space where protons accumulate | Intermembrane space | Thylakoid lumen |
| Compartment where ATP is made | Matrix | Stroma |
| Enzymes concentrated in the fluid | Link reaction and Krebs cycle | Calvin cycle (e.g. Rubisco) |

<Callout type="exam">

Do not mix up the two kinds of space: in a mitochondrion protons build up in the **intermembrane space**; in a chloroplast they build up in the **thylakoid lumen**, not between the two envelope membranes.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="nuclear-envelope" title="The double membrane of the nucleus" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.6">

The **nuclear envelope** consists of two membranes, each a phospholipid bilayer, separated by a narrow **perinuclear space**. The **outer membrane** is continuous with the membrane of the rough ER, and like the RER it has ribosomes on its cytoplasmic face; the perinuclear space is continuous with the ER lumen. The **inner membrane** is lined by a mesh of proteins that supports the envelope and anchors chromatin.

### Nuclear pores

At **nuclear pores**, the outer and inner membranes are joined, so the pore is a channel through the envelope lined with membrane. Each pore is filled by a **nuclear pore complex**, a large ring-shaped structure made of many proteins. A nucleus in a typical mammalian cell has a few thousand pores. They are needed because:

- **mRNA** and **ribosome subunits** are made in the nucleus but used in the cytoplasm, so they must move **out**;
- **proteins** needed in the nucleus, such as histones, DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase and the proteins of ribosomes, are made in the cytoplasm, so they must move **in**, along with nucleotides.

Small molecules diffuse through the pores freely, but large molecules are let through selectively.

### Benefits of a double membrane

1. **Pores can be formed** by joining the outer membrane to the inner membrane around an opening, linking nucleoplasm and cytoplasm without exposing the space between the membranes.
2. **The envelope can break up into vesicles** during **mitosis and meiosis**. At the end of prophase the envelope breaks down, releasing the chromosomes so that spindle microtubules can attach to them and move them. At telophase, the membrane vesicles are used to rebuild a nuclear envelope around each group of chromosomes, forming the daughter nuclei.

<Callout type="note" title="A note on accuracy">

Research shows that in many animal cells the membrane of the broken-down envelope is absorbed into the ER network rather than forming separate vesicles. For IB answers, describe the envelope as breaking into vesicles that are later used to rebuild the nuclear membranes.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

When asked about the functional benefits of the double membrane, give both: it allows **pores** to form (outer and inner membranes fused), and it can **break into vesicles** so chromosomes are released for division and new envelopes can be reassembled afterwards.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="ribosomes-rer" title="Free ribosomes and the rough ER" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.7">

Eukaryotic ribosomes in the cytoplasm are **80S**, made of a large (60S) and a small (40S) subunit, each built from ribosomal RNA and proteins. They are found in two places:

- **Free ribosomes** float in the cytosol.
- **Bound ribosomes** are attached to the cytoplasmic surface of the **rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)**.

The two kinds are **identical**. Translation of every protein coded by nuclear genes begins on a free ribosome. If the polypeptide begins with a **signal sequence**, the ribosome is directed to the RER and binds to it, and the growing polypeptide is fed through the membrane into the ER lumen. Without a signal sequence, the ribosome stays free.

| | Free ribosomes | Ribosomes on the rough ER |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Cytosol | Cytoplasmic face of the RER |
| Where the polypeptide goes | Released into the cytosol | Into the lumen of the RER (or into its membrane) |
| Proteins made | For **retention in the cell**, working in the cytosol, e.g. enzymes of glycolysis | For **transport within the cell** (to lysosomes, the Golgi or the plasma membrane) and for **secretion** |

### Structure and function of the rough ER

The RER is a network of flattened, membrane-bound sacs called **cisternae**, with ribosomes on their outer surface; the space inside is the **lumen**, and the network is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. In the lumen, polypeptides **fold** into their three-dimensional shape and begin to be modified, for example by the addition of carbohydrate. They are then carried to the Golgi apparatus in **transport vesicles** that bud from the RER.

Cells that secrete large amounts of protein, such as the cells of the pancreas that secrete digestive enzymes and plasma cells that secrete antibodies, are packed with rough ER.

<Section id="golgi" title="The Golgi apparatus: processing and secretion" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.8">

The **Golgi apparatus** is a stack of flattened, curved, membrane-bound sacs (**cisternae**), usually about four to eight in animal cells. Unlike the RER, the cisternae in a stack are separate from one another, and they have no ribosomes. The stack has two distinct faces:

- the **cis face**, which is convex and faces the rough ER: it **receives** proteins;
- the **trans face**, which is concave and usually faces the plasma membrane: it **ships** them out.

### Processing proteins

1. Transport vesicles from the RER fuse with the cisterna on the **cis** side, delivering their proteins.
2. The proteins are passed from cisterna to cisterna towards the **trans** side.
3. **Enzymes** in the cisternae modify them as they go. For example, carbohydrate chains are added or altered (making glycoproteins), and some polypeptides are cut or have other groups added. The final form of the protein is gradually produced: insulin, for instance, is made as a longer precursor, proinsulin, which is cut to form active insulin as it leaves the Golgi in secretory vesicles.
4. The finished proteins are sorted and packed into vesicles that bud from the **trans** face.

### Secretion

**Secretory vesicles** from the trans face carry proteins to the plasma membrane and release them outside the cell by **exocytosis**. Some are released continuously; others are stored until a signal arrives, as when β cells of the pancreas release insulin when blood glucose rises. Other vesicles from the trans face carry proteins to lysosomes or to the plasma membrane itself.

<Callout type="note" title="How the pathway was discovered">

In the 1960s, pancreatic cells were given a short pulse of radioactive amino acids and then only non-radioactive ones, and samples were examined at intervals. The radioactive proteins appeared first in the rough ER, then in the Golgi apparatus, then in secretory vesicles and finally outside the cell, revealing the route proteins take.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

Learn the sequence: **bound ribosomes → RER lumen → transport vesicle → cis face of the Golgi → cisternae (modification) → trans face → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane (exocytosis)**.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="vesicles" title="Vesicles and clathrin" level="AHL" covers="B2.2.9">

A **vesicle** is a small, spherical sac of single membrane (a phospholipid bilayer) enclosing a droplet of fluid. Vesicles form by **budding** from a membrane, are moved through the cytoplasm along microtubules by motor proteins, and deliver their contents by **fusing** with a target membrane. This works because membranes are fluid (see <TopicLink code="B2.1" />).

### Roles of vesicles

- **Transport between organelles**: from the RER to the Golgi apparatus, and from the Golgi to lysosomes.
- **Secretion**: carrying proteins to the plasma membrane for exocytosis.
- **Taking materials in**: endocytosis, including the phagocytic vacuoles of white blood cells.
- **Adding membrane**: when vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane, their phospholipids and membrane proteins become part of it, so the cell can grow.

### Clathrin and coated vesicles

Some vesicles are formed with the help of **clathrin**, a protein with three bent legs. At the plasma membrane:

1. **Receptor** proteins in the membrane bind particular molecules (cargo) outside the cell, such as cholesterol-carrying particles in the blood.
2. Clathrin molecules gather on the **cytoplasmic** side of that patch and link together into a curved, cage-like lattice of hexagons and pentagons. This pulls the membrane inwards into a **coated pit**.
3. The pit deepens until only a narrow neck remains, which is pinched off. A **clathrin-coated vesicle**, with the receptors and cargo inside, separates into the cytoplasm.
4. The coat is soon **removed** and the clathrin is reused. The uncoated vesicle then fuses with its target membrane inside the cell.

Clathrin-coated vesicles also bud from the trans face of the Golgi apparatus, for example carrying enzymes destined for lysosomes. Vesicles leaving the rough ER use different coat proteins. Steps 7–9 of the animation above show a clathrin-coated vesicle forming.

<Callout type="exam">

State clathrin's role precisely: it forms a **coat on the cytoplasmic side** that **shapes the membrane into a bud** so a vesicle can form. It is not a motor protein, and it does not stay on the vesicle.

</Callout>

</Section>
