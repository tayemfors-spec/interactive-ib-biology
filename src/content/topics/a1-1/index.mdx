<Section id="medium" title="Water: the medium of life" level="SL" covers="A1.1.1">

Life began in water, and water is still the medium in which almost every process of life takes place. A **medium** here means the surroundings in which something happens. The first cells are thought to have formed in water over 3.5 billion years ago (hypotheses include deep-sea hydrothermal vents and shallow pools), and every living cell today is filled with an **aqueous solution**, a solution in which water is the solvent.

- **Cytoplasm** is mostly water: water makes up about 70% of the mass of a typical cell, and about 60% of an adult human.
- **Metabolism** happens in solution. Reactants, products and enzymes are dissolved or suspended in water, so they can move and collide.
- **Transport**: blood plasma, tissue fluid, xylem sap and phloem sap are all aqueous solutions.
- **Reproduction and movement**: sperm swim through fluid to the egg, and countless organisms live their whole lives in water.

Several features of water make this possible. It is liquid over the range of temperatures found on most of Earth's surface, it dissolves a very wide range of substances, it sticks to itself and to other surfaces, and it changes temperature only slowly. All of these properties come from one cause: the **hydrogen bonds** that form between water molecules, explained in the next section.

<Callout type="note" title="The big idea">

For every property on this page, link structure to function in three steps: **polarity** of the molecule → **hydrogen bonding** between molecules → the **property** → its **consequence** for living things.

</Callout>

<Section id="polarity" title="Polar covalent bonds and hydrogen bonds" level="SL" covers="A1.1.2">

<Viewer3D topic="a1-1" id="water" />

### Why water is polar

A water molecule (H₂O) has one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms by **covalent bonds**: each bond is a pair of electrons shared between the two atoms. The sharing is **unequal**. The oxygen nucleus has a much larger positive charge (8 protons) than a hydrogen nucleus (1 proton), so oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly; oxygen is more **electronegative**. The electrons spend more time near the oxygen.

- The oxygen end gains a slight negative charge, written **δ−** (delta minus).
- Each hydrogen gains a slight positive charge, **δ+**.

Bonds like this are **polar covalent bonds**. Because the molecule is **bent**, with an angle of about **104.5°** between the bonds, both δ+ charges are on one side and the δ− on the other. The charges do not cancel out, so the whole molecule is **polar**.

### Hydrogen bonds

Opposite charges attract. The δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule. This attraction is a **hydrogen bond**.

| | Covalent O–H bond | Hydrogen bond |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Within one molecule (intramolecular) | Between molecules (intermolecular) |
| Cause | Shared pair of electrons | Attraction between δ+ H and δ− O |
| Strength | Strong | Weak, about 1/20 as strong |
| Drawn as | Solid line | Dashed line |

Each molecule can form up to **four** hydrogen bonds: two through its own hydrogen atoms and two through the two **lone pairs** of electrons on its oxygen. Individual hydrogen bonds are weak and in liquid water each lasts only about a picosecond (10⁻¹² s), but water molecules are so small and numerous that, at any instant, a huge number of them exist. Collectively they give water its unusual properties. In **ice**, every molecule is held by four hydrogen bonds in an open hexagonal lattice, which is why ice is less dense than liquid water (see the **Ice** model above).

<Section id="cohesion-adhesion" title="Cohesion and adhesion" level="SL" covers="A1.1.3 A1.1.4">

Hydrogen bonds make water molecules stick to each other and to other polar surfaces. These two effects have different names.

### Cohesion

**Cohesion** is the attraction of water molecules to one another by hydrogen bonds. It has two important consequences for organisms.

**Transport under tension in xylem.** In plants, water evaporates from the walls of cells inside the leaves and diffuses out through the stomata (**transpiration**). This pulls on the water in the xylem vessels, putting it under **tension** (a negative pressure). Because of cohesion, the column of water does not break: the whole column is pulled up from the roots, even to the top of trees over 100 m tall. No energy from the plant is used to lift the water. (More on this in <TopicLink code="B3.2" />.)

**Surface tension.** A molecule inside the liquid is pulled equally in all directions, but molecules at the surface have no water molecules above them, so they are pulled sideways and inwards. The hydrogen-bonded surface behaves like a stretched film that resists being broken. Small animals use this film as a habitat: **pond skaters** (*Gerris*) stand and run on it, and mosquito larvae hang from it to breathe.

### Adhesion

**Adhesion** is the attraction of water to other materials that are **polar or charged**, by hydrogen bonds or by attraction between water's partial charges and charged groups. Water adheres to glass, to clay and humus particles in soil, and to **cellulose**, whose many –OH groups form hydrogen bonds with water.

Adhesion and cohesion together produce **capillary action**: water is drawn along narrow spaces because it adheres to the walls, and cohesion pulls more water along behind. The narrower the space, the higher the water rises.

- **In soil**, water moves through the narrow pores between particles, including upwards from wetter layers, so roots can reach it.
- **In plant cell walls**, water spreads through the network of cellulose fibres. Cell walls stay wet, and walls inside leaves are refilled from the xylem as water evaporates from them.

<Section id="solvent" title="Water as a solvent" level="SL" covers="A1.1.5">

A wide variety of substances dissolve in water. They are called **hydrophilic** ("water-loving"):

- **Ions** such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻ dissolve because water molecules are attracted to their charges. The δ− oxygen atoms face positive ions and the δ+ hydrogen atoms face negative ions. Each ion becomes surrounded by a **hydration shell** of water molecules, which separates it from other ions and keeps it in solution.
- **Polar molecules** such as glucose, amino acids and urea dissolve because they form hydrogen bonds with water, through groups such as –OH and –NH₂.

<Anim topic="a1-1" id="solvation" />

### A medium for metabolism

Because so many substances dissolve in it, water is the medium in which most metabolic reactions happen. Most reactions catalysed by **enzymes** take place in water-based (**aqueous**) solutions: in the cytoplasm, in the matrix of mitochondria and in the stroma of chloroplasts. Dissolved substrates and enzymes move about freely, so they collide often. Water is also itself a reactant in hydrolysis reactions and a product of condensation reactions.

### A medium for transport

- **Animals**: blood plasma carries glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, urea, hormones and carbon dioxide (mostly as hydrogencarbonate ions) in solution.
- **Plants**: xylem sap carries water and mineral ions from the roots; phloem sap carries sucrose and amino acids.

### When being insoluble matters

Non-polar substances, such as fats, oils and the hydrocarbon tails of phospholipids, are **hydrophobic**. They are not repelled by water; water molecules simply attract each other more strongly than they attract the non-polar molecules, so the non-polar molecules are excluded and clump together. Some molecules work precisely **because** they are hydrophobic and insoluble:

- the hydrophobic core of **membranes** is a barrier to ions and polar molecules (see <TopicLink code="B1.1" />);
- **waxy cuticles** on leaves and oils on skin reduce water loss;
- **triglyceride** fat stores do not dissolve, so they do not affect osmosis in the cells that store them.

Hydrophobic molecules in blood need help: fats and cholesterol are carried inside **lipoprotein** particles, and oxygen, which is non-polar and only slightly soluble, is carried mostly by haemoglobin.

</Section>

<Section id="habitats" title="Living in water and in air" level="SL" covers="A1.1.6">

Water and air have very different physical properties, so animals that swim face different challenges from those that fly or live on land. Two animals that use the far north show the contrasts well:

- the **ringed seal** (*Pusa hispida*), a mammal of Arctic seas, which spends most of its life in the water and comes out onto the sea ice to rest, moult and give birth;
- the **black-throated loon** (*Gavia arctica*), a diving bird that breeds on northern lakes, catches fish underwater and flies between lakes and to the coast.

| Property | Water compared with air (20 °C) | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Buoyancy** | About 830× denser, so it gives far more upward force | Water supports a seal's weight; a loon in flight must generate lift |
| **Viscosity** | About 55× more viscous (resistance to flow) | Swimming meets more resistance than flying; streamlined bodies reduce drag |
| **Thermal conductivity** | About 23× higher | Animals lose heat much faster in water than in air at the same temperature |
| **Specific heat capacity** | 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹: about 4× air per gram, about 3,450× per litre | Water temperature changes slowly, so aquatic habitats are thermally stable |

The high viscosity, specific heat capacity and cohesion of water all come from hydrogen bonding, which also explains why water is liquid at all at Earth's temperatures (part 2 of the simulation compares it with methane).

<Sim topic="a1-1" id="properties" />

### The two animals

- **Ringed seal.** Its body density is close to that of seawater, so the water supports almost all its weight. A streamlined body and flippers reduce resistance in viscous water. Because water draws heat away so quickly, it has a thick layer of **blubber** (fat), a poor conductor of heat. Its habitat is thermally stable: the seawater under the ice stays close to −1.8 °C, its freezing point, while the air above may be far colder. Seals keep breathing holes open in the ice with their claws, and pups are born in snow lairs on the ice. Ice can form this floating roof only because it is less dense than water.
- **Black-throated loon.** Air gives almost no buoyancy, so in flight the loon must keep flapping to generate lift, and it needs a long run across the water to take off. Air's low viscosity lets it fly fast. Its dense, waterproof plumage traps air, a poor conductor, for insulation. On water it floats; to dive it presses air out of its feathers, and its bones are less air-filled than those of most birds. Its feet are set far back for swimming underwater, which makes walking on land awkward. In winter the lakes where it breeds freeze over, so loons migrate to coastal seas that stay ice-free: the large volume of seawater, with its high specific heat capacity, loses heat slowly and does not freeze over.

<Section id="origin" title="Where Earth's water came from, and why it stayed" level="AHL" covers="A1.1.7">

Earth has about 1.4 billion km³ of water, and it has had liquid oceans for most of its 4.5-billion-year history. This long-lasting abundance of water is what allowed life to begin and evolve. But where did the water come from?

### The asteroid hypothesis

When the Solar System formed from a disc of gas and dust, the inner region near the young Sun was too hot for water to condense as ice. The rocky planets, including Earth, formed there from mostly dry material, and the young Earth was itself very hot. Further out, beyond the "snow line" in the outer asteroid belt, ice and water-bearing minerals could form. The leading hypothesis is that much of Earth's water was **delivered by water-rich asteroids** that collided with Earth during and after its formation.

Evidence comes from the ratio of **deuterium** (heavy hydrogen, ²H) to ordinary hydrogen in water. Different parts of the Solar System have different D/H ratios, a little like a fingerprint:

- water in **carbonaceous chondrites**, meteorites from asteroids, has a D/H ratio close to that of Earth's oceans;
- most **comets** measured, such as comet 67P studied by the Rosetta spacecraft, have much higher D/H ratios, so they are unlikely to be the main source.

<Figure name="a1-1-origin" level="AHL" caption="The asteroid hypothesis. Top: rocky planets formed inside the snow line, where it was too hot for ice; water-rich asteroids from further out delivered water to the young Earth. Bottom: the D/H ratio of water in carbonaceous asteroids is close to that of Earth's oceans, but most comets measured have much more deuterium. Values are approximate." />

### Why Earth kept its water

Two factors let the water stay on Earth, as liquid, for billions of years:

1. **Gravity.** Earth is massive enough for its gravity to hold on to its atmosphere, including water vapour. Small bodies such as the Moon have too little gravity and have lost almost all their gases.
2. **Temperatures low enough for water to condense.** As the young Earth cooled, water vapour condensed into liquid oceans. At Earth's distance from the Sun, the surface stays mostly between 0 °C and 100 °C, and the upper atmosphere is cold enough that water vapour condenses before it can rise high enough to be split by ultraviolet light and lost to space. Venus, closer to the Sun, appears to have lost most of its water in this way.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope">

The IB guide limits the hypotheses to **asteroids**, and the reasons for retention to **gravity** and **temperatures low enough to condense water**. You may read that Earth's magnetic field also protects its atmosphere; how much it matters is debated, and you do not need it.

</Callout>

<Section id="search" title="Water and the search for life beyond Earth" level="AHL" covers="A1.1.8">

Every living thing on Earth depends on liquid water as its solvent and medium for metabolism, and no form of life is known that does without it. So when scientists search for life beyond Earth, a guiding strategy is to **"follow the water"**: they look first for places where liquid water exists now or existed in the past.

### The Goldilocks zone

A planet can only have liquid water on its surface if its temperature lies between the melting and boiling points of water. That depends mainly on the planet's distance from its star. The **Goldilocks zone** (also called the **habitable zone**) is the range of distances from a star within which a planet with a suitable atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface: not too hot, not too cold, but "just right". Its position depends on how bright the star is: it is closer in around a cool, dim star and further out around a hot, bright one.

<Figure name="a1-1-goldilocks" level="AHL" caption="The Goldilocks zone of the Solar System (boundaries are estimates). Venus is too close to the Sun and has lost its water; Earth has liquid oceans; Mars lies near the outer edge, but it is small and has a thin atmosphere, so its water is mostly frozen." />

- **Venus** is too close and too hot (about 464 °C); any oceans were lost.
- **Earth** is in the zone and has had liquid water for billions of years.
- **Mars** is near the outer edge. It once had rivers and lakes, but it is small: its weak gravity made it easier for most of its atmosphere to escape to space, and today its water is mainly ice.

### Where astronomers look

- **Exoplanets** (planets orbiting other stars) that lie in their star's Goldilocks zone are the main targets; thousands of exoplanets have been found, and telescopes now analyse some of their atmospheres for water vapour.
- **Mars** is explored for signs of past life in ancient lake beds.
- Some **icy moons** outside the zone, such as Europa (Jupiter) and Enceladus (Saturn), are thought to have liquid water oceans beneath their ice, kept warm by tidal heating.

<Callout type="exam">

When explaining the Goldilocks zone, refer to **distance from the star** and **temperature allowing liquid water** (between 0 °C and 100 °C at Earth-like pressure), and say why water matters: it is the medium in which the processes of life occur. Being in the zone does not guarantee water or life.

</Callout>
