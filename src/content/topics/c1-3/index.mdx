<Section id="energy" title="Photosynthesis: light energy into chemical energy" level="SL" covers="C1.3.1 C1.3.2 C1.3.3">

<Viewer3D topic="c1-3" id="photosynthesis" />

**Photosynthesis** is an **energy transformation**: **light energy** absorbed by pigments is converted into **chemical energy** in the bonds of carbon compounds such as glucose. Producers that photosynthesise supply this chemical energy to almost every food chain, so photosynthesis is the source of most of the energy used for life processes in ecosystems (see <TopicLink code="C4.2" />).

### The word equation

> carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light energy absorbed by chlorophyll)

The balanced equation is often written 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.

### Reducing carbon dioxide using hydrogen from water

Compare the molecules: carbon dioxide (CO₂) has one carbon to two oxygen atoms, while glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) has one carbon to two hydrogens and one oxygen. To turn CO₂ into glucose, hydrogen must be added and oxygen removed. This is **reduction**.

The hydrogen comes from **splitting water**. Water is a very stable molecule, so splitting it needs energy, and this is supplied by light. Each water molecule provides hydrogen in the form of electrons and protons (H⁺), and its oxygen is released.

### Oxygen is a by-product

Photosynthesis that splits water and releases oxygen is carried out by **plants**, **algae** and **cyanobacteria**. All of the oxygen released comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. Experiments in the 1940s with water labelled with the heavy oxygen isotope ¹⁸O showed that the labelled oxygen appeared in the O₂ given off.

Photosynthesising organisms use some of the oxygen in their own aerobic respiration, but in the light they produce far more than they use, and the rest diffuses out as waste. In aquatic plants it can be seen as bubbles; on land it diffuses unseen into the air through the stomata.

<Callout type="exam">

If asked where the oxygen in photosynthesis comes from, the answer is **water** (photolysis). Glucose gets its carbon and oxygen atoms from CO₂, and its hydrogen from water.

</Callout>

<Section id="chromatography" title="Separating photosynthetic pigments by chromatography" level="SL" covers="C1.3.4">

Leaves contain several **photosynthetic pigments**: **chlorophyll** a and b, and **accessory pigments** such as carotene and xanthophylls (together called carotenoids). They can be separated by **paper chromatography** or **thin-layer chromatography** (TLC).

### Method in outline

1. Grind leaf tissue with a little sand and a solvent such as propanone to extract the pigments.
2. Draw a **pencil** origin line about 1 cm from the end of the strip (ink would dissolve and run).
3. Build up a small, concentrated spot of extract on the origin by adding tiny drops and letting each dry.
4. Stand the strip in a sealed tube with the running solvent **below** the origin, so the spot does not dissolve into the pool.
5. Remove the strip before the solvent reaches the top and mark the **solvent front** at once, before it evaporates.

Each pigment moves at a different speed. A pigment that is more soluble in the running solvent, and less attracted to the stationary paper or TLC layer, travels further.

### Calculating R<sub>f</sub>

**R<sub>f</sub> = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent**

Measure both distances from the origin line, to the centre of the spot. For example, a blue-green spot 31 mm from the origin with a solvent front at 50 mm has R<sub>f</sub> = 31 ÷ 50 = 0.62, which fits chlorophyll a.

<Sim topic="c1-3" id="chromatography" />

Typical results, from the top of the strip: **carotene** (orange-yellow, R<sub>f</sub> close to 1), sometimes grey **phaeophytin**, **chlorophyll a** (blue-green), **chlorophyll b** (yellow-green) and **xanthophylls** (yellow) lowest. Exact R<sub>f</sub> values depend on the solvent, paper and temperature, so identify pigments by both colour and R<sub>f</sub>.

<Callout type="exam">

R<sub>f</sub> has no units and must lie between 0 and 1. Give it to two decimal places and show the working: "R<sub>f</sub> = 31 mm ÷ 50 mm = 0.62".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="spectra" title="Absorption and action spectra" level="SL" covers="C1.3.5 C1.3.6">

### Why pigments absorb only some wavelengths

Sunlight reaching the ground includes the visible wavelengths **400–700 nm**, the range used in photosynthesis. Shorter wavelengths (violet) carry more energy per photon than longer ones (red).

When a pigment absorbs a photon, the energy raises an electron in the molecule to a higher energy level: the electron becomes **excited**. This is how light energy starts to be converted into chemical energy. An electron can only jump between particular energy levels, so only photons with the right amount of energy (the right wavelengths) are absorbed. Other wavelengths are reflected or pass through.

### Absorption spectra

An **absorption spectrum** plots the percentage of light absorbed by a pigment against wavelength. Label the x-axis with **both** the wavelength in nm and the colours of light.

- **Chlorophylls** absorb violet-blue and red light strongly and green light very little. Green light is reflected or transmitted, which is why leaves look green.
- **Carotenoids** absorb violet, blue and blue-green light, but not yellow, orange or red.

### Action spectra

An **action spectrum** plots the **rate of photosynthesis** at each wavelength. Lighting a pondweed with one colour at a time (coloured LEDs or filters) and measuring the rate of oxygen production or CO₂ uptake gives the data.

<Sim topic="c1-3" id="spectra" />

| | Absorption spectrum | Action spectrum |
|---|---|---|
| y-axis | % light absorbed by a pigment | rate of photosynthesis |
| Peaks | blue and red | blue and red |
| Green | very low for chlorophyll | low, but well above zero |

The two have peaks in the same places because the light absorbed by chlorophyll is the light used. Some green light is still used because accessory pigments absorb it and pass the energy to chlorophyll.

### Calculating rates for an action spectrum

Rate = amount of O₂ produced (or CO₂ used) ÷ time. If a pondweed releases 1.8 cm³ of oxygen in 6 minutes under blue light, the rate is 1.8 ÷ 6 = **0.30 cm³ min⁻¹**. Repeat for each wavelength, then plot rate (y-axis) against wavelength (x-axis), joining the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.

<Callout type="exam">

Questions often ask you to "compare" the two spectra. Give similarities (peaks in blue and red, trough in green) **and** differences (the action spectrum is higher in green and does not match any single pigment's spectrum).

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="limiting" title="Limiting factors and how to investigate them" level="SL" covers="C1.3.7">

The rate of photosynthesis can be limited by **light intensity**, **carbon dioxide concentration** or **temperature**. At any moment, the **limiting factor** is the one that is furthest from its optimum: raising it increases the rate, while raising either of the others has no effect.

- **Light intensity**: at low intensity the rate rises in proportion to it, because the light-dependent reactions supply ATP and reduced NADP more quickly. Then the rate levels off (a plateau) as another factor becomes limiting.
- **CO₂ concentration**: there is no photosynthesis without CO₂; the rate rises with concentration and then plateaus.
- **Temperature**: the rate rises with temperature up to an **optimum**, because enzyme-catalysed reactions speed up. Above the optimum it falls steeply as enzymes such as Rubisco are denatured.

<Sim topic="c1-3" id="limiting" />

### Designing an experiment

Choose **one** factor as the **independent variable** and measure the rate as the **dependent variable**, keeping the other factors constant (**controlled variables**).

| Factor | How to vary it | How to keep it constant |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | move a lamp to different distances (intensity is proportional to 1/distance²) or use a light meter | fixed lamp distance; LED or heat shield so the water is not warmed |
| CO₂ concentration | add measured amounts of sodium hydrogencarbonate to boiled, cooled water | add excess hydrogencarbonate |
| Temperature | thermostatic water bath | water bath at, say, 25 °C |

The rate can be measured by counting oxygen bubbles from a cut pondweed stem, by collecting the gas and measuring its volume, by timing leaf discs as they float, or by measuring CO₂ uptake from the change in pH (hydrogencarbonate indicator).

A **hypothesis** is a provisional explanation that can be tested, for example: "The rate will increase with light intensity, because more light energy is available to excite electrons, until another factor limits it". Hypotheses can be proposed before an experiment, based on theory, or after it, based on the data, and need repeated testing.

<Callout type="exam">

When describing a limiting-factor graph, name the limiting factor on **each part**: on the rising part it is the factor on the x-axis; on the plateau it is one of the other factors. Explain the plateau by saying which factor now limits.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="co2-enrichment" title="Carbon dioxide enrichment experiments" level="SL" covers="C1.3.8">

Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialization to about 420 ppm today and will keep rising this century. Because CO₂ is often the limiting factor in bright light, scientists want to predict how future CO₂ levels will affect photosynthesis, crop yields and the carbon stored by forests.

### Greenhouse experiments

In **enclosed greenhouse experiments** plants are grown in glasshouses or chambers with CO₂ raised to set levels. Temperature, light, water and minerals can be closely controlled, so the effect of CO₂ alone can be measured. Commercial growers already enrich greenhouse air with CO₂ to raise yields. However, plants in pots and enclosures may not behave like plants in fields and forests.

### FACE experiments

**Free-air carbon dioxide enrichment (FACE)** experiments are done in the open, on crops, grassland and forest. Rings of pipes or towers release CO₂ on the upwind side, and computer-controlled sensors keep the concentration inside the ring at a target such as 550 ppm. **Control rings** are set up in the same way but release ordinary air, so the only difference is the CO₂ concentration. The first FACE experiments used crops and young tree plantations; later ones enriched mature, natural forests.

FACE results show that photosynthesis is faster at higher CO₂, but growth and yield increased less than many enclosed experiments had predicted, partly because other factors such as nitrogen and water then become limiting.

### Control in the laboratory and in the field

Control of variables is easier in a laboratory or greenhouse, but some questions can only be answered in the **field**, in natural ecosystems. In FACE, weather and soil cannot be controlled, so ecologists use many replicate rings, choose plots with similar soils and vegetation, and monitor uncontrolled variables such as temperature and rainfall.

<Callout type="exam">

To identify a **controlled variable** in a FACE study, pick something kept the same in enriched and control rings, such as plant species, ring size, soil type or the way the gas is released. The CO₂ concentration is the independent variable.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="photosystems" title="Photosystems: arrays of pigment molecules" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.9 C1.3.10">

Photosynthetic pigments do not work as separate molecules. They are organized into **photosystems**: large complexes of proteins holding many pigment molecules in fixed positions. Photosystems are always located in **membranes**: the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts in plants and algae, and the thylakoid membranes in the cytoplasm of **cyanobacteria**.

Each photosystem has two parts:

- **Light-harvesting (antenna) complexes**: hundreds of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoid molecules. When one absorbs a photon, the excitation energy is passed from pigment to pigment, always towards the centre.
- A **reaction centre**: a special pair of chlorophyll a molecules. When the energy reaches it, an electron is excited enough to be **emitted** to an electron acceptor. This is the step that turns light energy into chemical energy.

There are two types, named in the order they were discovered: **photosystem II**, with reaction centre P680, and **photosystem I**, with reaction centre P700 (the numbers are the wavelengths in nm they absorb best).

### Why an array is better than single pigments

- **More photons are caught.** Even in bright light one chlorophyll molecule absorbs only a few photons per second. Hundreds of antenna pigments funnel energy to one reaction centre, so it is supplied rapidly.
- **More wavelengths are used.** Different pigments absorb different wavelengths, so a greater proportion of the energy in sunlight is harvested.
- **Energy is funnelled precisely.** The proteins hold each pigment at the right distance and orientation for energy to pass towards the reaction centre.
- **Electrons can be passed on.** The reaction centre is next to the electron acceptors that take the excited electrons.

A single pigment molecule could not perform any part of photosynthesis. In solution, isolated chlorophyll simply releases absorbed energy as heat and red fluorescence, because there is no acceptor to take an excited electron.

</Section>

<Section id="photolysis" title="Photolysis of water in photosystem II" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.11">

When P680 emits an excited electron it becomes positively charged and is a very strong oxidising agent. It regains electrons by splitting water. This happens at a **water-splitting site** (the oxygen-evolving complex, which contains manganese) on the side of photosystem II facing the **thylakoid space**:

> 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

The splitting is called **photolysis** because it depends on light-driven loss of electrons from P680.

- The **electrons** replace those emitted by P680, so photosystem II can be excited again.
- The **protons** (H⁺) are released into the thylakoid space and add to the proton gradient used to make ATP.
- The **oxygen** is a waste product. It diffuses out of the chloroplast and the cell.

### Consequences of oxygen production

Photolysis evolved in the ancestors of cyanobacteria and transformed the Earth:

- Oxygen accumulated in the oceans and atmosphere, starting about 2.4 billion years ago (the Great Oxidation Event).
- Dissolved iron in the oceans was oxidised and precipitated, forming the **banded iron formations** that are now major iron ores.
- Aerobic respiration became possible, releasing much more energy from food than anaerobic pathways; organisms that could not tolerate oxygen were restricted to anoxic habitats.
- An **ozone** layer formed in the upper atmosphere, absorbing ultraviolet radiation and making life on land safer.

<Callout type="exam">

Emphasize that the protons and electrons from photolysis are **used** (electrons by PSII, H⁺ for the proton gradient) but the **oxygen is waste**.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="chemiosmosis" title="ATP production by chemiosmosis" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.12">

ATP is made in thylakoids by **chemiosmosis**, as in mitochondria (see <TopicLink code="C1.2" />). Making ATP using light energy is called **photophosphorylation**.

1. **Electron transport**: excited electrons pass along a chain of electron carriers in the thylakoid membrane — plastoquinone, the cytochrome complex and plastocyanin.
2. **Proton pumping**: energy released as electrons pass along the chain is used to pump H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Together with H⁺ from photolysis, this creates a high concentration of H⁺ in the small thylakoid space: a **proton gradient**.
3. **ATP synthase**: the membrane is impermeable to H⁺, so the protons can only diffuse back to the stroma through **ATP synthase**. Their flow down the gradient makes part of the enzyme rotate, and it phosphorylates ADP to ATP on the stroma side.

Excited electrons can come from either photosystem:

| | Non-cyclic photophosphorylation | Cyclic photophosphorylation |
|---|---|---|
| Electrons from | photosystem II, then re-excited in photosystem I | photosystem I only |
| Electrons end up | reducing NADP | back in photosystem I (via ferredoxin and the cytochrome complex) |
| Products | ATP, reduced NADP and O₂ | ATP only |
| Water split? | yes | no |

Cyclic photophosphorylation tops up ATP, because the Calvin cycle uses more ATP than reduced NADP.

</Section>

<Section id="thylakoid-system" title="NADP reduction and the thylakoid as a system" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.13 C1.3.14">

### Reduction of NADP by photosystem I

Electrons arriving at photosystem I from photosystem II have lost energy. Light absorbed by photosystem I re-excites them at P700. They are emitted to **ferredoxin**, a carrier on the stroma side of the membrane, and then passed to the enzyme **NADP reductase**. NADP is reduced when it accepts **two electrons** that came from photosystem I and **one H⁺ from the stroma**:

> NADP + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → reduced NADP

Reduced NADP and ATP are the two useful products of the light-dependent reactions, and both are released into the stroma, where the Calvin cycle uses them.

<Callout type="note">

Use one pair of terms consistently: either **NADP and reduced NADP**, or **NADP⁺ and NADPH**. This page uses NADP and reduced NADP.

</Callout>

<Figure name="c1-3-thylakoid" caption="The light-dependent reactions in a thylakoid membrane. Solid purple arrows show non-cyclic electron flow; the dashed arrow shows cyclic flow." />

### Thylakoids are systems

A thylakoid works as a system because its parts interact and depend on each other. Photosystem II supplies excited electrons to the carrier chain; the chain supplies energy to pump protons; the proton gradient drives ATP synthase; photosystem I supplies electrons to NADP reductase; photosystem II replaces photosystem I's electrons; and water replaces photosystem II's.

| Process | Where in the thylakoid |
|---|---|
| Photolysis of water | photosystem II, on the side facing the thylakoid space |
| Proton pumping | cytochrome complex: from stroma into thylakoid space |
| ATP synthesis | ATP synthase: H⁺ flows from thylakoid space to stroma; ATP made in the stroma |
| NADP reduction | NADP reductase, on the stroma side of the membrane |

Features of the thylakoid that make this work: the membrane is impermeable to H⁺; the thylakoid space is a small volume, so the H⁺ concentration rises quickly; the phospholipid bilayer holds the hydrophobic photosystems; and the components are held in the right order and orientation.

</Section>

<Section id="calvin" title="The Calvin cycle" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.15 C1.3.16 C1.3.17">

The **light-independent reactions** form the **Calvin cycle** in the stroma. They do not use light directly, but they depend on ATP and reduced NADP from the thylakoids.

### Carbon fixation by Rubisco

CO₂ diffuses into the stroma and is fixed by the enzyme **Rubisco** (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase), which joins it to **RuBP** (ribulose bisphosphate, 5C). The six-carbon product is unstable and splits at once into two molecules of **glycerate 3-phosphate** (GP, 3C):

> RuBP + CO₂ → 2 glycerate 3-phosphate

Rubisco is the **most abundant enzyme on Earth**. It makes up a large share of the protein in leaves because each molecule works relatively slowly and is not effective at low CO₂ concentrations, so high concentrations are needed in the stroma for a useful rate of fixation.

### Synthesis of triose phosphate

Glycerate 3-phosphate is an acid. It is **reduced** to **triose phosphate** (TP), a three-carbon sugar phosphate. **Reduced NADP** supplies the hydrogen and **ATP** supplies the energy, one of each per GP.

### Regeneration of RuBP

For the cycle to continue, every RuBP used must be replaced. In a series of reactions (details not needed), **five TP are converted into three RuBP** (15 carbon atoms in each case), using ATP.

<Anim topic="c1-3" id="calvin" />

| For each... | CO₂ fixed | GP made | ATP used | Reduced NADP used | TP made | TP recycled | TP available |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 turns | 3 | 6 | 9 | 6 | 6 | 5 | 1 |
| 6 turns (one glucose) | 6 | 12 | 18 | 12 | 12 | 10 | 2 |

If glucose is the product, **five-sixths** of all the TP made must be converted back to RuBP.

<Callout type="exam">

Name the molecules exactly: **RuBP** (5C), **glycerate 3-phosphate** (3C), **triose phosphate** (3C). Say that ATP is used twice in the cycle: in reducing GP to TP and in regenerating RuBP. Reduced NADP is used only in the reduction step.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="products" title="Making other carbon compounds" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.18">

**All** the carbon in the compounds of a photosynthesising organism is fixed by Rubisco in the Calvin cycle. Every other carbon compound is made by metabolic pathways that start from a Calvin cycle intermediate (pathway details are not needed).

- **Carbohydrates**: two TP can be combined to make glucose; glucose and fructose make **sucrose** for transport in phloem; glucose is built into **starch** for storage and **cellulose** for cell walls.
- **Amino acids**: made from intermediates such as glycerate 3-phosphate. They need **nitrogen**, absorbed by roots as nitrate or ammonium ions, and some also need **sulfur**, absorbed as sulfate.
- **Lipids**: fatty acids and glycerol are made from Calvin cycle products.
- **Nucleotides, ATP and phospholipids** need **phosphorus**, absorbed as phosphate ions.

These **mineral nutrients** supply all the elements other than carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Magnesium, for example, is needed for every chlorophyll molecule. This is why plants grown without nitrate show stunted growth even in bright light and high CO₂.

</Section>

<Section id="interdependence" title="Interdependence of the two stages" level="AHL" covers="C1.3.19">

The light-dependent and light-independent reactions depend on each other:

- The Calvin cycle needs a continuous supply of **ATP and reduced NADP** from the thylakoids.
- The thylakoids need a continuous supply of **ADP, Pi and NADP**, which the Calvin cycle regenerates.

**Lack of light.** With no light, no electrons are excited, so the light-dependent reactions stop. The ATP and reduced NADP in the stroma are used up within seconds, so glycerate 3-phosphate can no longer be reduced to triose phosphate. GP accumulates while RuBP is used up and not regenerated, and the Calvin cycle stops.

**Lack of CO₂.** With no CO₂, Rubisco cannot fix carbon and the Calvin cycle stops, so reduced NADP and ATP are no longer used. Soon all the NADP is reduced, leaving photosystem I with no acceptor for its excited electrons. The electron carriers stay reduced, so photosystem II cannot pass its excited electrons on. Photolysis stops and no oxygen is released, even in bright light.

<Callout type="exam">

Data from Calvin's experiments are often set: when the **light is switched off**, GP rises and RuBP falls; when **CO₂ is removed**, RuBP rises and GP falls. Explain each change from which reaction has stopped and which can still continue.

</Callout>

The interdependence also explains why the stroma and thylakoids are so close together in the chloroplast (see <TopicLink code="B3.1" /> for how leaves supply CO₂ and light to them).

</Section>
