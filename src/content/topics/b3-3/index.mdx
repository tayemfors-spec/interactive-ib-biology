<Section id="movement" title="Movement in living organisms" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.1">

Movement is one of the functions of life, and every organism has adaptations for it, even if it never leaves the spot where it lives. It helps to separate three kinds of movement.

- **Movement inside cells**: cytoplasm streams, chromosomes are pulled apart in mitosis and vesicles are carried along the cytoskeleton by motor proteins.
- **Internal movements of the body**: the heart pumps, peristalsis moves food along the gut and the ribs and diaphragm ventilate the lungs.
- **Locomotion**: moving the whole organism from place to place.

A species that can move from place to place is **motile**; one that stays fixed in one position is **sessile**.

### A motile and a sessile example

| | Arctic tern (*Sterna paradisaea*) — motile | Acorn barnacle (*Semibalanus balanoides*) — sessile |
|---|---|---|
| How it lives | A seabird that flies between the Arctic and the Antarctic each year | A crustacean cemented by its head to rocks on the shore, inside a case of hard plates |
| Movement | Long, narrow wings and powerful flight muscles for efficient, long-distance flight; it also dives to catch fish | The adult cannot move from place to place; it opens its plates and sweeps feathery legs (cirri) through the water to filter out food |
| Dispersal | By flight | By motile larvae, which swim in the plankton before settling and cementing themselves down |

Plants are also sessile, anchored by their roots, but they still move parts of their bodies. Young sunflower shoots turn to follow the sun across the sky, and the leaflets of *Mimosa pudica* fold within seconds of being touched.

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to compare a motile and a sessile organism, name both species and give a **specific** adaptation for each: for example, the barnacle's cirri for filter feeding while fixed, and its motile larva for dispersal.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sliding-filaments" title="The sliding filament model" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.2">

<Viewer3D topic="b3-3" id="muscle" />

Skeletal muscle is made of **muscle fibres**. Each fibre is a single long cell formed by the fusion of many cells, so it is **multinucleate**. It is surrounded by a plasma membrane called the **sarcolemma** and is packed with parallel **myofibrils**, with many **mitochondria** between them and a network of **sarcoplasmic reticulum** (SR) wrapped round each one. Switch the 3D model to **Muscle to myofibril** to see these levels of organization.

### The sarcomere

Each myofibril is a chain of **sarcomeres**, joined end to end at **Z-lines** (Z-discs). Two kinds of protein filament overlap in a regular pattern:

- **Thin filaments** of **actin**, fixed to a Z-line at one end, with the regulatory proteins tropomyosin and troponin.
- **Thick filaments** of **myosin**, in the centre, held together at the **M-line**. Myosin heads project from them towards the actin, except in a bare zone in the middle. In cross-section each myosin filament is surrounded by six actin filaments.

The overlap gives the bands seen in micrographs:

| Band | What it contains | During contraction |
|---|---|---|
| **A-band** (dark) | The full length of the myosin filaments, including where actin overlaps | Stays the same length |
| **I-band** (light) | Actin only; a Z-line runs through its middle | Shortens |
| **H-zone** | Myosin only, in the centre of the A-band | Shortens, and may disappear |

### How a sarcomere shortens

When a muscle fibre is stimulated, the **myosin heads** bind to actin, forming **cross-bridges**, and pull the actin filaments towards the M-line. The Z-lines are pulled closer together. Neither filament changes length: they **slide** past each other and their overlap increases. Thousands of sarcomeres in series shorten together, so the whole muscle shortens.

<Callout type="exam" title="Drawing a sarcomere">

A labelled diagram should show and name: **Z-lines** at both ends; **actin** filaments attached to the Z-lines; **myosin** filaments in the centre, with **heads**, overlapping the actin; the **M-line**; and brackets for the **A-band**, **I-band** and **H-zone**. Adding **titin** joining myosin to the Z-lines shows extra understanding. For a contracted sarcomere, keep the myosin (and A-band) the same length and draw the Z-lines closer. Compare with the **Exam diagram** view of the 3D model.

</Callout>

### Switching contraction on: calcium

A nerve impulse arriving at a neuromuscular junction triggers an action potential in the sarcolemma. This spreads deep into the fibre along **T-tubules**, which makes the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** release calcium ions (Ca²⁺) into the fluid around the myofibrils.

At rest, **tropomyosin** lies over the myosin-binding sites on actin. Ca²⁺ binds to **troponin**, which changes shape and pulls tropomyosin aside, so the binding sites are exposed.

### The cross-bridge cycle

1. A **cocked** myosin head, holding ADP and a phosphate (Pi), binds to an exposed site on actin, forming a cross-bridge.
2. Release of Pi triggers the **power stroke**: the head swivels towards the M-line, sliding the actin a few nanometres. ADP is then released.
3. **ATP binds** to the head, which detaches from actin.
4. The head **hydrolyses ATP** to ADP and Pi, and the energy released re-cocks it, ready to bind a site further along.

<Anim topic="b3-3" id="cross-bridge" />

The heads cycle independently, so at any moment some are attached and pulling while others are detaching, and the filaments slide smoothly, like a team pulling hand over hand on a rope. ATP is needed both to detach the heads and to re-cock them, which is why fibres are packed with mitochondria. After death ATP runs out, the heads stay locked to actin and the muscles become rigid (**rigor mortis**).

### Relaxation

When nerve impulses stop, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR by **active transport**, which also uses ATP. Tropomyosin covers the binding sites again, no new cross-bridges form and the muscle stops exerting force.

<Callout type="exam">

Give the steps in order and name the molecule at each: **Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → myosin binds actin (cross-bridge) → power stroke as Pi and ADP are released → ATP binds, head detaches → ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head.**

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="relaxation" title="Titin and antagonistic muscles" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.3">

### Muscles can only pull

A muscle exerts force only when it **contracts**, and then it can only pull. It cannot push, and a relaxed muscle cannot lengthen itself. So muscles are arranged in **antagonistic pairs** that move a part of the body in opposite directions, and each muscle is stretched back to its full length by the contraction of the other.

- The **biceps** contracts to **flex** (bend) the elbow; the triceps relaxes and is stretched.
- The **triceps** contracts to **extend** (straighten) the elbow; the biceps relaxes and is stretched.

Despite the name, the two muscles work together: their contractions are coordinated so that one relaxes as the other contracts, giving smooth, controlled movement.

<Anim topic="b3-3" id="antagonistic" />

### Titin

**Titin** is the largest protein known: a single chain of over 30,000 amino acids. Each titin molecule runs from a **Z-line** to the **M-line**, alongside a myosin filament. In the I-band it is folded into a springy section that behaves like an elastic band. Titin has three roles:

- It **anchors** each myosin filament to the Z-lines, keeping it in the centre of the sarcomere among its six actin filaments.
- It **prevents overstretching**: it resists more and more strongly as the sarcomere is lengthened, so actin and myosin are not pulled apart.
- It **stores potential energy** when a relaxed sarcomere is stretched by the antagonist. When the stretching force is removed, titin recoils, helping the sarcomere return to its resting length, and this elastic recoil adds to the force of the next contraction.

<Callout type="exam">

"Antagonistic" does not mean the muscles work against each other. Explain that each muscle **can only pull**, so the contraction of one is needed to **lengthen** the other, stretching its titin.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="motor-units" title="Motor units" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.4">

Skeletal muscle contracts only when it is stimulated by a **motor neuron**. The cell bodies of motor neurons lie in the spinal cord (or brainstem), and each axon runs to one muscle, where it divides into many branches.

- Each branch ends at a **neuromuscular junction** with one muscle fibre. This is a synapse: the neurotransmitter **acetylcholine** is released from vesicles, diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors in the sarcolemma, causing depolarization and an action potential in the fibre.
- Each muscle fibre receives a junction from **only one** motor neuron.
- A **motor unit** is one motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it stimulates.

<Figure name="b3-3-motor-unit" level="AHL" caption="Two motor units. The coloured motor neuron forms neuromuscular junctions with four fibres, scattered among the fibres of a second motor unit (grey). Below: one neuromuscular junction, where acetylcholine is released into the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors in the sarcolemma." />

### Why motor units matter

When a motor neuron fires, **all** the fibres in its unit contract together. The nervous system controls the force of a whole muscle by changing **how many** motor units are active (recruitment) and how often they fire.

- Muscles that make fine, precise movements, such as those that move the eyeball or the fingers, have **small** motor units of a few fibres each.
- Large postural or leg muscles, such as the calf muscles, have **large** motor units with a thousand or more fibres, producing big forces with relatively few neurons.

Because the fibres of one unit are spread through the muscle, the pull is even across the whole muscle. For how acetylcholine works at synapses, see <TopicLink code="C2.2" />.

</Section>

<Section id="skeletons" title="Skeletons as anchorage and levers" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.5">

A skeleton is a rigid framework that supports and protects the body. For movement it has two roles: it provides **anchorage** (fixed points for muscles to pull on) and it acts as a set of **levers**.

### Exoskeletons and endoskeletons

| | Exoskeleton | Endoskeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Arthropods (insects, crustaceans, spiders) | Vertebrates |
| Position | Outside the body, covering it | Inside the body |
| Material | Mainly chitin, hardened with proteins (and calcium carbonate in many crustaceans) | Bone and cartilage |
| Muscle attachment | To the inner surface of the skeleton | To the outside of the bones, by tendons |
| Joints | Thin, flexible regions of cuticle between rigid segments | Synovial and other joints between bones |
| Growth | Must be shed (moulting) for the animal to grow | Grows with the body |

<Figure name="b3-3-skeletons" level="AHL" caption="Left: in an insect leg, flexor and extensor muscles inside the tubular exoskeleton bend and straighten the joint. Right: in the human arm, the elbow joint is the fulcrum, the biceps supplies the effort just in front of it, and the forearm and hand are the load." />

### Levers

A lever has a pivot (the **fulcrum**), a force applied to it (the **effort**) and a force it works against (the **load**). In a limb, the joint is the fulcrum, the pull of a muscle at its point of attachment is the effort, and the weight of the limb and anything it holds is the load.

Each muscle has a fixed attachment (the **origin**) and an attachment on the bone it moves (the **insertion**), on the far side of the joint. The biceps is inserted close to the elbow, so a small shortening of the muscle swings the hand through a large distance, quickly, but the muscle must pull with a much larger force than the load. Arthropod limbs work in the same way: each leg segment is a lever, moved by antagonistic flexor and extensor muscles inside it.

</Section>

<Section id="hip-joint" title="Movement at a synovial joint: the hip" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.6">

Where two bones meet there is a joint. In a **synovial joint** the bones can move freely relative to each other. The **hip** is a **ball-and-socket** joint: the rounded head of the **femur** fits into a cup-shaped socket in the **pelvis**.

<Figure name="b3-3-hip" level="AHL" caption="Section through the hip joint. The head of the femur and the socket of the pelvis are covered by smooth cartilage and separated by synovial fluid, held in by the joint capsule. Ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone." />

| Part | Role in the hip joint |
|---|---|
| **Bones** (femur and pelvis) | Rigid levers and anchorage for muscles; the shape of the ball and socket allows movement in many directions |
| **Cartilage** | Smooth, tough tissue covering the head of the femur and lining the socket; it reduces friction and absorbs shocks |
| **Synovial fluid** | Fills the space between the cartilages and lubricates the joint, reducing friction and wear |
| **Joint capsule** | A tough sleeve of tissue enclosing the joint, holding in the synovial fluid; its lining (the synovial membrane) secretes the fluid |
| **Ligaments** | Tough bands of collagen-rich tissue joining bone to bone; they limit movement and prevent dislocation |
| **Muscles** | Contract to move the femur relative to the pelvis, in antagonistic pairs on opposite sides of the joint |
| **Tendons** | Cords of collagen fibres joining muscle to bone, transmitting the force of contraction |

<Callout type="exam">

Name the **femur** and **pelvis**, and give each part a **function**, not just a location. Keep **tendons** (muscle to bone) and **ligaments** (bone to bone) apart — swapping them is a common error.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="range-of-motion" title="Measuring range of motion" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.7">

The **range of motion** of a joint is how far it can move in each direction. It depends on the shape of the bones, the ligaments and the muscles around the joint.

- The **elbow** and **knee** are **hinge** joints: they allow flexion (bending) and extension (straightening) in **one plane** only. (Turning the palm up and down happens at the joints between the radius and ulna, not at the hinge.)
- The **hip** is a **ball-and-socket** joint, so it moves in **three planes**: flexion and extension, abduction (away from the midline) and adduction (towards or across it), and medial and lateral rotation.

<Figure name="b3-3-rom" level="AHL" caption="Range of motion of the hip in three planes. Angles are measured from the anatomical position (0°); the values are typical for young adults and vary between people." />

### How to measure a joint angle

- A **goniometer** is a protractor with two arms. Place its pivot over the centre of the joint, line the fixed arm up with the stationary bone (such as the upper arm or trunk) and the moving arm with the moving bone, then read the angle at the end of the movement. Step 3 of the antagonistic muscles animation shows this for the elbow.
- **Computer analysis of images**: photograph or film the movement, mark landmarks on the body (for example the shoulder, elbow and wrist) and measure the angle between the lines with image-analysis software.

Joint angles are usually recorded from the **anatomical position**, so a straight elbow is 0° of flexion. If the angle *between* the upper arm and forearm is measured instead, subtract it from 180°: an included angle of 35° is 145° of flexion.

<Callout type="tip" title="Designing a fair comparison">

Use the same starting position and landmarks each time, repeat each measurement at least three times and calculate a mean, and state whether the movement was **active** (by the subject's own muscles) or **passive** (moved by someone else). Then compare planes, joints, left and right sides, or groups of people.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="intercostals" title="Intercostal muscles: antagonists inside the body" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.8">

Antagonistic muscles also cause **internal** movements. Between each pair of ribs are two layers of **intercostal muscle**, attached to the rib above and the rib below. Their fibres run at different angles, so they move the ribcage in opposite directions.

- **External intercostals**: fibres run downwards and forwards. When they contract, the ribs are pulled **up and out**, the volume of the thorax increases, the pressure falls and air flows into the lungs (inspiration).
- **Internal intercostals**: fibres run downwards and backwards, roughly at right angles to the external fibres. When they contract, the ribs are pulled **down and in**, decreasing the volume of the thorax and forcing air out (forced expiration).

<Figure name="b3-3-intercostals" level="AHL" caption="The external and internal intercostals are an antagonistic pair. Contraction of the external layer raises the ribs (left); contraction of the internal layer lowers them (right). The layer that is not contracting is stretched." />

Whichever layer contracts, it **stretches** the other, which is relaxed. The titin in the stretched sarcomeres stores **potential energy**, released as elastic recoil when that layer next contracts. In quiet breathing, expiration is mainly passive; the internal intercostals are used when breathing out forcefully, for example during exercise. Ventilation is covered in <TopicLink code="B3.1" />.

</Section>

<Section id="locomotion" title="Reasons for locomotion" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.9">

Locomotion costs energy, so it must bring benefits that outweigh the cost. Four main reasons are listed below, each with an example.

<Figure name="b3-3-locomotion" level="AHL" caption="Four reasons for locomotion, each with an example: foraging, escaping danger, searching for a mate and migration." />

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| **Foraging for food** | Bottlenose dolphins (*Tursiops truncatus*) swim after shoals of fish, sometimes working together to herd them |
| **Escaping from danger** | A great scallop (*Pecten maximus*) that detects a predatory starfish claps its two valves together repeatedly, jetting out water and swimming away |
| **Searching for a mate** | Male emperor moths (*Saturnia pavonia*) fly upwind, following the scent (pheromone) released by a female, which can be detected from far away |
| **Migration** | Arctic terns breed in the Arctic during the northern summer, then fly to the Antarctic for the southern summer — a round trip of tens of thousands of kilometres each year |

Migration lets an animal use resources in places that are only suitable for part of the year, such as long summer days with plentiful food at high latitudes.

</Section>

<Section id="marine-mammals" title="Swimming adaptations of marine mammals" level="AHL" covers="B3.3.10">

Marine mammals, such as whales, dolphins and seals, evolved from land mammals. Water is much denser and more viscous than air, so moving through it needs different adaptations. The **bottlenose dolphin** (*Tursiops truncatus*) shows them clearly.

<Figure name="b3-3-dolphin" level="AHL" caption="Swimming adaptations of a bottlenose dolphin: a streamlined body, flippers, a dorsal fin, horizontal tail flukes beaten up and down, a blowhole on top of the head, and blubber under smooth, hairless skin." />

### Streamlining

- The body is **fusiform**: widest near the front and tapering towards the tail, which minimizes drag.
- The outline is smooth: there are no hind limbs and no external ears, the skin is hairless and a layer of blubber fills out the shape (and adds buoyancy).

### Limbs and tail

- The forelimbs are **flippers**. They still contain the bones of an arm and hand, but these are short, flattened and enclosed in one paddle, used for steering and braking.
- The tail ends in a **fluke**: two horizontal lobes with no bones inside. Muscles of the back and tail beat the fluke **up and down**, which provides the thrust. (A fish's tail fin is vertical and moves from side to side.)
- A **dorsal fin** on the back stabilizes the body and prevents rolling.

### Breathing between dives

- The nostril (in whales, one or two) has moved to the top of the head as the **blowhole**, so the animal can breathe with only the top of its head out of the water. A muscular plug or flap keeps it closed during a dive.
- The airway from the blowhole to the lungs is separate from the mouth and oesophagus, so the animal can open its mouth underwater to catch prey without water entering the lungs.
- The airways are reinforced with cartilage (and smooth muscle), so they stay open for very rapid exhalation and inhalation at the surface; each breath replaces most of the air in the lungs.

<Callout type="note">

Not all marine mammals swim the same way. True seals sweep their hind flippers from side to side, and sea lions row with their large fore-flippers. The up-and-down fluke stroke is typical of whales and dolphins (and manatees).

</Callout>
