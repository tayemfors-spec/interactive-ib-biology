<Section id="open-systems" title="Ecosystems: open systems powered by sunlight" level="SL" covers="C4.2.1 C4.2.2">

An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with its abiotic environment. Ecosystems are **open systems**: both **energy** and **matter** cross their boundaries.

- **Energy** enters mainly as light and leaves mainly as heat.
- **Matter** enters and leaves too: CO₂ and O₂ diffuse in and out, water flows through, seeds and pollen blow in, and animals migrate.

In a **closed system** only energy can pass in or out; matter stays inside. A sealed bottle garden is close to a closed system: light enters and heat leaves, but the same atoms must be recycled inside. The Earth as a whole is also nearly closed for matter but open for energy.

### Sunlight as the main energy source

In almost every ecosystem, **sunlight** is the source of energy. Photosynthesis by plants, algae and cyanobacteria converts light energy into chemical energy in carbon compounds, and every other organism depends on these compounds directly or indirectly. There are exceptions:

| Ecosystem | Where the energy comes from |
|---|---|
| **Caves** | Little or no light. Most energy arrives as dead organic matter carried in from outside (leaves washed in by water, droppings of bats that feed outside). A few caves, such as Movile Cave in Romania, are supported by chemoautotrophic bacteria. |
| **Deep ocean**, below the depth light reaches | Dead organic matter sinking from the sunlit surface layers ("marine snow"); at hydrothermal vents, chemoautotrophic bacteria that oxidize hydrogen sulfide. |

Organic matter that drifts into a cave or sinks into the deep sea was made by photosynthesis elsewhere, so only the chemoautotroph-based communities are truly independent of sunlight.

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: useful generalizations">

"Most ecosystems are powered by sunlight" is a **generalization**, like a scientific law: it describes a pattern seen in nature but does not explain it. Useful generalizations are based on many observations, are simple to state, allow predictions (a new ecosystem will probably depend on photosynthesis), and have known limits. Exceptions such as vent communities do not make the rule useless, as long as we know where it applies.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="food-chains" title="Food chains, food webs and trophic levels" level="SL" covers="C4.2.3 C4.2.4 C4.2.10">

A **food chain** is a sequence of organisms in which each one feeds on the one before it. When a consumer eats an organism at the previous stage, the **chemical energy** in that organism's carbon compounds passes to the consumer.

> oak leaves → winter moth caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk

### Arrows show the direction of transfer

The arrows mean "is eaten by" and point **from food to feeder**: the direction in which **energy and biomass** move. Most consumers eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator, so the chains in a community link up into a **food web**.

<Figure name="c4-2-food-web" caption="A simplified food web for a British oak woodland. Organisms are placed in rows by their usual trophic level; arrows point from food to feeder. Real woodland webs contain hundreds of species and links." />

### Trophic levels

| Trophic level | How it gets carbon compounds | Example from the web |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer** | Makes its own (autotroph) | oak, bramble, grasses |
| **Primary consumer** | Eats producers | caterpillars, rabbits |
| **Secondary consumer** | Eats primary consumers | blue tit, tawny owl |
| **Tertiary consumer** | Eats secondary consumers | sparrowhawk |

Many organisms have a **varied diet**, so they occupy **different trophic levels in different food chains**. A red fox is a secondary consumer when it eats a rabbit but a primary consumer when it eats blackberries. A wood mouse eating acorns is a primary consumer, but when it eats caterpillars it is a secondary consumer, so a tawny owl that eats that mouse is acting as a tertiary consumer.

<Callout type="exam" title="Building a food web for your local community">

Use real organisms from one habitat you know (a pond, woodland, rocky shore or school field). Put producers at the bottom and each consumer above the food it eats, keep arrows pointing to the feeder, and avoid crossing arrows where you can. You may be asked to identify the trophic level of an organism in a named chain — always use the chain given, not the organism's "usual" level.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="autotrophs" title="Autotrophs: photoautotrophs and chemoautotrophs" level="SL" covers="C4.2.6 C4.2.7">

All organisms need carbon compounds. **Autotrophs** make their own from **simple inorganic substances**: CO₂ (or hydrogen carbonate ions in water) plus sources of other elements such as nitrate and phosphate ions. They need an **external energy source** for two things:

1. **Carbon fixation**: converting CO₂ into an organic compound (a reduction reaction).
2. **Anabolic reactions** that build the fixed carbon into larger molecules: polysaccharides, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

Autotrophs differ in where that energy comes from.

### Photoautotrophs

**Photoautotrophs** use **light** as their energy source, through photosynthesis (see <TopicLink code="C1.3" />). Plants, algae and cyanobacteria are photoautotrophs, and they are the producers in almost all ecosystems.

### Chemoautotrophs

**Chemoautotrophs** use energy released by **oxidation reactions** of inorganic substances. Oxidation reactions are exothermic, so they release energy that cells can use. Only some bacteria and archaea are chemoautotrophs.

**Iron-oxidizing bacteria**, such as *Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans*, live in water that contains dissolved iron(II) ions, for example acidic drainage from mines. They oxidize Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺:

> Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻

The electrons removed pass along an electron transport chain in the bacterium's plasma membrane. The energy released is used to make **ATP** (by chemiosmosis) and **reduced NAD**, which are then used to fix CO₂ into carbon compounds. The Fe³⁺ often forms orange-brown deposits of iron hydroxide on stream beds. Each Fe²⁺ yields only a small amount of energy, so these bacteria must oxidize huge quantities of iron.

<Callout type="exam">

"Chemosynthesis" does not use light **or** heat. Name the substance oxidized (e.g. Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) and say that the energy released is used to synthesize carbon compounds from CO₂.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="heterotrophs" title="Heterotrophs, decomposers and cell respiration" level="SL" covers="C4.2.5 C4.2.8 C4.2.9">

**Heterotrophs** obtain carbon compounds from other organisms. Large molecules such as **proteins, polysaccharides and nucleic acids** cannot be absorbed into cells, so they are first **digested** into small, soluble molecules (amino acids, sugars, nucleotides). These are absorbed and then **assimilated**: used to build the heterotroph's own carbon compounds. For example, a rabbit digests grass proteins to amino acids and then makes rabbit proteins from them.

- **Internal digestion**: animals take food into the gut and digest it with enzymes there; unicellular consumers such as *Paramecium* digest food inside vacuoles.
- **External digestion**: **saprotrophs** (many fungi and bacteria) secrete enzymes onto their food and absorb the products.

### Decomposers

**Decomposers** are saprotrophs that obtain energy and carbon from **dead organic matter**:

- **faeces** (egested, undigested food);
- **dead parts of organisms**, such as fallen leaves, shed skin, hair and moulted exoskeletons;
- **whole dead organisms**.

This material still contains chemical energy in its carbon compounds. **Detritivores** such as earthworms and woodlice also feed on it, but they ingest it and digest it internally. Without decomposers, dead matter would pile up and the elements in it would be locked away.

### Both autotrophs and heterotrophs respire

All cells need a continuous supply of **ATP** (for anabolic reactions, active transport, movement and, in birds and mammals, keeping warm). In **both autotrophs and heterotrophs**, this ATP comes from **cell respiration** (see <TopicLink code="C1.2" />): carbon compounds such as glucose and fatty acids are **oxidized**, releasing energy that is used to phosphorylate ADP. Plants respire all the time, in the light and the dark; photosynthesis does not replace respiration.

| | Autotrophs | Heterotrophs |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon source | CO₂ / HCO₃⁻ (inorganic) | carbon compounds from other organisms |
| Energy source for making carbon compounds | light or oxidation of inorganic substances | energy from carbon compounds eaten |
| Release energy by cell respiration? | yes | yes |

</Section>

<Section id="energy-losses" title="Energy losses between trophic levels" level="SL" covers="C4.2.12 C4.2.13">

Only a small fraction of the energy in one trophic level reaches the next, typically around **10%**, though values between about 5% and 20% are common. There are three main reasons:

1. **Not all of the organisms are eaten.** Some die of other causes, and some parts (roots, bark, bones, fur) are left uneaten. Their energy passes to decomposers and detritivores instead.
2. **Not all of what is eaten is digested and absorbed.** Indigestible material such as cellulose, lignin or bone is **egested** in faeces, and its energy also goes to decomposers.
3. **Cell respiration.** Much of what is absorbed is oxidized to CO₂ and water to release energy. That energy is used by the organism and ends up as **heat**; the carbon compounds respired can never pass to the next level.

<Anim topic="c4-2" id="energy-flow" />

### Heat is produced at every stage

Energy transfers in cells are never 100% efficient. Heat is produced:

- **when ATP is made** in cell respiration, because only part of the energy released by oxidizing glucose is captured in ATP;
- **when ATP is used** to do work in cells (muscle contraction, active transport, synthesis), because these processes are also inefficient.

This happens in **autotrophs and heterotrophs alike**, and in decomposers. Heat radiates away into the environment and **cannot be converted back into chemical energy** by organisms, so energy cannot be recycled: it flows through the ecosystem once. Ecosystems therefore need a continuous input of energy, from sunlight in most cases.

**Decomposers and detritivores** are not usually drawn as part of food chains, but energy that "leaves" a chain in uneaten matter and faeces passes to them, and they release it as heat in their own respiration.

<Callout type="exam">

For "explain the energy losses between trophic levels", give all three causes (uneaten parts, egestion of undigested material, respiration releasing heat), and state that heat cannot be reused. Never say energy is "used up" or "destroyed".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="pyramids" title="Energy pyramids and the length of food chains" level="SL" covers="C4.2.11 C4.2.14">

An **energy pyramid** shows how much energy each trophic level of an ecosystem gains per year, so it represents both the energy transferred along the food chain and the energy lost between levels.

<Sim topic="c4-2" id="pyramid" />

### Constructing an energy pyramid from data

H. T. Odum measured energy flow in **Silver Springs**, Florida. Converted to kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹:

| Trophic level | Energy (kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| Producers (gross production) | 87,100 |
| Primary consumers | 14,100 |
| Secondary consumers | 1,600 |
| Tertiary consumers | 88 |

The **transfer efficiency** from primary to secondary consumers is 1,600 ÷ 14,100 × 100 = **11.3%**, so 88.7% of the energy was lost. The other transfers were about 16% and 5.5%: the "10% rule" is only a rough average.

<Callout type="exam" title="Drawing an energy pyramid: must include">

- **Stepped** horizontal bars, one per trophic level — not a smooth triangle.
- **Producers at the bottom**, then primary, secondary and tertiary consumers.
- Each bar **labelled** with its trophic level and energy value.
- **Units**: kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ (energy per unit area per year).
- Bar lengths **to scale** where possible. Energy pyramids can never be inverted.

</Callout>

### Why food chains are short

Because most energy is lost at each step, the energy available falls geometrically: with 10% efficiency, the fourth level receives only 0.1% of what producers gained. After four or five levels there is not enough energy to support a viable population of another level, so food chains rarely have more than **four or five trophic levels**.

At each successive level there are **fewer organisms, or smaller ones**, so there is **less biomass**. However, the **energy content per unit mass** is **not** lower: a gram of hawk contains about as much energy as a gram of blue tit. The top levels simply have less biomass. This is why top predators are rare and need large territories.

</Section>

<Section id="production" title="Primary and secondary production" level="SL" covers="C4.2.15 C4.2.16">

**Production** is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass, which happens when organisms **grow** or **reproduce**. It is a rate, measured as **mass of carbon per unit area per unit time**, usually **g m⁻² yr⁻¹**.

### Primary production

**Primary production** is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by **autotrophs**.

- **Gross primary production (GPP)**: the total carbon compounds made by photosynthesis.
- **Net primary production (NPP)** = GPP − carbon compounds used by the producers in their own respiration. NPP is the biomass available to consumers and decomposers.

**Biomes vary** in their capacity to accumulate biomass. Production is highest where conditions for photosynthesis are good all year: warm temperatures, plenty of water and light, a long growing season and enough mineral nutrients. It is lowest where one factor is severely limiting: water in deserts, temperature and season length in tundra, mineral nutrients in the open ocean.

<Figure name="c4-2-production" caption="Approximate mean net primary production of some biomes (dry biomass, after Whittaker and Likens; carbon is roughly 45% of dry mass). Values vary widely within each biome." />

### Secondary production

**Secondary production** is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by **heterotrophs** (consumers and decomposers). It is always **lower than primary production** in an ecosystem because, at every trophic level, a large part of the carbon compounds is **oxidized to CO₂ and water in cell respiration** and so is lost from biomass. Uneaten and undigested material also limits what consumers can turn into their own tissue.

<Callout type="exam">

Production questions often ask for units. Always include area **and** time (g m⁻² yr⁻¹). GPP − respiration = NPP; there is more GPP than NPP, and more primary than secondary production.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="carbon-cycle" title="The carbon cycle, sinks and sources" level="SL" covers="C4.2.17 C4.2.18">

Unlike energy, **carbon is recycled**. A carbon cycle diagram shows **pools** (boxes: where carbon is stored) and **fluxes** (arrows: transfers between pools). The three fluxes caused directly by organisms are:

- **photosynthesis**: CO₂ from air or water is fixed into carbon compounds by producers;
- **feeding**: carbon compounds pass to consumers, and to decomposers via dead organic matter;
- **cell respiration**: all organisms oxidize carbon compounds, returning CO₂ to the air or water.

<Anim topic="c4-2" id="carbon-cycle" />

<Figure name="c4-2-carbon-cycle-exam" caption="Exam-style carbon cycle. Boxes are pools of carbon; arrows are fluxes. In aquatic ecosystems the inorganic pool is dissolved CO₂ and hydrogen carbonate ions." />

<Callout type="exam" title="Carbon cycle diagram: must include">

- Pools: **CO₂ in the atmosphere** (or dissolved in water), **producers**, **consumers**, **decomposers**/dead organic matter, and **fossil fuels**/peat.
- Arrows labelled **photosynthesis**, **feeding**, **death/egestion**, **cell respiration** from **every** group of organisms (including decomposers), **fossilization** and **combustion**.
- Arrows pointing the right way: photosynthesis into producers, respiration back to CO₂.

</Callout>

### Carbon sinks and carbon sources

For a whole ecosystem, the rates of photosynthesis and respiration are not always equal:

| | Carbon sink | Carbon source |
|---|---|---|
| Balance | photosynthesis **>** respiration | respiration **>** photosynthesis |
| Net CO₂ flux | uptake from the atmosphere | release to the atmosphere |
| Examples | a young, growing forest; a peat bog, where waterlogged, acidic, anaerobic conditions slow decomposition so dead matter accumulates | a drained peatland, where peat is decomposed; a forest after felling or burning |

</Section>

<Section id="combustion-keeling" title="Combustion and the Keeling curve" level="SL" covers="C4.2.19 C4.2.20">

### Combustion releases stored carbon

Carbon can stay locked in some pools for very long periods. These stores formed at very different times:

- **Biomass** (wood): carbon fixed over years to centuries.
- **Peat**: partly decomposed plant matter in waterlogged bogs, mostly accumulated over the last 10,000 years or so, and still forming.
- **Coal**: from plant matter buried in swamps, much of it about 300–360 million years ago (the Carboniferous).
- **Oil and natural gas**: mostly from the remains of marine plankton buried in sediments over tens to hundreds of millions of years.

**Combustion** oxidizes these carbon compounds and releases **CO₂** into the atmosphere. Fires can start **naturally**, when lightning strikes dry vegetation or peat, but **human activities** — burning coal, oil and gas for energy, and burning forests to clear land — have **greatly increased** the rate of combustion since the Industrial Revolution.

### The Keeling curve

In 1958 Charles David Keeling began measuring atmospheric CO₂ at **Mauna Loa Observatory, Hawaii**, far from local sources of pollution. The record, the **Keeling curve**, shows two patterns.

<Sim topic="c4-2" id="keeling" />

1. **Annual fluctuation (the zig-zag).** CO₂ falls from about **May to October** and rises from about **October to May**, by roughly 6 ppm at Mauna Loa. Most land, and so most vegetation, is in the **Northern Hemisphere**. In the northern summer, photosynthesis exceeds respiration and removes CO₂; in the northern autumn and winter, many plants lose their leaves or stop growing while respiration (especially by decomposers) continues, so respiration exceeds photosynthesis.
2. **Long-term trend.** Each year's rise is not fully reversed, so concentration climbs: from about **316 ppm in 1959 to over 420 ppm in the 2020s** (before the Industrial Revolution it was about 280 ppm). This is due mainly to **combustion of fossil fuels**, with deforestation adding to it. The rise has **accelerated**, from under 1 ppm per year in the 1960s to over 2 ppm per year recently.

<Callout type="exam">

When analysing the Keeling curve, link each pattern to processes: the **zig-zag** to the seasonal balance of **photosynthesis and respiration** in the Northern Hemisphere; the **upward trend** to **combustion** (fossil fuels, deforestation). Quote values with units (ppm) and a time period.

</Callout>

The consequences of rising CO₂, including climate change and ocean acidification, are covered in <TopicLink code="D4.3" />.

</Section>

<Section id="recycling" title="Interdependence and the recycling of elements" level="SL" covers="C4.2.21 C4.2.22">

### Photosynthesis and respiration depend on each other

**Aerobic respiration** depends on **oxygen** in the atmosphere (or dissolved in water), and almost all of that oxygen was released by **photosynthesis**, when water is split. In turn, **photosynthesis** depends on **carbon dioxide** that is released by **cell respiration** (and combustion). Autotrophs also respire, but the overall exchange links autotrophs and heterotrophs.

<Figure name="c4-2-interdependence" caption="Oxygen from photosynthesis supports aerobic respiration, and carbon dioxide from respiration supports photosynthesis." />

The fluxes are huge: on land alone, photosynthesis fixes **more than 100 gigatonnes** (10¹⁷ g) of carbon each year, and a similar amount is returned by respiration. This makes it one of the largest interactions between autotrophs and heterotrophs on Earth.

### All elements are recycled

Carbon is not special. **Every element** that organisms use is recycled: C, H, O, N, P and S in organic molecules, and ions such as K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ and Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺. The amount of each on Earth is finite, yet none has run out in over three billion years of life, because:

- autotrophs absorb elements from the abiotic environment as **inorganic** ions and molecules (e.g. CO₂, NO₃⁻, PO₄³⁻);
- the elements pass along food chains in carbon compounds, or are absorbed by heterotrophs as ions;
- **decomposers** digest dead organic matter and release the elements back into soil, water and air as inorganic substances, which producers can absorb again.

Atoms are not changed by any of these steps, so they can be reused indefinitely. Details of the nitrogen cycle and other nutrient cycles are not required. For how populations and communities interact, see <TopicLink code="C4.1" />.

<Callout type="exam">

To answer "why can matter be recycled but energy cannot?": elements are returned to the abiotic environment in inorganic form by decomposers and reabsorbed by producers; energy is converted to heat, which organisms cannot convert back to chemical energy.

</Callout>

</Section>
