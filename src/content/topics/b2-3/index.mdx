<Section id="differentiation" title="From zygote to specialized cells" level="SL" covers="B2.3.1">

A human body contains hundreds of different types of cell, yet all of them come from one cell. **Fertilization** is the fusion of a haploid sperm with a haploid egg, giving a single diploid cell, the **zygote**. The zygote divides repeatedly by **mitosis** to form an **embryo**. Because mitosis produces genetically identical cells, every cell of the early embryo has the same genes as the zygote, and at first all the cells are **unspecialized**.

### Differentiation

As the embryo develops, groups of cells follow different developmental pathways and become **specialized** for particular functions: nerve cells, muscle cells, red blood cells and so on. This process is **differentiation**. It does not involve losing genes. Each cell type **expresses** (transcribes and translates) only some of its genes, for example the genes for haemoglobin in red blood cell precursors or for actin and myosin in muscle cells, while its other genes stay switched off.

### How do cells know what to become?

In an early-stage embryo, some cells release signalling chemicals called **morphogens**. A morphogen diffuses away from its source, so a **concentration gradient** forms: high near the source, lower further away. Cells detect the concentration around them, and different concentrations switch on different genes, often genes for transcription factors that control many other genes. A cell's position in the gradient therefore decides which pathway of differentiation it follows.

- Gradients set up the main body axes, such as head to tail (anterior to posterior). In fruit fly embryos, a gradient of the protein Bicoid from the head end helps determine where the head and thorax form.
- In vertebrate embryos, a gradient of the signalling protein Sonic hedgehog across the developing spinal cord makes cells at different distances from its source develop into different types of neuron.

<Section id="stem-cells" title="Stem cells and potency" level="SL" covers="B2.3.2 B2.3.4">

A **stem cell** is an unspecialized cell with two key properties:

1. **Self-renewal**: it can divide again and again, in principle without limit, to produce more stem cells.
2. **Differentiation along different pathways**: its descendants can develop into more than one type of specialized cell.

Often a stem cell divides so that one daughter cell stays a stem cell and the other goes on to differentiate. This keeps a supply of stem cells while also producing new specialized cells. The cells of an early embryo are all stem cells; in an adult, only a small fraction of cells are stem cells, but they allow tissues to grow, be maintained and be repaired.

### Levels of potency

**Potency** describes how many different cell types a stem cell can become. It narrows as an embryo develops.

| Potency | Can become | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Totipotent** | Any cell type, including the placenta and other membranes outside the embryo | The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions |
| **Pluripotent** | Any of the cell types of the body, but not the placenta | Embryonic stem cells from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst (about 5 days after fertilization) |
| **Multipotent** | A limited range of closely related cell types | Adult stem cells, such as those in bone marrow that make all the types of blood cell |

Cells in early-stage animal embryos are therefore **totipotent**, but they soon become **pluripotent** as the embryo forms a blastocyst, and stem cells in adult tissues are **multipotent**.

<Figure name="b2-3-potency" caption="Potency narrows during development. Totipotent cells of the very early embryo can form a whole organism; pluripotent embryonic stem cells can form any body cell; multipotent adult stem cells, found in niches such as the bone marrow and hair follicles, form only a few related cell types." />

<Callout type="exam">

When asked to distinguish the three terms, give the **range of cell types** for each and an **example**, and use the placenta as the dividing line between totipotent and pluripotent.

</Callout>

<Section id="niches" title="Stem cell niches in adults" level="SL" covers="B2.3.3">

Adult stem cells are not scattered at random. Each type is found in a particular place in its tissue, called its **stem cell niche**. The niche is the microenvironment around the stem cells: neighbouring cells, the extracellular matrix and the blood supply. It provides the signals that control what the stem cells do. A niche can:

- **maintain** the stem cells, keeping them inactive (dormant) and undifferentiated for long periods; or
- **promote proliferation and differentiation**, making them divide rapidly and produce specialized cells when more are needed.

### Two examples

| Niche | Stem cells | What they produce |
|---|---|---|
| **Bone marrow**, in the spongy bone of the pelvis, sternum, ribs, vertebrae and the ends of long bones such as the femur | Haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells | All types of blood cell: red blood cells, the white blood cells and the cells that make platelets. About two million red blood cells are made every second. A rich blood supply brings oxygen and nutrients. |
| **Hair follicles** in the skin | Stem cells in the bulge, a region of the follicle wall below the sebaceous gland | New hair at the start of each growth phase (their descendants divide rapidly in the bulb at the base of the follicle), and new epidermis when the skin is wounded |

Most of the time the hair follicle stem cells are inactive. Signals from cells at the base of the follicle activate them at the start of a new growth phase, which is why hair grows in cycles.

<Callout type="note" title="Why niches matter in medicine">

Bone marrow transplants work because haematopoietic stem cells from a donor can settle in the patient's marrow niche and then produce all the blood cell types for life. Understanding the signals in niches is part of the challenge of growing stem cells for therapies.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="cell-size" title="Cell size as an aspect of specialization" level="SL" covers="B2.3.5">

Human cells vary greatly in size, and size is part of how each type is specialized for its function. Most are between about 5 and 30 µm across, but there are striking exceptions.

| Cell | Approximate size | How size relates to function |
|---|---|---|
| **Sperm** (male gamete) | Head about 3 µm wide; whole cell 50–60 µm long | Small and streamlined, with very little cytoplasm, so it can swim fast to the egg |
| **Red blood cell** | About 7–8 µm across, 2 µm thick | Small enough to squeeze through capillaries; thin, so it has a large SA:V for rapid O₂ uptake and release |
| **White blood cells** | About 7–20 µm across | Lymphocytes are small when inactive but enlarge when activated, producing plasma cells with extra cytoplasm for making antibodies |
| **Egg** (female gamete) | About 110 µm across | Very large, with a big volume of cytoplasm containing food reserves for the early embryo |
| **Motor neuron** | Cell body 20–100 µm; axon up to about 1 m long | A single long axon carries impulses all the way from the spinal cord to a muscle, for example in the foot |
| **Striated muscle fibre** | 10–100 µm wide; millimetres to over 10 cm long | Very large and long, so that one fibre can contract along much of the length of a muscle, giving a strong pull |

<Figure name="b2-3-sizes" caption="The range of human cell sizes on a logarithmic scale. Most cells are tiny, but the egg is visible to the naked eye (just), and neurons and muscle fibres can be very long while staying narrow. Values are typical; real cells vary." />

Notice that the largest cells are large in only one or two dimensions. Neurons and muscle fibres are long but **narrow**, and the egg is large but does very little exchange with its surroundings for its size. The next section explains why.

<Callout type="exam">

When comparing cell sizes, quote a size with its unit and link it to the function: for example, "the egg is about 110 µm across because its cytoplasm contains the food reserves for the early embryo".

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sav" title="Surface area-to-volume ratio limits cell size" level="SL" covers="B2.3.6">

Every cell exchanges materials with its surroundings across its plasma membrane: oxygen and nutrients enter, and carbon dioxide and other waste leave. Two quantities matter:

- the **rate of exchange** depends on the **surface area** of the membrane;
- the **need for exchange** depends on the **volume** of cytoplasm, where the metabolic reactions happen.

### The maths

For a cube of side length L, the surface area is **6L²** (six square faces) and the volume is **L³**, so

**SA:V = 6L² ÷ L³ = 6 / L**

| Side length | Surface area | Volume | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 µm | 6 µm² | 1 µm³ | 6 µm⁻¹ (6 : 1) |
| 2 µm | 24 µm² | 8 µm³ | 3 µm⁻¹ |
| 3 µm | 54 µm² | 27 µm³ | 2 µm⁻¹ |
| 10 µm | 600 µm² | 1,000 µm³ | 0.6 µm⁻¹ |

Doubling the side length multiplies the surface area by 4 but the volume by 8, so the SA:V ratio halves. Volume increases faster than surface area as any object grows.

### Why this limits cell size

As a cell grows, each unit volume of cytoplasm is served by less membrane. Eventually materials cannot enter or leave fast enough to meet the cell's needs, and distances from the membrane to the centre are longer. A cell that reaches a certain size therefore divides, restoring a high SA:V. Heat produced by metabolism is also lost across the surface, so the same reasoning applies to heat.

<Sim topic="b2-3" id="sav" />

### Cubes as a model

Cells are not cubes. The cube is a **model**: a deliberately simplified version of a complex system. It is useful because scale factors work in the same way for any shape: if every length is multiplied by a factor x, all areas are multiplied by x² and all volumes by x³. The simulator shows that a sphere and a flattened cell of the same volume give curves of the same shape, just higher or lower.

<Section id="sav-adaptations" title="Adaptations that increase SA:V" level="AHL" covers="B2.3.7">

Cells specialized for exchange, such as absorption, secretion or excretion, have shapes that give a large area of plasma membrane for their volume. For a given volume a **sphere** has the smallest surface area, so any departure from a sphere increases SA:V. Three common adaptations are:

- **Flattening**: a wide, thin cell has a much larger SA:V than a round one of the same volume, and no part of the cytoplasm is far from the surface. **Erythrocytes** (red blood cells) are flattened **biconcave discs**: about 7–8 µm across but only about 2 µm thick at the rim and about 1 µm at the centre. **Type I pneumocytes** are an extreme example (next section).
- **Microvilli**: finger-like projections of the plasma membrane on the exposed surface of an epithelial cell. Packed closely together they form a **brush border**.
- **Invagination**: inward folding of the plasma membrane to form deep folds, channels or tubules.

### Proximal convoluted tubule cells

The cells lining the **proximal convoluted tubule** of the nephron reabsorb useful substances, such as glucose, amino acids, Na⁺ and water, from the filtrate. They show two of these adaptations:

- The surface facing the lumen has a dense **brush border of microvilli**, greatly increasing the area of membrane containing cotransporters and channels that take substances in from the filtrate.
- The opposite (basal) surface has deep **invaginations** of the plasma membrane, increasing the area for the **Na⁺/K⁺ pumps** and other transporters that move reabsorbed substances out of the cell towards the blood. Pumping Na⁺ out keeps the Na⁺ concentration inside the cell low, which drives sodium-dependent glucose cotransport at the lumen side (<TopicLink code="B2.1" />). Many **mitochondria** lie between the folds, supplying ATP.

<Figure name="b2-3-sav-adaptations" level="AHL" caption="Two ways to raise SA:V. Top: the flattened, biconcave erythrocyte. Bottom: a proximal convoluted tubule cell with microvilli facing the filtrate and basal invaginations packed with mitochondria. Not to scale." />

Match each adaptation to its example: **flattening** — erythrocytes (and type I pneumocytes); **microvilli** and **invagination** — proximal convoluted tubule cells. Say what the extra membrane is for, not just that the area is larger.

</Callout>

<Section id="pneumocytes" title="Type I and type II pneumocytes" level="AHL" covers="B2.3.8">

The wall of each alveolus in the lungs is an **epithelium only one cell thick**. It contains two cell types with very different adaptations.

### Type I pneumocytes

- **Extremely thin** and flattened, with very little cytoplasm and few organelles. Together with the capillary wall, the distance from air to blood is only about 0.5 µm.
- They cover about 95% of the alveolar surface.
- Their thinness keeps the **diffusion distance** for oxygen and carbon dioxide as short as possible, so gas exchange is rapid.

### Type II pneumocytes

- **Rounded (cuboidal)** cells with dense cytoplasm, microvilli on the surface facing the air, and many **lamellar bodies**: secretory vesicles that store **surfactant**.
- Lamellar bodies discharge surfactant by **exocytosis** into the thin film of fluid lining the alveolus. Surfactant, a mixture of phospholipids and proteins, forms a layer at the air–water surface and **reduces surface tension**. This prevents the walls of the alveoli sticking together and the alveoli collapsing when we breathe out.
- The moist lining also lets oxygen dissolve before it diffuses into the blood.
- They cover only about 5% of the surface but are more numerous than type I cells. They can also divide to replace damaged type I cells.

<Figure name="b2-3-pneumocytes" level="AHL" caption="Part of an alveolar wall in section. Thin type I pneumocytes form most of the surface for gas exchange; a type II pneumocyte with lamellar bodies secretes surfactant into the fluid lining. The thickness of the type I cell is exaggerated so that it can be seen." />

### A tissue with more than one cell type

No single cell could be both extremely thin and full of the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and vesicles needed for secretion. The alveolar epithelium is therefore an example of a **tissue in which more than one cell type is needed**, because the overall function of the tissue requires different adaptations. For ventilation and the alveoli themselves, see <TopicLink code="B3.1" />.

<Callout type="exam">

Keep the two cell types separate: **type I** — extremely thin, short diffusion distance, gas exchange; **type II** — lamellar bodies, surfactant secreted by exocytosis, reduces surface tension.

</Callout>

<Section id="muscle" title="Cardiac muscle cells and striated muscle fibres" level="AHL" covers="B2.3.9">

Both cardiac muscle and skeletal (striated) muscle contain **myofibrils**, bundles of actin and myosin filaments arranged in repeating sarcomeres that give the striped (striated) appearance and produce contraction (<TopicLink code="B3.3" />). Both also contain many **mitochondria** to supply ATP. Their other features differ.

| Feature | Cardiac muscle cells | Striated muscle fibres |
|---|---|---|
| Branching | **Branched**, each joined to several others | **Unbranched** cylinders |
| Junctions | Joined end to end by **intercalated discs** with gap junctions | No intercalated discs; fibres lie side by side in bundles |
| Length | About 100 µm (roughly 50–150 µm) | Often several centimetres; some over 10 cm |
| Nuclei | Usually **one**, in the centre | **Many**, at the edge just under the plasma membrane |

<Figure name="b2-3-muscle" level="AHL" caption="Cardiac muscle (top) is a network of short, branched cells, each usually with one nucleus, joined by intercalated discs. A striated muscle fibre (bottom) is a single long, unbranched cylinder with many nuclei. The two drawings are not to the same scale." />

### Suggesting reasons for the differences

- **Branching and intercalated discs**: impulses pass through gap junctions from cell to cell and spread in all directions through the branched network, so the wall of each heart chamber contracts almost at once and squeezes the blood out. The discs also hold the cells together as the heart beats continually throughout life.
- **Length and number of nuclei**: a skeletal fibre forms when many embryonic muscle cells **fuse**, so it can be long enough to pull along much of a muscle's length and is controlled as one unit. Its many nuclei supply the instructions for making proteins throughout its large volume of cytoplasm.

### Is a muscle fibre a cell?

- **For**: it is surrounded by one continuous plasma membrane, it has one continuous cytoplasm and it behaves as a single unit.
- **Against**: it has many nuclei, it is formed by the fusion of many cells and it is far larger than a typical cell. It does not fit the usual idea of a cell as having one nucleus.

Many biologists describe it as a **syncytium**, a structure formed by cell fusion. Either view can be argued, as long as the evidence is used.

<Callout type="exam">

For a "compare" question, write matched pairs of statements (cardiac … whereas striated …) and include **at least one similarity**, such as both having contractile myofibrils.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="gametes" title="Adaptations of human sperm and egg cells" level="AHL" covers="B2.3.10">

Sperm must travel to the egg and get through its outer layers; the egg must support the early embryo. Their adaptations reflect these very different roles. (Gamete production is in <TopicLink code="D3.1" />.)

### Sperm

- **Acrosome**: a cap-like vesicle over the front of the head containing **hydrolytic enzymes**. When a sperm reaches the egg the enzymes are released and digest a path through the zona pellucida.
- **Haploid nucleus**: 23 chromosomes, with the DNA very tightly packed so the head is small and streamlined.
- **Centriole**: at the base of the head; in humans it organizes the centrosome used for the zygote's first mitotic spindle.
- **Mid-piece** packed with **mitochondria**, wound in a helix around the tail, producing ATP by aerobic respiration for swimming.
- **Tail (flagellum)**: a core of **microtubules in a 9 + 2 arrangement** whose bending propels the sperm.
- Very little cytoplasm, so the cell is small and light. Millions of sperm are made every day.

### Egg (secondary oocyte)

- **Large**, about 110 µm across, with a big volume of **cytoplasm** containing lipid droplets and other nutrients, plus mitochondria and ribosomes, to support the embryo during its early divisions.
- **Haploid** set of 23 chromosomes. At ovulation these are held at metaphase of the second meiotic division, which is completed only after a sperm enters.
- **Cortical granules**: vesicles just inside the plasma membrane. After one sperm has entered, they release their contents by exocytosis, **hardening the zona pellucida** so that no more sperm can get in (preventing polyspermy).
- **Zona pellucida**: a thick layer of glycoproteins around the egg. Sperm must bind to it and digest a way through it, so it protects the egg and restricts entry to sperm of the same species.
- **Corona radiata**: a layer of follicle cells from the ovary that surrounds the egg when it is released.
- Usually only **one** egg is released each month; it is not motile.

The sperm tail is shortened. The egg is a secondary oocyte, so its haploid chromosomes are on a spindle rather than in a nucleus." />
