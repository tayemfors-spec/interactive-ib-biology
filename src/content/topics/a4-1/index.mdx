<Section id="defining" title="What evolution is, and what it is not" level="SL" covers="A4.1.1">

**Evolution** is change in the **heritable characteristics** of a **population** over generations. Two words in this definition do a lot of work.

- **Heritable** means coded for by genes and passed from parents to offspring in gametes. Evolution is therefore a change in the frequencies of alleles in a population's gene pool.
- **Population** means a group of organisms of the same species living and interbreeding in the same area. Individuals do not evolve: they live and die with the genes they were born with.

### Acquired characteristics are not evolution

During its life an organism can change in ways that are **not** caused by its genes. A weightlifter builds muscle, a plant grown in shade becomes tall and pale, and an injury leaves a scar. These **acquired characteristics** do not alter the base sequences of genes in the gametes, so offspring do not inherit them.

Before Darwin, many naturalists accepted **Lamarckism**: the idea that characteristics acquired through use or disuse are inherited. The standard example is a giraffe stretching to reach leaves and passing a slightly longer neck to its young. No mechanism for this has ever been found, and claimed cases have not stood up to testing. In Darwinian evolution the variation comes first, from random mutation and sexual reproduction, and natural selection then changes which alleles become common (see <TopicLink code="D4.1" />).

<Callout type="note" title="Nature of science: why is it still called a theory?">

The **theory of evolution by natural selection** explains and predicts a huge range of observations: the patterns in DNA sequences, homologous and analogous structures, the fossil record, and the rapid spread of antibiotic and pesticide resistance. It is very unlikely ever to be **falsified**. Even so, science can never formally *prove* that a theory matches reality in the way a mathematical proof does. It is accepted because it works (a **pragmatic truth**), and so it keeps the name *theory* despite the overwhelming evidence.

</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">

When you define evolution, always include both **heritable** and **population**. "Organisms change to suit their environment" scores nothing, because it describes neither.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sequences" title="Evidence from DNA, RNA and protein sequences" level="SL" covers="A4.1.2">

All living organisms store their genetic information in DNA built from the same four bases, and translate it into proteins using almost exactly the same genetic code. This unity is itself evidence that all life shares a single origin. More detailed evidence comes from comparing the sequences of the **same gene or protein** in different species.

### How sequence differences arise

When one ancestral species splits into two, each lineage starts to accumulate its own **mutations**: changes to the base sequence of its DNA. Some change the amino acid sequence of the protein that the gene codes for. The longer ago two lineages separated from their **common ancestor**, the more differences they have accumulated. So:

- species that look closely related, such as humans and chimpanzees, have very similar base sequences;
- distantly related species, such as humans and yeast, have many more differences.

Base sequences of DNA or RNA (for example ribosomal RNA, used for comparing all forms of life) and amino acid sequences of proteins can all be compared.

| Species compared with humans | Approximate number of amino acid differences in cytochrome c (104 amino acids) |
|---|---|
| Chimpanzee | 0 |
| Rhesus monkey | 1 |
| Dog | 11 |
| Horse | 12 |
| Chicken | 13 |
| Tuna | 21 |
| Baker's yeast | about 45 |

The order matches the relationships worked out from anatomy and fossils. The pattern of shared and unshared differences is best explained by repeated splitting of ancestral species, and it is used to build **cladograms** (see <TopicLink code="A3.2" />).

<Callout type="exam">

Data questions often give a table of sequence differences. State the trend (fewer differences = more closely related = more recent common ancestor), then explain it: differences build up gradually by mutation after lineages split.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="breeding" title="Evidence from selective breeding" level="SL" covers="A4.1.3">

Domesticated animals and crop plants were developed from wild species by **selective breeding** (**artificial selection**): in each generation, people chose individuals with useful heritable traits and bred from them. Many breeds can still interbreed with their wild relatives, which shows they come from the same stock.

### Examples

- **Dogs** were domesticated from grey wolves, at least 15 000 years ago. Breeds as different as a Chihuahua, a greyhound and a St Bernard are still one species and can interbreed.
- **Wild cabbage** (*Brassica oleracea*) has been bred into several crops by selecting for different parts of the plant:

| Crop | Part enlarged by selection |
|---|---|
| Cabbage | terminal bud (tightly packed leaves) |
| Kale | leaves |
| Brussels sprouts | lateral (side) buds |
| Broccoli and cauliflower | flower clusters (inflorescences) |
| Kohlrabi | stem |

- **Maize** was bred from a wild grass, teosinte, in Mexico about 9000 years ago. Teosinte ears hold a few hard-cased grains; modern maize cobs hold hundreds of exposed kernels.
- **Carrots**: wild carrot roots are thin and pale, while cultivated varieties are thick and, in most modern varieties, orange because of their high carotene content.

### Why this counts as evidence

The differences between varieties, and between them and the wild species, have arisen in only a few thousand years — a short time in evolutionary terms. This shows that heritable variation exists and that selection can change a population quickly. If artificial selection can do this so fast, natural selection acting over millions of years can plausibly account for the differences between species.

<Callout type="tip">

Keep the two processes separate: artificial selection is chosen by humans for human purposes; natural selection depends on which individuals survive and reproduce best in their environment.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="homologous" title="Homologous structures: the pentadactyl limb" level="SL" covers="A4.1.4">

**Homologous structures** have the same underlying structure in different organisms because they were inherited from a **common ancestor**, even when they are now used for different things.

The classic example is the **pentadactyl limb** ("five-fingered" limb), found in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Whether a limb is used for grasping, flying, swimming, running or digging, its bones follow the same plan:

| Part of limb | Bones in a forelimb |
|---|---|
| One bone nearest the body | humerus |
| Two bones further along | radius and ulna |
| A group of wrist bones | carpals |
| A series of bones in each of five digits | metacarpals and phalanges |

<Sim topic="a4-1" id="limbs" />

### Why homology is evidence for evolution

There is no engineering reason why a bat's wing, a dolphin's flipper and a mole's spade should be built from the same bones. Different designs would work at least as well. The shared plan makes sense only if all these animals descended from an ancestor that had a pentadactyl limb. As its descendants spread into different habitats and ways of life, natural selection modified the limb in each group:

- bones became **longer** (bat fingers), **shorter and flatter** (dolphin), **fused** (bird carpometacarpus) or **reduced** (the horse's splint bones);
- digits were **lost** (horse, bird) or gained **extra phalanges** (dolphin).

This modification of one inherited structure in different directions in different lineages is called **divergent evolution**: natural selection in each lineage adapts the shared plan to a different function.

<Callout type="exam">

To describe the pentadactyl limb, name the bones in order from the shoulder: one bone (humerus), two bones (radius and ulna), a group of wrist bones (carpals), then five digits made of metacarpals and phalanges. Then say how one example has been modified for its function.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="analogous" title="Analogous structures and convergent evolution" level="SL" covers="A4.1.5">

**Analogous structures** do the same job but have **different structures and different evolutionary origins**. They arise by **convergent evolution**: unrelated lineages face similar selection pressures, so natural selection favours similar solutions.

### Examples

- **Wings of birds and insects.** Both produce lift for flight. A bird wing is a modified pentadactyl limb with bones, muscles and feathers. An insect wing has no bones: it is a thin outgrowth of the exoskeleton, made of cuticle containing chitin and stiffened by veins, and it is not a limb at all (insects also have six legs).
- **Camera eyes of octopuses and vertebrates.** Both have a lens that focuses an image on a retina. They develop differently, and in vertebrates the nerve fibres run across the front of the retina and leave through it, making a blind spot; in the octopus they run behind the retina, so there is no blind spot. Their last common ancestor, over 500 million years ago, did not have a camera eye.
- **Streamlined bodies** of sharks (fish) and dolphins (mammals), and the tail of a fish compared with the flukes of a whale: a fish's tail fin is vertical and beats from side to side, while whale flukes are horizontal, are stiffened mainly by tough fibrous tissue rather than bony fin rays, and beat up and down.

| | Homologous | Analogous |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | same basic plan | different |
| Function | often different | same or similar |
| Origin | common ancestor had the structure | evolved separately |
| Process | divergent evolution | convergent evolution |

Compare the insect wing with the bird and bat wings in the limb comparator above.

<Callout type="exam">

A structure can be homologous at one level and analogous at another. Bird and bat **forelimbs** are homologous, but their **wings** evolved separately (bats from non-flying mammals, birds from non-flying dinosaurs), so as wings they are analogous.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="splitting" title="Speciation: one species splits into two" level="SL" covers="A4.1.6">

A **species** is a group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (see <TopicLink code="A3.1" /> for this and other ways of defining species). **Speciation** is the formation of new species, and it happens in only one way: an existing species **splits** into two or more populations that evolve separately until they become distinct species.

### Speciation, extinction and biodiversity

- Speciation **increases** the total number of species on Earth.
- **Extinction** is the opposite process: it **decreases** the number of species.
- The number of species at any time therefore depends on the balance between the two. Periods of rapid speciation and mass extinctions both show up in the fossil record.

### Gradual change is not speciation

A single species can change a great deal over time. Fossils may show its body size or tooth shape changing slowly over millions of years, and palaeontologists sometimes give the earlier and later forms different names. But at any one time there is still only one species, so the number of species has not increased. **Gradual evolutionary change within one lineage is not speciation.** Only splitting adds species.

Darwin's notebooks of 1837 already sketched evolution as a branching tree: each fork is a speciation event, and each branch that stops short is a lineage that went extinct.

<Callout type="tip">

When you read a cladogram, every node (branching point) represents a speciation event: one ancestral species splitting into two.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="isolation" title="Reproductive isolation and differential selection" level="SL" covers="A4.1.7">

For one species to split into two, two things are needed.

1. **Reproductive isolation.** The populations must stop interbreeding. While they interbreed, alleles move between them (**gene flow**) and keep their **gene pools** similar. Isolation stops gene flow, so each gene pool can change independently. The most obvious cause is **geographical isolation** by a barrier such as a mountain range, a wide river or a stretch of sea.
2. **Differential selection.** If natural selection acted in the same way on both populations, they would stay alike. Speciation needs the selection pressures to differ — for example in **climate**, **predators** or **competition** for food — so that different traits are favoured in each. **Genetic drift**, random change in allele frequencies, adds to the divergence, especially in small populations.

Eventually the populations are so different that taxonomists judge them to be separate species.

### Bonobos and common chimpanzees

**Bonobos** (*Pan paniscus*) and **common chimpanzees** (*Pan troglodytes*) are our closest living relatives. Their ranges do not overlap: chimpanzees live north and east of the **Congo River**, and bonobos only south of it. The river is wide and deep, and neither species swims.

<Anim topic="a4-1" id="speciation" />

The probable history: the common ancestor lived north of the river. Roughly one to two million years ago, during a dry period when the river was low, a small group reached the south bank and was then cut off. In the south there are no gorillas competing for plants on the forest floor, so food is more plentiful and reliable. The two populations were subject to different selection pressures, and they diverged:

- **bonobos** are more slender, with longer legs and smaller, rounder heads;
- bonobo groups are **female-led** and much less aggressive than chimpanzee groups, which are dominated by males.

<Callout type="exam">

A good answer on bonobos and chimpanzees links four steps: one ancestral population → **geographical isolation** by the Congo River (no gene flow) → **different selection pressures** on each side (plus drift in the small founder group) → divergence into **two species**.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note">

Bonobos and chimpanzees have produced hybrids in captivity, and their genomes show traces of small amounts of interbreeding in the past. Speciation is usually gradual, and the boundary between "two populations" and "two species" is not always sharp.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="sympatric" title="Allopatric and sympatric speciation" level="AHL" covers="A4.1.8">

Speciation is classified by where the diverging populations live.

- **Allopatric speciation** ("other homeland"): the populations live in **different areas**, separated by a geographical barrier. Bonobos and chimpanzees are an example.
- **Sympatric speciation** ("same homeland"): the populations live in the **same area**, but something other than geography stops them interbreeding.

### Three ways to be reproductively isolated

| Type of isolation | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Geographic** | a physical barrier separates the populations | Congo River: chimpanzees and bonobos |
| **Behavioural** | differences in behaviour (courtship, habitat or host choice) mean they do not mate | apple maggot fly host races |
| **Temporal** | they breed at different times of day or year, or in different years | 13-year and 17-year periodical cicadas |

**Apple maggot flies** (*Rhagoletis pomonella*) originally laid their eggs only on hawthorn fruit. After apple trees were introduced to North America, some flies began to use apples. Flies court and mate on the kind of fruit they grew up in, and apples ripen a few weeks earlier than hawthorn fruit, so apple and hawthorn flies now rarely interbreed even where both trees grow together. They are distinct **host races**, apparently part-way to becoming separate species.

**Periodical cicadas** (*Magicicada*) spend 13 or 17 years underground as nymphs, then emerge together to mate. A 13-year cicada, *M. neotredecim*, is thought to have arisen from 17-year ancestors when some switched their cycle; the two types share an area but breed in different years.

The AHL steps at the end of the speciation animation above show both examples. Polyploidy (below) is another important route to sympatric speciation in plants.

### Similarities and differences

- **Both** need reproductive isolation and differential selection (or drift), so that the gene pools diverge.
- In allopatric speciation the isolation is **geographic** and the populations are in different places; in sympatric speciation it is **behavioural** or **temporal** (or due to polyploidy) and they share an area.
- Allopatric speciation is usually gradual. Sympatric speciation can be gradual (host races) or, with polyploidy, happen in a single generation.

</Section>

<Section id="radiation" title="Adaptive radiation as a source of biodiversity" level="AHL" covers="A4.1.9">

**Adaptive radiation** is the evolution of many species from one common ancestor in a relatively short time, each becoming adapted to a different way of life. It often happens when a species reaches a place with **vacant ecological niches** — for example, a new island with few competitors, or a world emptied by a mass extinction. (Niches are covered in <TopicLink code="B4.2" />.)

A population that moves into a new niche faces new selection pressures. Its adaptations make it more and more different from other populations, and once it is reproductively isolated it becomes a separate species. Repeated many times, this produces a group of related species that each use different resources.

<Figure name="a4-1-radiation" caption="Darwin's finches: a simplified tree of seven of the Galápagos species, following genome studies (the warbler finch branches off first, then the vegetarian finch). All are descended from one ancestral finch, and their beaks have become adapted to different foods. Relationships within the ground finches are simplified." level="AHL" />

### Darwin's finches

Within the last few million years (estimates range from about one to three million), finches from South America reached the volcanic **Galápagos Islands**. Their descendants have diversified into about 15–18 species (the exact number depends on how species are defined), which differ above all in beak size and shape:

- **seed eaters** with deep, crushing beaks of different sizes (large, medium and small ground finches) specialise in seeds of different sizes and hardness;
- the **cactus finch** has a long, pointed beak for cactus flowers, nectar and pollen;
- the **warbler finch** has a thin beak for picking insects off leaves;
- the **woodpecker finch** uses a cactus spine or twig as a tool to extract insect larvae from wood;
- the **vegetarian finch** eats buds, leaves and fruit.

### Why this increases biodiversity

Because each species uses a different food or habitat, closely related species can **coexist without competing** for the same resources. As many as ten finch species have been recorded living together on one island. Without the specialisation produced by adaptive radiation, competition would be too intense for so many similar species to survive in one place. Other famous radiations include the Hawaiian honeycreepers and the hundreds of cichlid fish species in the African Great Lakes.

</Section>

<Section id="barriers" title="Barriers to hybridization and sterile hybrids" level="AHL" covers="A4.1.10">

An **interspecific hybrid** is the offspring of a cross between two different species. If hybrids were common and fertile, alleles would flow between the species and their gene pools would merge. Two kinds of mechanism prevent this.

### Barriers that stop hybrids forming

- **Courtship behaviour.** In many animals, courtship is a series of signals and responses that a partner must give correctly before mating happens. A bird or insect of another species does not respond correctly, so courtship stops. Examples are the elaborate displays of birds of paradise and cranes, species-specific songs of birds and frogs, and the flash patterns of fireflies. Courtship needs to be distinctive for each species, which helps to explain why it is so varied.
- **Breeding at different times or in different habitats** (temporal and ecological isolation).
- **Incompatible gametes.** Even if gametes meet, molecules on their surfaces may not match. Sea urchins release eggs and sperm into the sea, but a protein on the sperm surface (bindin) binds only to receptors on eggs of its own species, so cross-fertilization rarely happens.

### Hybrids that are sterile

Some hybrids are formed but are **sterile**, so they cannot pass alleles on. The best-known example is the **mule**.

| | Chromosome number (2n) |
|---|---|
| Horse (female parent) | 64 |
| Donkey (male parent) | 62 |
| Mule (hybrid) | 63 |

A mule grows normally, because mitosis does not require chromosomes to pair. But its 32 horse and 31 donkey chromosomes are **not homologous**: they differ in number and structure, so they cannot pair properly in meiosis, and with an odd number at least one chromosome is always unpaired. Balanced gametes are very rarely produced, so mules are almost always sterile.

Producing a sterile hybrid wastes a parent's resources, so natural selection tends to favour individuals that avoid mating with other species. This can make barriers to hybridization stronger over time.

<Callout type="exam">

To explain hybrid sterility, the key idea is **homologous pairing at meiosis**: chromosomes from two different species cannot pair, so meiosis fails and viable gametes are not formed.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="polyploidy" title="Abrupt speciation in plants by hybridization and polyploidy" level="AHL" covers="A4.1.11">

Most speciation takes thousands of generations, but plants can form a new species in one step.

A **polyploid** organism has more than two sets of homologous chromosomes. Polyploidy happens when the chromosomes in a cell are replicated but the cell does not divide, so the chromosome number doubles (whole-genome duplication).

- **Autopolyploidy** is doubling within one species (AA → AAAA). Crossed with a diploid of its own species, an autotetraploid gives sterile triploids (AAA).
- **Allopolyploidy** is doubling in an **interspecific hybrid** (AB → AABB). This is the route to new plant species.

<Anim topic="a4-1" id="polyploidy" />

### Why doubling turns a sterile hybrid into a new species

1. A hybrid between two diploid species has one set of chromosomes from each (AB). No chromosome has a homologous partner, so meiosis fails and the hybrid is sterile.
2. If the chromosome number doubles, every chromosome gains an identical partner. The **allotetraploid** (AABB) can now carry out meiosis normally and produce AB gametes, so it is fertile.
3. It can breed with other allotetraploids, or fertilize itself, but crosses with either parent give sterile **triploids**. It is reproductively isolated from both parents straight away: a new species, formed in the same place as its parents (sympatric).

This works well in plants because many plants can self-fertilize or reproduce asexually, so a single new polyploid can found a population.

### Knotweeds and smartweeds (*Persicaria*)

The genus ***Persicaria*** (knotweeds and smartweeds) contains over 100 species, and many of them arose by hybridization followed by polyploidy. One example is ***Persicaria maculosa*** (redshank), with 2n = 44: molecular evidence shows it is an allotetraploid derived from two diploid species, *P. lapathifolia* and *P. foliosa*, each with 2n = 22.

Many crop plants are also allopolyploids. Bread wheat, for example, has six sets of chromosomes (2n = 42) from three ancestral grass species.

<Callout type="tip">

Use letters for chromosome sets when you explain polyploidy: AA × BB → AB (sterile) → AABB (fertile). Then show why a cross back to a parent gives AAB, a sterile triploid.

</Callout>

</Section>
